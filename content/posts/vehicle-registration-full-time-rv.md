Roughly 1 million Americans currently live in their RVs full-time, according to the RV Industry Association, and a surprising number of them are driving around with vehicle registrations that are quietly costing them hundreds of dollars a year more than they need to. I know because I was one of them for my first two years on the road. I had my rig registered in California, where I'd been living before I left, and I was paying nearly $800 a year in registration fees alone. The fix, once I finally figured it out, cost me $98 and an afternoon at a DMV in [South Dakota](/south-dakota-rv-domicile/).

If you're new to full-time RV life, or you're planning the transition and this question is nagging at you, you're probably wondering: where am I supposed to register my vehicle if I don't have a permanent address? That's the exact right question to ask, and it's one of the most practically important decisions you'll make in your first year. Get it wrong and you're overpaying, potentially for years.

Here's what I tell people who email me about this: the "where you park it most" answer that feels intuitive is almost always the wrong one financially. States have wildly different registration cost structures, and for full-timers, the right choice is almost always one of three states: South Dakota, Texas, or Florida.

South Dakota wins on annual fees almost every time for large rigs. The state calculates registration on a flat weight/length schedule rather than vehicle value, which matters enormously if you're driving a newer Class A. In California, newer RV values translate directly into higher fees because the state uses market value as part of its calculation formula. On a 2022 Tiffin Allegro Bus, that difference can be $600 or more per year.

Texas is competitive and popular, partly because of the infrastructure for full-timers in the Houston area (a lot of mail services cluster there), and partly because their registration process is genuinely painless. But the 6.25% sales tax stings if you're buying your rig in-state. Florida is the warm-weather compromise: more expensive than South Dakota but popular among snowbirds who spend significant time there anyway.

## Establishing Domicile Without a House

This is the part that tripped me up, and I suspect it trips up most people. "Domicile" is the legal term for the state you claim as your permanent home. For vehicle registration purposes, your [domicile state](/rv-domicile-state-options/) is where you register. You can't just pick South Dakota on a whim; you have to establish it.

The good news: for South Dakota, it's genuinely easy. You need a physical mailing address in the state (not a P.O. box, an actual street address), which a [mail forwarding service](/best-mail-forwarding-service-for-full-time-rvers/) provides. Services like America's Mailbox in Box Elder, SD, or the Escapees club's Dakota address service give you a real street address for around $100–$200 a year. You make one trip to the state, visit the DMV, get a South Dakota driver's license, register your vehicle, and you're done. No waiting period. No proof of employment or prior residency.

When I did this in 2019, the clerk at the Pennington County DMV asked me two questions: "Is this your permanent mailing address?" and "Are you planning to make South Dakota your domicile?" That's basically it. The whole thing took about two hours including the license office.

Florida requires a bit more: you'll need to show up in person, but you can register at any county tax collector office, and they're used to full-timers. Texas similarly handles this routinely, especially at DMVs in the Houston metro.

One thing most guides skip: you should also update your voter registration to match your domicile state. It's legally consistent, it matters if you're ever audited by a previous state's tax authority, and frankly, it's the right thing to do.

## The Tow Vehicle Question

If you're in a fifth wheel or travel trailer setup, you have two vehicles to register: the tow vehicle and the trailer itself. They don't have to be registered in the same state, technically, but keeping them together simplifies your life enormously. In my experience, registering everything in South Dakota on the same trip is the cleanest approach.

Class B van lifers, you're actually in the simplest situation here, one vehicle, and the process above applies directly.

Trailers get registered differently than motorized RVs. In South Dakota, trailers over 4,000 lbs use a weight-based schedule, and a lot of full-timer fifth wheels fall in the $98–$120 range annually. A reader named Dave, who reached out after I mentioned this on a forum last spring, had been paying $340 a year in Oregon for his 36-foot fifth wheel. He switched to South Dakota domicile, and his combined trailer and truck registration dropped from about $590 to $247. That's $343 a year, every year, for a two-day trip to Rapid City.

## What Can Go Wrong

Here's a scenario worth thinking through: you establish South Dakota domicile, register everything, and then you spend six months parked in California because your kid lives there and you want to be close. California has an aggressive policy about vehicles "used predominantly in California" -- they consider 90+ days of continuous presence as establishing California residency for vehicle purposes, and they can come after you for back registration fees and taxes.

This isn't just theoretical. California has a non-resident vehicle use enforcement program, and they do audit. The practical answer is: keep moving, document your travel, and don't treat a single state as your de facto home base even if you love it. Full-time RVing means full-time moving, at least enough to maintain your domicile legally.

Texas has a similar rule around 30-day presence, though enforcement is considerably less aggressive in practice.

Another thing that catches people: if you financed your RV, your lender may have requirements about what state it can be registered in. Check your loan paperwork. A few lenders get twitchy about South Dakota specifically (I don't entirely understand why, but it comes up), and you don't want registration drama while also managing loan paperwork.
