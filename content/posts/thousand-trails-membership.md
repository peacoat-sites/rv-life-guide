[Thousand Trails](/is-thousand-trails-membership-worth-it-for-full-timers/) memberships are genuinely useful for full-timers who camp frequently in the same regions, and they can pay for themselves within a single season if you pick the right tier. But they're often misunderstood, over-sold by resellers, and badly matched to people whose travel patterns don't fit the network's geographic footprint. Worth it or not depends entirely on how you actually camp, not how you imagine you might.

The core concept: you pay a one-time membership fee (or buy a used membership on the secondary market), plus an annual dues payment, and you get access to Thousand Trails' campground network with no nightly fees at the campgrounds included in your zone or Elite tier. Some locations also have "Trails Collection" partner parks that require a small nightly add-on fee. The company is owned by Equity LifeStyle Properties (ELS), which also owns a large portfolio of manufactured home communities and other RV resorts, including Sun Outdoors and Encore Parks locations that can be accessed through upgraded tiers.

## What You Actually Get

The membership structure has evolved a lot over the years, so ignore anything you read that's more than two or three years old. [Right now](/why-right-now-is-a-rare-buyers-window-in-the-rv-market/) Thousand Trails sells two main tracks: zone memberships (covering specific geographic regions like the Northwest, Southwest, Southeast, etc.) and the Elite network membership, which covers all zones.

Zone memberships are cheaper upfront but they're a trap for anyone who moves around. If you're a full-timer doing a loop across multiple regions each year, a single-zone membership covers maybe 20-30 of the 80-plus network campgrounds. You can pay to add zones, but the incremental cost can push you close to Elite pricing anyway. Run the actual campground list against your planned routes before you commit.

Elite memberships open up the full Thousand Trails network plus access to hundreds of partner locations through the Trails Collection, including some Sun Outdoors and Encore properties. The nightly fee at Trails Collection parks varies by location, typically in the $5-$20 range per night, but that's still well below market rates at comparable private campgrounds. Some premium park locations (beach-front, resort-style) may cost more.

There's also an add-on called the Camping Pass, which gives access to additional campgrounds beyond the core Thousand Trails network, but most full-timers find it unnecessary if they're already using the Thousand Trails network heavily.

## The Real Cost Breakdown

This is where people get burned. The pitch sounds simple. The reality involves more line items.

| Cost Component | Approximate Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| New membership (direct, single zone) | $4,000 - $7,000+ | Price varies; ELS sales reps negotiate |
| New membership (Elite) | $7,000 - $12,000+ | Varies significantly by sales channel |
| Used membership (resale market) | $500 - $3,000 | Facebook groups, eBay, RV forums |
| Annual dues | $600 - $900/year | Required every year; non-negotiable |
| Trails Collection nightly fee | $5 - $25/night | Varies by park and season |
| Upgrade fees (adding zones) | $1,000 - $4,000 | If bought zone and want more coverage |

Annual dues are non-negotiable and they don't go away. A common mistake is calculating break-even only against the upfront cost and forgetting that $700/year (a reasonable midpoint) is a fixed baseline cost you're paying whether you camp 2 nights or 200. If you camp infrequently for a couple of years, you've spent $1,400+ in dues and gotten very little.

**Example:** Assume you buy a used Elite membership for $1,500 and pay $750/year in annual dues. Your all-in cost after two years is $3,000. If you camp 50 nights per year at Thousand Trails campgrounds where the going comparable rate would be $45/night, you've avoided $4,500 in camping fees over two years. Net positive: $1,500. At 25 nights per year, you're roughly breaking even. Below 20 nights per year in-network, the math stops working. These numbers assume consistent use of the full network, which requires actual campground availability in your area.

## Availability: The Problem Nobody Mentions Enough

Campground availability is the biggest practical complaint among active members, and it's worth being blunt about it. Popular Thousand Trails locations (coastal California, Pacific Northwest, parts of Florida) book up fast, particularly in summer and during holidays. The network uses a reservation system, and without an upgraded "extra nights" package or a Thousand Trails Select add-on, your advance booking window may be limited compared to paying members at higher tiers.

If your travel is flexible and you can move on short notice, availability is manageable. If you have hard commitments (school schedules, work locations, specific events), you may find yourself unable to get into a network park when you need it. This is not a dealbreaker, but it is a real operating constraint that the sales pitch glosses over.

The 14-day stay limit applies at most locations. After 14 consecutive nights, you must leave for a set period (often 7 days at a minimum distance, check current member rules) before returning to the same park. For full-timers who prefer to stay put for a month at a time, this is genuinely limiting. It forces movement, which some people love and others find exhausting.

## Buying Used: The Smarter Path for Most People

The resale market for Thousand Trails memberships is active and well-established. Facebook groups specifically dedicated to TT membership resales exist, and listings also appear regularly on eBay and RV-specific forums. Used memberships transfer to new owners, though Thousand Trails charges a transfer fee (verify the current amount directly with the company, as it changes periodically).

The key questions to ask a seller: What tier is the membership (zone or Elite)? What are the current annual dues? Is the membership current on dues, or are there arrears? Has the membership been used in the past couple of years (dormant memberships can sometimes be harder to reactivate)? Get the membership number and call Thousand Trails member services directly to verify the status before you hand over money.

Avoid any resale deal where the seller won't let you verify with Thousand Trails first. That's the only protection you have.

## Who Should Pass

People who camp primarily in the Northeast will find the network thin. The mid-Atlantic and New England states have limited Thousand Trails locations compared to the West Coast and Southeast. If most of your camping happens in those regions, you're paying for coverage that doesn't match your routes.

Weekend warriors who camp 10-15 nights per year won't recoup costs even on a cheap used membership. And anyone who primarily uses federal lands (BLM, National Forest [dispersed camping](/dispersed-camping-guide/), national parks) won't find any overlap with the Thousand Trails network at all, since these are private RV resort campgrounds.
