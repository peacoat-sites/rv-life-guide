You just found the perfect rig, mapped out your first few destinations, and then someone asks: "So what's your address?" The excitement stalls. It's a deceptively simple question with surprisingly complicated answers. Your address affects your driver's license, vehicle registration, voting rights, taxes, insurance rates, and your ability to open a bank account. Getting this wrong costs people hundreds or thousands of dollars every year. Getting it right is one of the most important logistical moves you'll make before hitting the road full time.

## Why Your Legal Domicile Matters More Than You Think

Let's get the terminology straight. There's a difference between a mailing address and a legal domicile. A mailing address is just where physical mail gets delivered. Your legal domicile is the state you officially call home, and it carries real financial and legal weight.

Your domicile state determines:

- **State income tax.** Texas, South Dakota, and Florida have no state income tax. California and New York can take 9-13% of your income. That's not a small difference if you're working remotely or running a business.
- **Vehicle registration fees.** South Dakota charges around $36 to register most passenger vehicles. California can run $200 or more depending on the car's value.
- **Insurance premiums.** Insurers rate policies based on your registered state. Some states are dramatically cheaper than others for both auto and RV insurance.
- **Jury duty, voting, and legal residency.** These all tie back to your declared domicile.

The three states most full-timers choose are South Dakota, Texas, and Florida. Each has no state income tax, relatively relaxed residency requirements, and established infrastructure for nomadic living. South Dakota's especially popular because you can establish residency with just one night in a motel, hit the DMV, and be done.

Full-timers generally use one of three approaches: a family member's address, a commercial mail forwarding service, or a private mailbox service. Each has real trade-offs.

**Using a family member's address** is free and feels simple. But it creates problems. If you're using your mom's house in Ohio as your legal address while you're actually domiciled in South Dakota for tax purposes, you're mixing signals across different official records. That inconsistency can cause issues with banking, insurance, and the IRS. Only use this option if you're genuinely maintaining ties to that state.

**Commercial mail forwarding services** are built specifically for full-timers and travelers. The big names:

- **Escapees RV Club (Livingston, TX):** One of the oldest and most trusted. Membership costs around $50/year, and mail forwarding plans start at about $15/month. You get a real street address in Texas, which helps with domicile establishment.
- **America's Mailbox (Box Elder, SD):** Specifically designed for South Dakota domicile. They'll help you get a driver's license and vehicle registration in a single trip.
- **Traveling Mailbox:** Digitizes your mail so you can see everything online. Plans start around $15/month. Great for people who rarely need physical items forwarded.
- **Anytime Mailbox:** Similar digital scanning model. Works with a network of locations.

**Private Mailbox (PMB) services** like The UPS Store give you a real street address instead of a P.O. Box. Banks and government agencies will accept these. Costs run roughly $20-50/month depending on location and volume.

## Establishing Residency: A Step-by-Step Walkthrough for South Dakota

South Dakota is the most popular choice for good reason. Here's exactly how you do it.

**Step 1: Get a physical address.**
Sign up with America's Mailbox or another SD-based mail forwarding service before your trip. You'll get a street address in Box Elder or Sioux Falls.

**Step 2: Book one night in a motel.**
South Dakota law requires you to be "physically present" in the state to establish residency. One night qualifies. Keep your receipt as proof.

**Step 3: Visit the DMV in person.**
Bring your motel receipt, your mail forwarding address documentation, your current driver's license, your Social Security card or proof of SSN, and your birth certificate or passport. You'll surrender your old license and get a South Dakota one. The photo is taken same day.

**Step 4: Register your vehicle(s).**
You can usually do this at the same DMV visit. Bring your current title. Registration is fast and cheap.

**Step 5: Register to vote (optional but recommended).**
You can register at the same visit. South Dakota allows full-time travelers to register without claiming a fixed address, using your last known SD address (your mail forwarder's address).

**Step 6: Update your records.**
Notify your bank, insurance company, Social Security (if applicable), and any subscription services of your new address. This step takes a few hours but is critical.

The whole SD trip takes one to two days if you plan it right. Many people do it as a dedicated side trip within their first month on the road.

## Banking, Insurance, and Other Practical Complications

Banks are where a lot of full-timers run into friction. Most traditional banks require a physical residential address, not a P.O. Box. A commercial mail forwarding address with a real street number (not "P.O. Box 123") usually satisfies this requirement. If you're setting up new accounts, use your domicile state's address from day one.

For insurance, make sure your health, auto, and RV policies all show the same state address. Mismatched records raise red flags and can create problems at claim time. Health insurance is particularly tricky for full-timers, since many plans restrict coverage to a specific service area. Check out our article on [health insurance for full-time RV living](/health-insurance-full-time-rv/) for options that actually work on the road.

RV-specific insurance is a separate matter from your tow vehicle or truck. Many insurers offer "full-timer" policies that treat your RV as a primary residence, which affects liability coverage. Progressive, National General, and Good Sam Insurance all have full-timer options worth comparing.

One thing people overlook: voter registration. Your domicile address is where you'll vote. Absentee voting is standard for full-timers. Most states allow it without requiring a physical reason. You just request an absentee ballot ahead of each election cycle.

Here's a quick breakdown to help you decide between the three most popular options:

|---|---|---|---|
| State income tax | None | None | None |

Texas has the disadvantage of requiring annual vehicle inspections, which is a genuine hassle if you're not near Texas when the deadline hits. Florida is a solid middle ground and popular with snowbirds who spend winter in the southeast.

South Dakota wins for most people who want the cleanest, lowest-cost setup with the least bureaucratic friction.

## Common Mistakes That Create Real Problems

People make these errors constantly. Avoid them.

**Splitting your address across states.** Using your California driver's license while registering your RV in South Dakota is a red flag. Consistency matters. All your major records should point to the same domicile state.

**Using a P.O. Box where a street address is required.** The IRS, banks, and many insurers reject P.O. Boxes. Always confirm your mail service provides a real street address.

**Waiting too long to update records.** Many states require you to update your driver's license within 30 to 60 days of changing your address. If you get pulled over six months after establishing SD residency but still have a Texas license, you could technically be out of compliance.

**Forgetting about professional licenses.** If you hold a contractor's license, real estate license, nursing license, or similar credential, check the reciprocity rules. Some licenses are state-specific and may need to be transferred or renewed through your new domicile state.

**Underestimating the cost of mail forwarding.** A $15/month service adds up to $180/year, plus per-piece forwarding costs. If you receive a lot of physical mail, budget $30-50/month realistically.
