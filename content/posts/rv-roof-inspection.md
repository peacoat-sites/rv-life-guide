Most RV owners I've met inspect their roof exactly once: right after they buy the rig. Then they forget about it until water is dripping onto their mattress at 2 a.m. somewhere in rural New Mexico.

I was one of those people. Bought my first Class C in 2016, glanced at the roof during the dealer walkthrough, nodded along while the salesman talked, and didn't think about it again for eight months. Then one October morning in Moab I noticed a soft spot in the ceiling panel above the dinette. The repair bill ended up being $2,400 because the damage had been spreading the entire time. That was an expensive lesson, and I've talked to enough full-timers since then to know it's practically a rite of passage.

Here's what I've learned from eight years of inspecting, maintaining, and occasionally screwing up: [a proper roof inspection](/rv-maintenance-diy/) isn't complicated, but most people are doing it wrong in ways that cost them thousands.

## What You're Actually Looking For (And Why Most Walkthroughs Miss It)

The standard advice is "check for cracks and soft spots." Not wrong. Just incomplete enough to barely help.

What you're really trying to catch is any breach in the membrane or sealant that allows liquid water in, any sign that water has already been in, any mechanical failure in seams or penetrations, and any UV degradation that's weeks away from becoming a failure. Those are four different things, and they require four different types of attention.

The penetrations are where I see people consistently rush. Vents, AC units, antennas, ladder brackets, plumbing vents, solar panel mounts, anything that interrupts the roof surface is a potential entry point. A standard 34-foot Class A might have 12 to 18 of them. Every single one needs close attention, not just a glance from six feet away.

What surprised me was how often the problem isn't the sealant itself. It's the transition zone between the sealant and the roofing material. The sealant can look perfect and still be lifted along one edge by 1/16th of an inch. In light rain, nothing happens. But in a heavy downpour with wind pushing water sideways, that gap becomes a funnel. I've seen this on brand-new rigs under warranty. Not a manufacturing defect exactly, just the reality of materials expanding and contracting through temperature cycles.

## The Actual Inspection Process, Start to Finish

Before you go up there, check your roof load rating in the owner's manual. Many RVs, especially older ones with EPDM rubber roofs, aren't rated for a person standing on them without plywood distribution boards. I keep a pair of 24-by-24-inch plywood squares in my basement compartment for exactly this reason. Walk on the boards, not the roof.

Do this on an overcast day or in the morning shade. Not for comfort (though that helps), but because glare from a sunny day flattens shadows and you will miss microcracks in the membrane.

**Step 1: Photograph everything before you touch it.** Use your phone. Walk a systematic grid. This takes maybe five minutes and has saved me multiple times, both for warranty claims and for tracking whether something has grown since the last inspection.

**Step 2: Feel the surface with your palms, not your fingertips.** You're looking for delamination, soft spots, or areas where the substrate gives slightly underfoot. Move in slow strips from front to back. If you find a soft area, mark it with a piece of blue painter's tape before you keep moving, otherwise you'll lose track of where it was.

**Step 3: Inspect every sealant bead.** Get close. You're looking for cracking (a fine network of surface cracks that looks like dried mud), separation from the substrate, shrinkage that's pulled the bead thin in the middle, and any discoloration that might indicate prior moisture intrusion. Old Dicor lap sealant that's been baking in the Arizona sun for four or five years often goes gray and brittle. It may still be technically adhered, but it'll fail in the next hard freeze.

**Step 4: Check the seams.** Every RV roof has seams where panels meet, and where the [roof membrane meets sidewalls](/rv-roof-repair-sealing/) or the front cap. Run your finger along these. On an EPDM roof, the seam tape should feel firmly bonded, with no lifting edges. On a TPO roof, the seams are heat-welded and failure usually shows up as a crack along the weld line.

**Step 5: Look at the AC unit base gasket.** This one specifically, because AC units vibrate constantly while running and that vibration works on the mounting seal for years. The gasket compresses over time. On my current rig (a 2018 Tiffin Allegro Bus), I replaced the Dometic Penguin II base gasket at around 50,000 miles of highway travel because it had compressed from about 3/4 inch to nearly nothing.

**Step 6: Go back inside and look up.** With all the lights off, use a bright flashlight to look at the ceiling panels in the areas above where you found anything suspicious outside. Press gently on the ceiling material. Any sponginess, staining, or musty smell is a red flag.

## Rubber vs. TPO vs. Fiberglass: The Inspection Differences Actually Matter

| Roof Type | UV Sensitivity | Sealant Type | Seam Construction | Common Failure Points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EPDM Rubber | High (requires annual conditioning) | EPDM-compatible lap sealant | Taped seams | Penetrations, UV chalking, micro-cracks |
| TPO | Low (more UV-resistant) | Heat-welded | Heat-welded seams | Corners, sharp transitions, seam load sensitivity |
| Fiberglass | Varies | Depends on substrate | Varies | (covered in article) |

I've lived with all three roof types over the years. They don't fail the same way.

**EPDM rubber** (the black or white membrane that most older and mid-range rigs use) is UV-sensitive and needs conditioning. Dicor makes an EPDM roof coating that I apply once a year; it runs about $25 for a 32-ounce bottle. Without it, the rubber chalks and eventually micro-cracks. The sealant around penetrations on EPDM roofs is also EPDM-compatible lap sealant, not silicone. Using the wrong sealant is a mistake I see constantly in online forums. Silicone on EPDM doesn't bond correctly and will fail.

**TPO** (the white membrane on most rigs made in the last decade) is more UV-resistant but the heat-welded seams are load-sensitive. TPO is harder to repair yourself if you find a failure. Most failures I've seen on TPO roofs happen at corners and transitions where the material has to bend sharply, not in the flat field.

**Fiberglass** is the most durable and easiest to inspect because failures show up as visible cracks or crazing in the gelcoat. But gel coat problems can hide subsurface delamination. If you knock on a fiberglass roof and you hear a hollow thud in a spot that should sound solid, that's delamination, and it may have nothing to do with the surface condition.

The research on which roof type actually lasts longest is honestly mixed. Every RV forum has passionate arguments for all three. My experience is that condition of installation and maintenance history matter more than the material itself. A well-maintained EPDM roof at 15 years old can be in better shape than a neglected TPO at 5.

## How Often and When to Do This

Every six months is the minimum if you're full-timing. Every three months is better, especially if you're doing a lot of highway miles or parking under trees. Tree sap and debris accelerate sealant degradation faster than almost anything else.

The two critical times are before winter storage (or your first hard freeze if you don't store), and in spring before your active season. The freeze-thaw cycle is brutal on any existing crack because water expands when it freezes. A hairline crack in October becomes a real problem by March.

I do a quick scan after any significant hailstorm. Hail won't typically penetrate a healthy EPDM or TPO roof, but it can dent AC housings, crack vent covers, and damage antennas in ways that compromise the seals around them.

Most sealant work is genuinely DIY-friendly if you're willing to prep correctly. Prep is 90% of a good sealant repair. Clean the area with a dedicated cleaner (Dicor makes one, so does Proflux), let it dry completely, and apply the correct sealant for your roof type.

[Dicor lap sealant](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=dicor+lap+sealant&tag=contentportfo-20) runs about $9 to $12 per tube and is the standard for EPDM roofs. For TPO roofs, you want a TPO-compatible product. A reader emailed me last month asking why their repair was peeling after two weeks. They'd used the wrong product, and the incompatibility between the sealant and the TPO membrane just wouldn't allow adhesion. The right product matters more than technique.

What I won't DIY: any repair involving re-seaming, significant membrane patching over 6 inches, or anything near a slide-out seal on a roof. Those jobs have enough complexity that I'd rather pay a certified RV tech at an RVIA-member shop and get the work documented for resale purposes.

A [good surge protector](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+surge+protector&tag=contentportfo-20) protects your electrical system. A good roof inspection protects everything else. Both cost significantly less than what they prevent.
