I've spent somewhere around $40,000 on campground fees over eight years on the road. That number made me want to lie down when I actually calculated it. And the honest part? A big chunk of that was paid before I figured out which memberships were actually worth buying and which ones were just well-marketed money traps.

Here's what nobody tells you upfront: the RV membership game is complicated, the savings are real but context-dependent, and if you camp less than 60 nights a year, most of the premium programs won't pay off. Let me walk you through what I've actually used, what broke my heart, and what I'd buy again tomorrow.

## Passport America: The One Most People Overlook

Half-price camping sounds too good to be true. With Passport America, it mostly isn't. You pay $44 per year (as of 2024) and get 50% off nightly rates at around 1,900 campgrounds across the U.S., Canada, and Mexico. The restrictions are real: most parks limit you to two or three consecutive nights at the discount rate, and a handful of higher-demand parks black out summer weekends. But if you're moving frequently -- which is the actual lifestyle -- those restrictions rarely matter.

My first year with Passport America, I broke even in two nights. Two. I used it at a Gulf Coast park in Mississippi that normally ran $48/night and paid $24. That's a $44 membership recouped in one weekend stop.

What I'll be honest about: the directory has some clunkers. You'll find a few parks that are, generously speaking, not up to the photos on their listings. Part of my process now is cross-referencing Passport America parks with Google Maps satellite view and recent reviews before I book. But the network has genuinely improved over the last four years, and the $44 price point makes it the lowest-risk membership purchase in this space. I'd tell any full-timer to buy it before anything else.

## Thousand Trails: Where It Gets Complicated

Thousand Trails is the program that generates the most confused questions in RV Facebook groups, and honestly, the confusion is earned. The pricing structure has changed multiple times, the resale market for older memberships is a whole separate rabbit hole, and the experience at their parks varies wildly from location to location.

Here's how it works now: their current Zone Passes start around $545/year for a single zone (about 20 parks) and go up to $845/year for their Elite Membership, which covers 90+ parks nationwide. You can stay up to 14 consecutive days, then you have to leave for a week before returning to the same park.

Whether it pencils out depends almost entirely on your travel patterns. If you're a westerner who spends time near their Pacific or Southwest Zone parks, the math works beautifully. A week at their Lake Minden park in California would cost $50-70/night without a membership. Seven nights there and your annual fee is paid. But if you're roaming the Southeast and you're not near their clusters? You'll drive out of your way to justify the cost and still come up short.

What surprised me was how much the quality varies property to property. Their Soledad Canyon park in California is legitimately beautiful. Some of their Midwest locations feel like they haven't seen a capital improvement since 2003. The campground ratings on Campendium are your best research tool here -- don't rely on the Thousand Trails website photos.

One thing I'd push back on: the resale membership angle. Older Thousand Trails memberships (from before their corporate structure changed) can be found on eBay and RV forums for $200-400 and sometimes include perks the current products don't. I have a friend who bought a legacy membership for $250 that gives her essentially unlimited camping across the full network with no blackout dates. That market is getting thinner as old memberships age out, but if you're patient and do the research, it's worth exploring.

## Harvest Hosts and Boondockers Welcome: A Different Category

These two don't compete with Passport America or Thousand Trails. They're solving a different problem: the desire to get off the beaten path and camp in interesting places without paying for a hook-up site you don't need.

Harvest Hosts ($99/year, though they've been running promos around $79 fairly regularly) connects self-contained RVers with wineries, breweries, farms, golf courses, and museums that let you park overnight for free in exchange for, generally, a purchase from the business. I've stayed at a sheep farm in Virginia, a lavender farm in Oregon, and three different wineries in Sonoma County. None of those nights cost me anything beyond a $25 bottle of wine I was happy to buy anyway.

The limitation is that you need to be self-contained. No hookups, no dump stations on-site usually. If you're in a 40-foot diesel pusher running three air conditioners, this probably isn't your network. For anyone in a Class B, small Class C, or a van conversion, it's one of the best $99 spends in the full-timer toolkit.

Boondockers Welcome ($50/year) works differently: you're staying in the driveways or private land of other RVers who've opened their spots to the network. The stays are genuinely free. The community is generous. I've used it maybe 15 times and had one experience that was just a little awkward (host was more hands-on than I expected). The other 14 were excellent. It's since merged with Harvest Hosts, and you can buy both together for around $119 now, which is a no-brainer.

## Escapees and Good Sam: The Practical Reality

Good Sam gets marketed hard because Camping World has a financial stake in pushing it. The $35/year membership gives you 10% off at Camping World locations and supposedly 10% off at affiliated parks. Here's my candid take: the park discounts are inconsistently honored, and a lot of the parks in their network are the same ones charging $65-75/night for a gravel pad and 30-amp power. I've had Good Sam for years. I've renewed it mostly out of habit and occasional use at Camping World when I needed parts urgently. If you're not buying gear regularly at Camping World, the math is thin.

Escapees RV Club ($49/year) is a better story. The discount network overlaps with some Good Sam parks but also includes independent parks that are often better maintained. More importantly, Escapees is a real community organization. Their SKP parks (member-owned co-ops in a handful of states) are excellent long-term options for full-timers, and the mail forwarding service they offer at around $165/year is one of the best-reviewed options for full-timers who need a permanent address. The membership opens doors beyond just campsite discounts.

## Boondocking and the Apps That Cost Nothing (Almost)

I'd be doing you a disservice if I only talked about paid memberships without mentioning the free or nearly-free options that are sometimes better than any of them.

The BLM (Bureau of Land Management) and National Forest land in the western U.S. is free camping that doesn't require any membership at all. I've spent months on BLM land in Arizona, Utah, and Nevada without paying a single campsite fee. The Dyrt Pro ($35.99/year) and iOverlander (free) are apps that map dispersed camping areas, user-reviewed with GPS coordinates. I find new spots with The Dyrt constantly. The Pro version adds offline maps, which matters when you're in the desert.

FreeCampsites.net is free to use and has a surprisingly good database, though the comment sections age out without updates sometimes.

None of this replaces a good membership if you want hookups or amenities. But if your rig can handle boondocking, the combination of BLM knowledge and a $35 app subscription will outperform a $500 membership nine times out of ten.

## Building a Stack That Actually Works

My current setup, which I've refined over about three years of tweaking: Passport America ($44), Harvest Hosts + Boondockers Welcome bundle ($119), and Escapees ($49). Total: $212/year. I canceled Thousand Trails after year two because my travel route didn't justify it. I keep Good Sam only for the Camping World discount when I'm within driving distance of a store during a specific parts need.

That $212 stack probably saves me $3,000-4,000 a year compared to paying full rates at comparable parks. My camping costs run around $400-500 a month now, down from $900+ when I was paying rack rates everywhere and had no system.

The key is matching memberships to your actual travel pattern, not the hypothetical travel pattern you imagine when you're sitting in a sales meeting or watching a YouTube tour of a beautiful Thousand Trails property in California. Pull up your last 12 months of camping locations, map them, and see which membership networks actually cover where you go.
