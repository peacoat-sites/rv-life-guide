A couple from Ohio once told me they spent $68,000 on a brand-new Class A motorhome, drove it home, and discovered the slide-out motor was already failing, the roof had a delamination bubble the size of a dinner plate, and the fresh water tank had a crack that the dealership had painted over with Flex Seal. The PDI, or pre-delivery inspection, lasted 45 minutes and consisted mostly of a salesperson showing them how to work the TV remote. That rig spent more days in the shop during year one than it did on the road. I've watched this story repeat itself across campgrounds from the Florida Keys to the Yukon, and it almost always comes down to the same thing: buyers skipped the checklist, trusted the handshake, and paid for it later.

---

## Why Most RV Buyers Skip the Checklist (And What It Costs Them)

The RV buying process feels exciting. You've been watching YouTube tours for six months, you've narrowed it down to a floorplan you love, and the salesperson is friendly and the financing looks manageable. In that moment, a written inspection checklist feels like paranoia. It isn't.

RVs are built fast. The major manufacturers in Elkhart, Indiana operate production lines where a unit can go from bare frame to finished rig in days. Quality control is inconsistent, and that's me being charitable. Even at the premium end of the market, you'll find staples shot through wiring, cabinet doors that don't close flush, and roof seams that weren't properly sealed. On used units, you're also inheriting every repair the previous owner attempted and every problem they ignored.

A proper checklist does two things. It catches issues before you own them, and it gives you negotiating leverage when you find them. A dealer who knows you've documented a failing slide seal and a soft spot near the rear cap has to deal with that conversation before you sign anything.

I've personally walked away from three rigs over the years because of what a systematic inspection turned up. One had water damage hiding under new flooring, only visible because the subfloor flexed slightly when I walked near the bathroom. Another had an inverter that was wired backwards, which someone clearly installed themselves. The third had a frame crack visible only when you lay under the rig with a flashlight. None of those sellers were upfront about the problems. The checklist found them.

## Exterior Inspection: Where the Real Damage Hides

Start outside, always. Walk the entire perimeter slowly before you ever step through the door.

**Roof condition** is the most critical item on any RV inspection. Delamination shows up as bubbles or ripples in the sidewall or roof membrane. Press gently on any suspicious area. Soft spots mean moisture has gotten into the laminated wall construction and the wood substrate is rotting. On rubber membrane roofs, check every seam, every vent boot, and every edge seal. Cracked or dried-out sealant is a maintenance failure, and on an older unit it may have been leaking for seasons.

**Slide-out seals and operation** deserve their own audit. Run every slide out and back in at least twice. Watch for hesitation, grinding, or misalignment. Inspect the rubber seals on all four sides of each slide. They should compress evenly and spring back. Dry, cracked seals are a $200-400 fix, but they're also a sign of deferred maintenance throughout.

Check the **underbelly** if you can get under there. Look at the frame welds, the holding tank condition, and whether any drain lines show evidence of cracking or repairs. On tow vehicles, inspect the hitch receiver and examine the ball mount and coupler for wear. On fifth wheels, look at the kingpin and the pin box for cracks or unusual wear patterns.

Examine the **tires** carefully, even on new units. RV tires are often underrated for the loads they carry. Check the DOT date code stamped on the sidewall. The last four digits indicate the week and year of manufacture. Many dealers have rigs sitting on the lot for a year or more, and tires degrade from UV exposure and ozone even when they're not being driven. I won't buy any rig with tires older than three years, period.

## Interior Inspection: Systems, Surfaces, and Smells

Open the door and stop. Just stand there for thirty seconds with your eyes closed. A musty smell, a mildew smell, or a propane smell all mean something. Fresh pine scent means someone used an air freshener recently, which isn't necessarily a red flag, but notice it.

Work your way through every system methodically.

**Propane system:** Turn on the propane, check the stove burners, the oven, and the furnace. Use a propane leak detector or soapy water on all the fittings you can access. This is non-negotiable.

**Electrical system:** Test every outlet with a simple outlet tester. Run the air conditioner. Check the inverter if there is one. Plug in a 1500-watt space heater and confirm the circuit doesn't trip. Check the battery condition by looking at the voltage under load.

**Water system:** Fill the fresh tank and run water to every faucet, the shower, and the outdoor shower if equipped. Flush the toilet. Check under every sink and behind the toilet for moisture, staining, or soft spots in the flooring. Run the water heater on both propane and electric, and confirm it actually heats.

**Slideout interiors:** Where the slides meet the main floor, get on your knees and look. That transition is a common spot for water intrusion. Soft flooring there is a serious problem.

Check every cabinet, every drawer, every door. Open the refrigerator and look at the cooling fins in the back panel. Look for rust or frost that shouldn't be there.

## Step-by-Step: The Checklist Walk-Through

Use this sequence on any rig, new or used.

1. **Arrive early and bring tools.** You need a flashlight, a non-contact voltage tester, a simple outlet tester, a moisture meter (you can buy one on Amazon for under $25), and a small rubber mallet. Tap on walls near windows and slideout frames. Hollow sounds in a solid-wall area mean delamination.
2. **Do the exterior perimeter walk.** Note every ding, crack, bubble, and sealant failure.
3. **Inspect the roof.** Ask permission to climb up if possible. If they won't let you, that alone tells you something.
4. **Check all tires.** Date codes, tread depth, sidewall cracking.
5. **Go under the rig.** Frame, tanks, plumbing.
6. **Run every slideout, twice.**
7. **Check propane lines and appliances.**
8. **Test the electrical system** at every outlet and run a load test.
9. **Run the water system** completely, including water heater.
10. **Use your moisture meter** along every wall seam, window edge, and slideout transition.
11. **Check the roof from inside** by looking at ceiling panels near the edges. Staining means water has come in.
12. **Drive it or tow it.** Even a short test drive reveals brake issues, steering problems, or weird vibrations that no amount of static inspection will catch.

## New vs. Used: What the Checklist Looks Like for Each

New rigs and used rigs share most of the same inspection items, but the emphasis shifts.

| Item | New RV Priority | Used RV Priority |
|---|---|---|
| Roof seam sealing | Medium | High |
| Water damage / delamination | Low | Very High |
| Tire age | Medium | Very High |
| Appliance function | High | High |
| Build quality / fit and finish | High | Medium |
| Frame and structural integrity | Low | High |
| Previous repair quality | Not applicable | Very High |
| Warranty documentation | Very High | Medium |

On a new rig, your leverage is the pre-delivery inspection list you hand the dealer before you sign. Everything found during PDI should be repaired before you take delivery, in writing. Get that in the purchase agreement. Verbal promises from dealers are worth nothing when you're 800 miles from the selling dealership.

On a used rig, seriously consider paying for a third-party RV inspection. A certified RV inspector from NRVIA (National RV Inspectors Association) typically charges $400 to $700 and can save you many times that. For any rig over $30,000, it's a straightforward financial decision.

## Gear You Should Buy Before the Keys Hit Your Hand

Once you've signed and before you spend a single night in the rig, there are a few pieces of equipment that aren't optional in my view.

A **surge protector and EMS** (Electrical Management System) is the single most important piece of gear for campground use. Wiring at RV parks varies wildly, and a power surge or miswired pedestal can fry your appliances or start a fire. I use a Progressive Industries unit and it's saved my rig at least twice that I know of. [A quality 30-amp or 50-amp EMS on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+surge+protector+EMS) runs $150-$300 and is worth every cent. (This site may earn a commission on purchases.)

A **water filter** goes between the campground water supply and your rig, not just an inline filter. Many campgrounds have old plumbing. [An inline water filter designed for RV use](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+inline+water+filter) is a $20-40 item that protects your tank and your stomach.

[Leveling blocks](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+leveling+blocks) are something most new buyers assume will be provided or figured out. They won't be. A set of interlocking plastic leveling blocks for around $30-50 will serve you for years.

If you're buying a rig with solar or planning to add it, a **battery monitor** lets you know your actual state of charge instead of guessing. The Victron BMV-712 is the one I see in more rigs than any other, and for good reason.

## FAQ

### How long should an RV inspection take?

A thorough inspection of a mid-size travel trailer or Class C motorhome should take two to four hours minimum. A large Class A or fifth wheel can take four to six hours when done properly. If a dealer is pushing you to finish in under an hour, slow down or reschedule. There's no rule that says you have to rush.

### Should I hire a professional RV inspector even on a new rig?

For a new rig, you can usually handle the inspection yourself with a thorough checklist and a few tools. For used rigs over $25,000 or any rig you're buying sight-unseen, yes, hire a certified inspector from NRVIA. The cost is a rounding error compared to the cost of missing a water-damaged subfloor or a cracked frame.

### What are the most commonly missed problems on used RVs?

Roof damage and delamination are the most frequently missed because they often start subtle and buyers don't get on the roof. Soft subfloor near the bathroom and slideout transitions is second. Previous electrical DIY work that violates code is a close third, and you'll often see it in the power center where amateur wiring is visible once you remove the panel cover.

### Can I negotiate price based on inspection findings?

Absolutely, and you should. A documented list of defects with rough repair estimates is a legitimate negotiating tool. On private party sales especially, sellers who aren't upfront about problems will often accept a lower price rather than lose the deal. On dealer sales, the better play is usually to require repairs before delivery rather than a price reduction, because a price reduction just means you're inheriting the problem.

### What should I look for on the title and paperwork?

Check that the VIN on the title matches the VIN on the rig. On motorhomes, there will be a chassis VIN and a coach VIN; make sure you have clear title on both. Check the title for any lien holder. Check the registration and confirm the year matches what the dealer is representing. On a used rig, a CARFAX or similar report won't cover the RV unit itself, but on motorhomes it will show accident history on the chassis, which matters.

The best RV purchase I ever made took me three weeks, two separate inspection visits, a professional inspector on the second visit, and a negotiated price reduction based on what we found. The worst purchase I ever made took an afternoon and a lot of enthusiasm. Six years and 90,000 miles have taught me that the checklist isn't the enemy of excitement, it's the thing that keeps the excitement from turning into regret somewhere in the Nevada desert at 11pm with a failing water pump and no backup plan.
