California's most coveted state park campsites have been plagued for years by the same problem: someone books a site at Big Sur or Crystal Cove, never shows up, and the spot sits empty while a hundred other people would've taken it in a heartbeat. The state finally got serious about it. As of July 1, 2026, Assembly Bill 618 is live, and the consequences for serial no-shows are steep enough to actually change behavior.

If you're an RVer who books California state parks regularly, this affects you directly. Read the policy wrong once and you could be locked out of ReserveCalifornia for a full year, right when you need it most.

## What the Law Actually Does

| Cancellation Timing | Financial Penalty | Account Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Less than 48 hours before arrival or no-show | 100% of all reservation fees | Counts as one strike toward 3-strike ban |
| 2 to 6 days before arrival | First night + all processing fees | Counts as one strike toward 3-strike ban |
| More than 6 days before arrival | Standard processing fee | No strike |
| Three strikes in one calendar year | N/A | 365-day ReserveCalifornia account lockout |

The headline change: cancel within 48 hours of arrival or simply don't show up, and you forfeit 100% of all fees paid for the entire reservation. Not a partial credit. Not a processing fee. Everything.

Cancel between two and six days out and the penalty is lighter but still real: you lose the cost of your first night plus all reservation processing fees.

The ban threshold is three strikes in a single calendar year. Three no-shows and your ReserveCalifornia account gets locked for 365 days, per the [California State Parks reservation policy FAQ](https://www.parks.ca.gov/?page_id=31977). State Parks says the system will flag your account and notify you before any restriction is applied, giving you a window to dispute a misunderstanding. That's a reasonable safeguard, but don't count on it bailing you out if the no-shows are legitimate.

## Why Ghost Bookings Were Such a Real Problem

California manages roughly 15,000 campsites and lodging options across the state park system. Demand crushes supply at the desirable ones. Sites at Pfeiffer Big Sur, Doheny, Carpinteria, Salt Point, all of them disappear within seconds of the 8 a.m. booking window opening, sometimes weeks or months in advance.

The ghost booking problem was straightforward: scalpers and overly optimistic planners would grab multiple sites speculatively, then cancel or no-show when plans didn't materialize. Meanwhile, the site showed "unavailable" to everyone else for weeks. The old cancellation penalty, a modest processing fee, wasn't painful enough to discourage it.

As [CamperFAQs reported on July 1](https://camperfaqs.com/california-campgrounds-are-cracking-down-on-no-shows), the previous system created a cycle where serious campers couldn't get spots while casual holders sat on inventory they never intended to use. AB 618 is designed to break that cycle by making speculative booking genuinely expensive.

## The Part That Catches RVers Off Guard

Here's where full-timers and serious road-trippers get burned differently than weekend campers.

If you're planning a California coastal run, you're probably booking multiple sites across multiple parks on the same trip. Maybe Atascadero State Beach, then Andrew Molera, then a spot near Lake Tahoe on the way out. If your rig breaks down in Bakersfield (and it will eventually, because that's how rigs work), you might miss one or two of those bookings with less than 48 hours notice.

Under AB 618, each one of those is a strike. Two mechanical breakdowns in a bad year and you're at two strikes. One more bad day and you're locked out for 365 days.

This isn't a hypothetical. Anyone who's spent real time in an RV knows that "the plan" has a shelf life of about 400 miles. Weather, breakdowns, health, family situations, all of it can blow up a booking. The new policy doesn't have a hardship exemption written into public-facing documentation, which means documentation of your circumstances matters if you want to contest a strike before the ban lands.

## The Lottery Pilot Is Actually Good News

Buried in the AB 618 rollout is something that doesn't get enough attention: a pilot lottery reservation system at several high-demand park units. The state hasn't published a full list of participating parks as of early July 2026, but the intent is clear. Instead of rewarding whoever has the fastest internet connection at 7:59 a.m., the lottery gives every applicant during a window an equal shot at high-demand sites.

For full-time RVers who work odd hours or can't always be at a computer at exactly 8 a.m., this is a genuine improvement. The refresh-and-pray booking experience at popular California parks is exhausting and unfair. A lottery doesn't fix inventory scarcity, but it does distribute the scarcity more equitably.

Watch the ReserveCalifornia site for which parks enter the lottery program and what the application windows look like. That detail will matter for trip planning as the pilot expands.

## How to Protect Your Booking Record

The practical adjustments aren't complicated, but they require more discipline than most people apply to campsite reservations.

Don't book speculatively. If you're not 80% confident you're going to be at a site, don't hold it. The financial penalty alone should enforce this, but the ban risk makes it non-negotiable.

Build cancellation buffer into your itinerary. If there's any uncertainty about arrival dates, book sites that fall outside your most vulnerable travel days. A two-night buffer between hard travel and a California state park booking gives you cancellation flexibility.

Use the 2-to-6-day window strategically. Losing your first night and processing fees hurts, but it's not the same as losing a full multi-night booking. If plans start to unravel, act early rather than hoping the situation resolves.

Keep documentation. If you're canceling due to a rig breakdown, save the repair invoice with dates. If it's a medical issue, keep a record. [Hoodline noted in their July 2 coverage](https://hoodline.com/2026/07/serial-campground-flakes-face-yearlong-ban-at-california-state-parks/) that State Parks will notify account holders before finalizing restrictions, which means there's a window to make a case. Having documentation makes that conversation go better.

California's ghost booking crackdown is overdue and mostly fair. The penalty structure is aggressive, but the problem it's solving was real and the people it protects most are the serious campers who couldn't get sites in the first place. Adjust your booking habits now, before peak summer season teaches you the hard way.
