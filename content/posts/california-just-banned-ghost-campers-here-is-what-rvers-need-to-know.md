You open ReserveCalifornia on your phone at 7:59 a.m., finger hovering, and the second that booking window flips open for Pfeiffer Big Sur you start hammering refresh. Gone in four seconds. Again. Meanwhile, you've driven past that same campground three times [this summer](/how-to-camp-by-rv-at-a-fifa-world-cup-host-city-this-summer/) and counted the empty sites. Not a fluke. Ghost bookings, where someone reserves a site with zero intention of showing up, or just casually holds six sites "to keep options open," have been eating California's campground system alive for years. That ends now.

As of July 1, 2026, Assembly Bill 618, authored by Assemblymember Rebecca Bauer-Kahan and signed by Governor Gavin Newsom, kicked in with real teeth. California State Parks has overhauled the ReserveCalifornia system with a tiered penalty structure and, for repeat offenders, a first-ever 365-day reservation ban. If you're living the RV life and California is part of your circuit, this affects how you plan, how you book, and what happens when life on the road throws you a curveball. And it will.

The short version: the era of low-stakes ghost booking in California state parks is done. The San Francisco Chronicle reported more than 1.4 million [campsite bookings](/california-just-changed-the-rules-for-state-park-campsite-bookings/) in California in 2023 alone, with popular coastal sites selling out within seconds of the window opening. That kind of demand made abuse of the system almost inevitable. AB 618 is the state's attempt to actually match availability to the people who show up.

## The New Penalty Tiers, Laid Out Plainly

Here's what California State Parks is now enforcing, and I want to be direct about how steep the late-tier penalties actually are.

| Cancellation Timing | What You Lose |
| --- | --- |
| 7+ days before arrival | Full refund minus $8.25 fee |
| 2–6 days before arrival | First night's site fee (forfeited) |
| Within 48 hours OR no-show | Entire amount paid for the full stay |

That bottom row is the one to tattoo somewhere visible. If you're booked for four nights at a coastal site running $55 a night and you no-show or cancel the day before, you lose $220. Not a warning. Not a partial credit. Gone. California State Parks' official policy FAQ confirmed these figures when the rules went live in July 2026.

What most people don't realize is how fast three no-shows can stack up for a full-[time RVer](/how-to-get-health-insurance-as-a-full-time-rver/) who books speculatively. Say you grab an early-season site at Salt Point, something goes sideways with your rig, and you cancel last-minute. You do it again in June when a family emergency pulls you off route. One more time before December and you are locked out of the entire ReserveCalifornia system for a full calendar year. That's 15,000 campsites and lodging options managed by California State Parks that you simply cannot access.

## What "Ghost Booking" Actually Looked Like and Why It Was So Bad

I've seen this play out in campgrounds up and down the coast. Someone books five different sites across a long weekend, planning to pick one depending on weather, then cancels the others the night before. Someone else holds a site "just in case" while waiting to see if friends can make it. Multiply that by thousands of bookings and you get a system where a park looks full on ReserveCalifornia for months while physically sitting at 40% capacity on a Saturday afternoon.

The 2026 rule changes didn't happen in a vacuum, either. A broader industry shift is already underway. Many campgrounds nationwide have adopted stricter cancellation policies this year, and the data is showing it actually improves real-world availability for spontaneous RV travelers, according to recent reporting from CamperFAQs. California just went further than most.

The people this hurts most aren't malicious. They're disorganized, or they're hedging because the booking anxiety is real. But the practical result for everyone else, the people who drive hours only to find empty sites they couldn't book, has been genuinely frustrating. AB 618 puts the financial weight on the person holding the site, which is where it belongs.

## The Lottery Pilot: Possibly a Bigger Deal Than the Penalties

Buried in AB 618 is something that doesn't get as much attention as the fee structure but could matter a lot more long-term. The bill authorizes California State Parks to run a pilot lottery program at select high-demand parks. The specific example in circulation is Pfeiffer Big Sur.

If this rolls out the way it's designed, you'd apply for a site during a window and get selected randomly, rather than winning or losing based on who has the fastest internet connection at 8 a.m. and the most flexible work schedule. I've talked to full-timers who have basically given up on booking Pfeiffer Big Sur through the normal system because the refresh scramble is just not winnable for someone managing a day job from their rig. A lottery doesn't fix everything, but it does fix that specific unfairness.

No hard launch date has been announced yet for which parks get the lottery first or exactly when it starts, so this one is worth watching. Check the California State Parks website directly for updates rather than relying on secondhand announcements.

## How to Protect Yourself Under the New Rules

The old approach of booking a few maybes and canceling whatever you don't use is now genuinely risky. Here's how I'd adjust.

Be realistic about your plans before you commit. If there's a real chance your schedule shifts, don't book inside the 7-day window unless you're certain. That $8.25 cancellation fee for early cancellations is essentially nothing. Losing a full four-night stay because you canceled the day before is a different kind of pain entirely.

If something genuinely goes wrong, like a mechanical issue (and it will happen), act fast. Get your cancellation in before that 48-hour clock runs out. I've had a generator fail at a rest stop in the Central Valley and still managed to cancel two reservations from my phone before the deadline. It's worth building that habit now before the stakes get higher.

Also keep records. If you believe a no-show was logged against you incorrectly, you want documentation: confirmation emails, cancellation receipts, anything. The Contact Center is part of the ban system too, so this isn't just an online problem.

The national trend toward stricter enforcement is real, and California is leading it hard. That's mostly good news for the people who actually show up.
