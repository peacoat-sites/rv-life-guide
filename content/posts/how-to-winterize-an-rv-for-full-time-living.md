Most winterizing guides are written for people who park their rig in October and don't think about it again until May. That's not you. And doing this wrong when you live in your RV full-time is a completely different kind of problem, because you can't just walk inside and call a plumber.

I made a mess of my first winter in a 2016 Keystone Montana. Not catastrophically, but I had a cracked water line behind my shower wall by February, which I didn't discover until it had been slowly weeping into the subfloor for probably three weeks. The repair ran me $800 with a mobile RV tech in Flagstaff. That was the year I got serious about cold-weather systems.

What I've learned is that winterizing for full-time living isn't one event. It's an ongoing process that looks completely different depending on whether you're stationary for the season, still moving through cold climates, or somewhere in between. The approach changes. The stakes don't.

## The Plumbing Question (and Why Blowing It Out Isn't Enough for Full-Timers)

Here's the thing most guides miss: if you're living in your rig, you can't fully winterize your plumbing the way a seasonal camper can. You need running water. You need your toilet. You need your shower. So the standard "blow it out with compressed air and dump pink antifreeze everywhere" method only works if you're going away for an extended stretch or bringing in fresh water daily from jugs.

For full-time winter living, the real strategy is heat. Keeping your water lines warm enough that they never freeze in the first place.

If you have a newer rig with an enclosed and heated underbelly, you're already ahead. My current setup is a 2021 Grand Design Reflection, and the heated underbelly running off the furnace keeps my tanks and lines functional down to about 0°F without supplemental help. Below that, I add a small ceramic space heater pointed toward the wet bay door and run the furnace on a timer to cycle more frequently at night.

For rigs without a heated underbelly, or older builds where insulation has compressed and lost R-value, you've got options.

Pipe heating cables are the most reliable thing I've found. The Frost King 6-foot self-regulating cable (around $25 at Lowe's or Amazon) wraps around your water inlet and fresh water lines near the underbelly. Self-regulating is key. The cable draws more power when temperatures drop and backs off when it's warmer, so you're not running it full blast 24/7. I've also had good luck with the Camco TastePURE heated water hose (about $80-100), which stays flexible and unfrozen down to -20°F. Worth every dollar if you're on city water hookups.

Your holding tanks are different. Gray and [black tanks will freeze](/rv-black-tank-treatment/) if temps stay below 20°F for more than a couple days. Tank heater pads like the Facon brand 12V pads (about $30 each) stick directly to the tank and pull from your 12V system. They work, but they also draw constant power, which matters if you're off-grid.

One more thing people overlook: your water pump. If you're dry camping and your fresh tank is inside or insulated, the pump itself is usually fine. But the lines from the pump to your fixtures can still freeze in the cabinet spaces. Leaving cabinet doors under sinks open overnight lets ambient cabin heat reach those lines.

I resisted skirting for two winters. Thought it looked bad. Thought it would be too much work to set up and take down every time I moved. I was wrong.

Good skirting does more than protect your underbelly from wind chill. It traps ground heat, which stays warmer than ambient air throughout most of winter. It dramatically cuts how hard your furnace has to work, which if you're on propane means real money. In a parked winter setup in Durango, Colorado (elevation 6,500 feet, regular overnight lows in the single digits), I was burning through a 100-lb propane tank every 8-10 days without skirting. With skirting, I stretched that to 16-18 days. That's the difference.

EZ Snap skirting is what I use now. It runs $400-700 depending on your rig size, but it's reusable, snaps on and off in an hour or so once you've installed the base strip, and looks presentable. Some parks actually require skirting in winter. Foam board (1.5" XPS, the pink or blue stuff from Home Depot, around $40-50 per sheet) is cheaper and works nearly as well. You cut it to fit each panel, lean it against the rig, and use sand bags or landscape staples to hold it in place. Not pretty. Works.

Whatever you use, don't forget to leave access panels near your connections. Reaching the water hookup after you've fully skirted the rig gets old fast.

## Heat: The Furnace Isn't Enough by Itself

Your RV furnace is designed to bring a cold space up to temperature, not run continuously as your sole heat source. Running a propane furnace nonstop will burn through tanks fast, wear out the blower motor, and in a tight-sealed winter setup can affect air quality if combustion isn't perfect.

What I actually run is a layered system. Primary heat is a Mr. Heater Little Buddy (the 3,800 BTU indoor-safe version, about $90) for daytime when I'm actively in the space and can keep an eye on it. Secondary is a 1,500-watt ceramic space heater for overnight if I'm on shore power. The propane furnace acts as backup and kicks on via thermostat only when inside temps would otherwise drop below 60°F.

A few things I've learned with propane heat: ventilate a little, even in cold weather. Just cracking a window slightly prevents moisture buildup, which is serious in winter. I've seen rig interiors with actual mold growth on the walls from sealed winter living. Also, CO detectors should be tested monthly and replaced every 5-7 years regardless of what the sticker says. Mine are Kidde brand, under $25.

The ceiling fan running on low in reverse (pushing warm air down from the ceiling) makes a noticeable difference. Seriously, try it.

## Roof, Windows, and Seals: The Overlooked Part

Cold snaps and temperature cycling are brutal on your rig's seams. I do a full roof and seal inspection every fall, without exception. What I'm looking for: cracking or shrinking around roof vents, skylights, and anywhere the roof meets the sidewalls. Self-leveling lap sealant (Dicor 501LSW for TPO/EPDM roofs, about $12 a tube) is the standard fix, and you want it applied when temps are above 40°F so it cures properly.

Windows are a major heat loss point in older rigs. If you've got single-pane windows (common in anything built before the mid-2010s), you'll feel the cold radiating off them in winter. Reflectix cut to fit inside the window frames is a cheap solution, around $25 for a roll. Not beautiful, but it cuts draft and helps with condensation. I've also used the 3M Indoor Window Insulator Kit (shrink-to-fit plastic film, about $12 for 3 windows) and found it surprisingly effective for windows I'm not opening all winter.

Door seals deserve attention too. My slide-out seals get treated with rubber conditioner (Thetford makes a good one, around $10) every fall. A cracked slide-out seal in January is not fun to deal with.

If you're wintering in a campground with 30 or 50 amp hookups, the electric side of winter is relatively forgiving. Shore power runs your space heater, your tank heaters, your heated hose.

But if you're boondocking in winter, or even in a campground where shore power is unreliable or limited, your battery bank and solar situation becomes critical.

The research here is honestly mixed on how well lithium batteries perform in extreme cold. Most lithium (LiFePO4) batteries stop accepting a charge below 32°F, and their output capacity drops. My Battle Born 100Ah batteries (around $1,000 each, though the price has moved around) perform noticeably better in cold than the AGM batteries I had before, but they're not magic. They still need to be in an insulated compartment. I've wrapped my battery bay in Reflectix and added a small temperature-controlled heat mat (the kind used in reptile enclosures, about $20) that kicks on below 40°F.

Solar in winter also requires recalibration. If you're parked with snow potential, you'll be on the roof more often than you want. Shorter days and lower sun angles reduce production. Winter boondocking really means reducing your power consumption aggressively and leaning harder on propane than solar for heat.

A battery monitor is non-negotiable if you're off-grid. The Victron SmartShunt ($100-130) gives you accurate state-of-charge, voltage, and current draw. Flying blind on battery state in winter is a bad position.

Eight years in, I still do a pre-winter checklist every September. Not because it's become automatic, but because the specific failure points change every year as my rig ages and as I end up in different places. The goal isn't to make winter feel like summer. It's to make it feel like home, on your terms, with no unexpected $800 surprises in February.
