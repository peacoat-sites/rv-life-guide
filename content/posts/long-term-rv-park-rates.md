## What "Long-Term Rate" Actually Means (And What It Doesn't)

The term "long-term" gets thrown around loosely in this industry. To one park, it means a 30-day stay. To another, it means you've signed a six-month lease. The distinction matters enormously because the pricing structures are completely different.

Most parks define long-term as 30 days or more, and that's where the first discount tier kicks in. But what most people don't realize is that many parks have two or three additional tiers beyond that. A month-to-month tenant might pay $800. Someone who pre-pays three months might pay $700. A six-month or seasonal commitment can drop that to $550 or even lower. The park doesn't advertise this aggressively because they'd rather you pay more.

There's also a legal layer that catches people off guard. In most U.S. states, once you've been at an RV park for 30 consecutive days, you may acquire tenant rights under landlord-tenant law. Some parks deliberately structure billing as 29-day cycles or require you to leave and return specifically to avoid triggering that legal status. It's not the park being sneaky about rates. It's them managing liability. Know this before you commit, especially if you're planning to stay several months and want some stability.

The other thing that changes with long-term rates is what's included. A nightly or weekly rate almost always includes water, electric, and sewer. Monthly rates often separate electric onto a metered sub-account, meaning you pay actual consumption. In summer in Arizona or Texas, that can add $80 to $150 a month to your bill. Always ask what utilities are included before comparing rates across parks.

## The Real Range of Monthly RV Park Rates Across the Country

I've stayed in parks from coastal Maine to the California desert, and the range is genuinely wide. Here's an honest picture based on real experience, not brochure prices.

**Budget end ($300 to $550/month):** These are usually older parks in rural or inland areas, sometimes with dated amenities. Think gravel pads, basic bathhouses, maybe a laundry room with two machines. The sites are often full of long-timers with permanent-looking setups. These parks can be fantastic for the price if you're not picky about aesthetics or WiFi speeds.

**Mid-range ($550 to $900/month):** This is where most people end up. You get paved or concrete pads, 50-amp service, decent restrooms, sometimes a pool or gym, and staff that actually answer the phone. In suburban areas around mid-size cities, this range is very achievable with a long-term commitment.

**Higher-end ($900 to $1,500+/month):** Coastal areas, popular Sun Belt destinations (Scottsdale, Naples, Sarasota, the Florida Keys), and mountain resort towns. Amenities are often excellent, parks are well-maintained, but you're paying a premium for location. These parks also tend to fill up fast with seasonal residents who book the same site year after year.

Where you are in the country changes everything. A long-term site in the Tucson area might run $500 a month. That same money gets you a partial month in Marin County, California. If location flexibility is your actual lifestyle, you have enormous leverage over your housing costs.

## How to Negotiate a Better Rate (This Is Real, Not a Gimmick)

Most people walk up to the office, get quoted a rate, and either say yes or drive away. Almost nobody negotiates. That's a mistake.

Park managers, especially at privately-owned parks, have real flexibility on pricing. Their worst case scenario is an empty site generating zero revenue. You are worth more to them than nothing, and they know it. Here's how I approach it:

**Ask explicitly for the long-term rate.** Don't assume they'll volunteer it. The quoted nightly or weekly rate is what you get if you don't ask. The phrase "what's your best monthly rate if I stay at least 90 days" opens a different conversation than "how much per night."

**Mention what you bring.** Are you retired and will be home during quiet hours? Do you keep a clean site? Are you flexible on timing and can arrive in their slow season? These things matter to park managers more than you'd expect.

**Offer pre-payment.** Offering to pay three months upfront in exchange for a discount works more often than not. You're reducing their administrative overhead and giving them guaranteed cash. Many will knock 10 to 20 percent off for that.

**Ask about work-camping arrangements.** Some parks will discount or even waive site fees in exchange for part-time work, usually 15 to 20 hours a week doing maintenance, landscaping, or front desk coverage. If you have free time and want to dramatically cut costs, this is the most underused option out there.

**Negotiate timing.** Rates are lowest in shoulder seasons. If you're trying to get into a popular Arizona park, arriving in October instead of December can save you $200 a month or more.

**Mail and residency.** Can you receive mail at the park? Some parks allow it, others explicitly forbid it. If you're establishing residency in a new state, you need a workable mail solution.

**Lot leveling.** Ask which direction your assigned site slopes. I can't count how many nights I've slept at a slight tilt because I didn't check before unhitching. **Quiet hours and pet policies.** If you have dogs, confirm size and breed limits upfront. Many parks have breed restrictions and some cap pets at two. Find this out before you're in a lease.

**Cell signal and WiFi.** Park-provided WiFi is usually terrible for working remotely. Check signal strength from the actual site for your carrier before you commit.

## FAQ

### How much should I realistically budget for a long-term RV site?

A realistic budget for most of the country is $500 to $900 per month, not counting electric if it's metered separately. Add $50 to $150 for electric in climates where you'll run AC or electric heat heavily. If you're targeting popular Sun Belt destinations in peak season, budget $900 to $1,400. If you're flexible about location and willing to negotiate, $550 to $700 a month is very achievable for a decent site with full hookups.

### Is it cheaper to sign a long lease or stay month-to-month?

A longer lease almost always gets you a lower rate, sometimes significantly lower. The tradeoff is flexibility. If you need to leave before the lease is up, most parks will keep your prepaid money or charge a penalty. If cost is the priority and you're confident about the location, a seasonal lease usually wins. If you value the ability to move, month-to-month is worth the extra cost.

### Do long-term RV parks require a rig inspection before accepting you?

Yes, many do. It's called a "rig approval" or "visual inspection" and it's more common at nicer parks and 55+ communities. They're checking age (some parks exclude rigs older than 10 or 15 years), condition of the exterior, and whether it meets their standards. This isn't universal, but if you have an older rig with cosmetic issues, call ahead and ask about their policy before making the drive.

### What's the difference between a long-term RV park and an RV community or manufactured home park?

The line has blurred, but generally: an RV park rents you a site, you stay in your own rig, and the expectation is that you could theoretically leave. An RV or manufactured home community often involves lot leases, sometimes permanent utility connections, and a more residential legal structure. Some people own their unit but lease the land. The permanence is higher, the flexibility is lower, and the monthly cost is often lower as well because you're taking on a longer commitment.

### Can I negotiate utilities into a flat-rate monthly fee?

Sometimes, especially if you're driving a hard bargain or the park is motivated to fill a site. Some parks offer an "all-inclusive" monthly rate that bundles electric at a flat add-on, say $75 or $100, regardless of actual usage. If you run electric heat in winter or heavy AC in summer, an all-inclusive deal is often worth paying a bit more for the rate. If you're a light user with good solar and propane, you'll probably save money on metered electric.

Long-term RV living absolutely can be affordable, sometimes dramatically more affordable than stick-and-brick housing, but only if you understand how the pricing works and aren't afraid to ask for what you want. The neighbors paying $650 at that $1,400-asking-price park didn't stumble into a deal. They knew to ask, knew to negotiate, and knew what to look for before they signed anything. That knowledge gap is the only real difference between people who make full-timing work on a budget and people who burn through savings faster than they expected.
