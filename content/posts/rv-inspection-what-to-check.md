A $47,000 mistake. That's what a friend of mine made when she bought a used Class A motorhome without a proper inspection. The roof had been painted over to hide delamination, the slide-out seals were cracked through, and the generator hadn't run in two years. She found all of this out within the first 90 days of ownership. I've been living in my fifth wheel full-time since 2019 and I've watched this scenario play out more times than I care to count. A thorough RV inspection before you hand over a check isn't optional. It's the only thing standing between you and a very expensive education.

---

## Why RV Inspections Are Nothing Like a Home Inspection

Most people approach buying an RV the way they'd approach buying a used car. They kick the tires, run the AC for two minutes, flush a toilet, and call it good. That approach works about as well as you'd expect.

RVs are uniquely complicated machines because they combine a vehicle (with all the mechanical complexity that implies) with a living space built on top of a chassis that flexes, vibrates, and gets rained on constantly. The joints, seams, and seals that hold everything together are under stress every single mile. And because most RVs sit unused for months at a time, problems compound quietly until someone like you comes along to buy them.

The RV Industry Association estimated that roughly 10 million American households own an RV. A significant portion of those were bought used. And the resale market is famously full of sellers who either don't know about problems or absolutely do know and are counting on you not to find them.

A certified RV inspector, someone credentialed through the National RV Inspectors Association (NRVIA), will charge you between $400 and $700 for a thorough inspection. That feels like a lot until you remember that a slide-out rebuild can run $3,000 to $8,000, a roof replacement on a Class A can exceed $10,000, and fresh water tank replacements are rarely under $1,500 installed. Pay the $500. Always.

That said, whether you're hiring a pro or doing a preliminary walkthrough yourself, you need to know what you're actually looking for.

## The Roof: Start Here, Every Time

If you only have 30 minutes to look at an RV, spend 20 of them on the roof. This is where the most expensive damage starts and where sellers most reliably hope you won't look.

Get up there. Bring your knees if you have to, because you need to walk the entire surface. You're looking for:

- **Soft spots or sponginess underfoot.** Press firmly with your foot across the whole surface. Any give at all suggests water intrusion and possible rot or delamination in the decking below. This is serious.
- **Cracks or separations in the sealant** around vents, air conditioner bases, antennas, and any roof penetration. Sealant should look continuous and slightly pliable. If it's cracked, chalky, or pulling away from the edge, water has likely been getting in.
- **Bubbling or wrinkling** on rubber (EPDM) roofs. A perfect roof lies flat. Bubbles mean moisture has found its way underneath the membrane.
- **Patches or previous repairs.** One careful repair isn't a dealbreaker, but multiple patches tell you this roof has been fighting water for a while.

While you're up there, check every vent cover for UV brittleness. Squeeze them gently. Plastic that crumbles or cracks easily means the whole roof has been sun-baked for years, and the sealant is probably in the same shape.

Come back down and look at the interior ceiling, especially in corners and near slide-out openings. Brown staining, soft drywall, or that particular musty smell are all signs water found a path inside.

## The Systems Check: Water, Electric, and Gas

These three systems are where RVs get dangerous, not just expensive. Don't rush this section.

**Fresh water and plumbing:** Fill the fresh water tank and run every faucet, both hot and cold. Flush every toilet. Run the shower. Check under every sink and in every cabinet while the water is running. A slow drip becomes a slow disaster over time. While you're at it, look at the water heater bypass valves (most RVs have them for winterization) and make sure they're set correctly. A bypassed water heater will give you cold showers and confused troubleshooting for weeks if you miss it.

Ask when the water filter was last changed and whether the unit has an inline filter at all. If you end up buying, a [countertop or under-sink RV water filter](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+inline+water+filter&tag=rvsite-20) is one of the first things you'll want to install. **Electrical:** Shore power first. Plug the RV into a 30-amp or 50-amp connection (whichever it uses) and test every single outlet with a plug-in circuit tester, available at any hardware store for about $12. Test every light switch. Run the roof AC and the furnace simultaneously if you can, because that load combination reveals weak wiring and tripped breakers that intermittent use won't expose.

If the RV has solar, check what's actually installed versus what's advertised. A [battery monitor](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+battery+monitor&tag=rvsite-20) will show you the real state of the battery bank. A seller who says "the batteries are fine" without showing you actual voltage and charge data is telling you nothing useful. Batteries that sit discharged for months sulfate and lose capacity. I've bought "good" batteries that needed immediate replacement every time I've skipped this step.

Check the shore power inlet for corrosion and burn marks. A [surge protector](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+surge+protector&tag=rvsite-20) is non-negotiable for full-time living, but a burned inlet tells you the unit has been plugged into bad power and possibly suffered damage already.

**Propane:** Turn on every burner on the stovetop, one at a time. Light the oven. If the unit has a propane furnace, run it long enough to confirm it actually cycles and produces heat. Smell carefully around all gas connections. If you have a gas leak detector (the handheld wand style), use it. Leaks are not always obvious by smell alone.

## Slide-Outs, Seals, and Structure

Slide-outs add living space and they add complexity. Every slide-out is a potential leak and a potential mechanical failure point. Run every slide in and out at least twice. Watch for:

- Hesitation, grinding, or unusual sounds
- Visible gaps when the slide is fully extended
- Worn or cracked slide seals (these are the rubber gaskets that press against the wall when the slide is out)
- Water stains on the floor directly inside the slide opening

The floor at the base of slide-outs takes more abuse than almost anywhere else in the RV. Get down and press on it. Soft or spongy floors mean water damage and potentially subfloor rot. A floor replacement is a major repair.

Look at the exterior corners of the RV, especially any area where two different materials meet. Delamination, where the fiberglass or aluminum skin separates from the substrate underneath, shows up as bubbling or waviness in the sidewalls. Press on any suspicious area. A hollow sound or give means the wall construction has been compromised.

## The Mechanical Side: What to Check Under the Hood and Underneath

For towable RVs (fifth wheels and travel trailers), the mechanical inspection shifts to the hitch system, frame, and axles. For motorhomes, you also have an engine and drivetrain to evaluate.

**Towables:** Look at the frame rails for rust, cracks, or any signs of repair welding. Check the axles for proper alignment (a unit that has been in an accident or traveled hard miles off-road will sometimes show a visibly bent axle). Inspect the hitch coupler or fifth wheel pin box for wear and cracks. Spin the tires and look at tread wear patterns. Uneven wear suggests alignment or suspension issues.

**Motorhomes:** Check engine oil, coolant, and transmission fluid. Look under the unit for fresh drips. Start the engine cold if at all possible; a warm engine hides a lot of cold-start problems. Check the chassis battery (separate from house batteries on most motorhomes) and ask for maintenance records. If there are none, price that into your offer.

Tire age matters regardless of tread depth. Find the DOT code on the sidewall. The last four digits tell you the week and year of manufacture. Any tire more than 6 to 7 years old is a blowout risk regardless of how it looks, and RV tires are expensive. A set of six tires on a Class A can run $2,000 to $4,000.

Check the [leveling blocks](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+leveling+blocks&tag=rvsite-20) situation too. It sounds minor, but it tells you something about how the previous owner operated the unit. A set of stacked Lynx blocks or a proper leveling system in good shape is a small sign of a careful owner.

## Step-by-Step Inspection Walkthrough

Use this sequence to stay organized during your inspection. It's easy to get distracted.

1. **Exterior walk (10 minutes):** Walk a full circle around the unit. Look for dents, cracks, delamination, misaligned panels, and evidence of accident repair. Check all exterior compartment door latches.
2. **Roof (15 minutes):** Get up there. Check every penetration, every seam, every soft spot. Note any patches.
3. **Slide-outs (10 minutes):** Run each one in and out twice. Inspect seals and floors at each opening.
4. **Plumbing (15 minutes):** Fill and pressurize, run all water sources, check under every cabinet and around the toilet base.
5. **Electrical (15 minutes):** Test all outlets, all lights, run high-draw appliances simultaneously, check battery state of charge.
6. **Propane (10 minutes):** Test all burners, the oven, and the furnace. Check connections for leaks.
7. **Mechanical (15 minutes):** Check tires, frame, fluids, and under the unit for drips. Cold-start the engine if applicable.
8. **Interior detail (10 minutes):** Open every cabinet, check for soft spots in floors and walls, look for staining on ceilings, and smell for mold.

Total time: about 90 minutes minimum for a competent self-inspection. Add more time for any area that raises concerns.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I really need to hire a professional inspector if I know a lot about RVs?

Even experienced owners benefit from a professional NRVIA-certified inspector. They carry moisture meters, gas leak detectors, and thermal imaging cameras. They find things you won't find with your eyes alone. Moisture behind a wall reads completely differently on a thermal camera than it does from a visual check. If you're spending over $20,000, professional inspection costs are trivial by comparison.

### Can I inspect an RV at a dealership the same way I'd inspect a private sale?

Yes, but expect some resistance. Reputable dealers will give you access to the roof and will let you run all systems. Any dealer who says you can't inspect the roof or who rushes you through the walkthrough is telling you something important. Walk away if you feel pressured.

### What's the single most expensive problem to miss?

Water damage. It touches everything: structural integrity, electrical systems, flooring, cabinetry, and HVAC. A unit with significant water intrusion history can require six figures to restore properly. Roof and slide seal condition are your first line of defense in catching this early.

### How do I check for hidden water damage?

A handheld moisture meter, available for $20 to $40 online, is your best basic tool. Press the probes into walls near slide openings, ceiling corners, and floors at the base of exterior walls. Anything over 15 to 20 percent moisture content in wood substrate is a red flag. Anything over 25 percent means active or recent water intrusion.

### Should I negotiate down the price or walk away if I find problems?

It depends on the problem. Surface cosmetic issues and worn seals are fair grounds for negotiating a lower price. Soft floors, delaminated walls, or a compromised frame are often walk-away situations unless the price is adjusted so dramatically that you're essentially buying it for parts. Know your repair cost estimates before you negotiate.

---

The reality is that most RVs for sale have something wrong with them. That's not cynicism, that's just the nature of vehicles that live outside, travel constantly, and often sit unused between owners. The goal of an inspection isn't to find a perfect unit. It's to understand exactly what you're buying so you can price it correctly and decide whether you want to take it on. Take your time. Bring a flashlight, a moisture meter, and your patience. The right RV is out there, and a solid inspection is what separates a great find from a very expensive story you'll be telling strangers on the internet for years.
