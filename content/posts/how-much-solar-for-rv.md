Most people Google "how much solar do I need for my RV" and land on some manufacturer's page claiming 200 watts is a great starter system. Then they buy it, spend their first dry-camping weekend watching their battery voltage drop while their coffee maker sits unplugged and useless, and wonder what went wrong.

200 watts might work for a weekend warrior charging a phone and running LED lights. For someone actually living in their rig? It's a joke. The real answer depends almost entirely on how you use power, not on what size RV you drive.

## Start Here: Calculate What You Actually Consume

Before you buy a single panel, you need a consumption number. Not a guess. A real number.

Every device in your rig has a wattage. Run it for however many hours per day. Multiply those together and you get watt-hours. Add everything up and you have your daily load. That's the foundation of every solar decision you'll make.

Here's a realistic breakdown from my own 40-foot fifth wheel during a typical full-time desert summer:

| Device | Watts | Hours/Day | Daily Watt-Hours |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residential fridge | 150 | 8 (compressor cycles) | 1,200 |
| Laptop | 65 | 5 | 325 |
| Phone + tablet charging | 30 | 3 | 90 |
| LED lighting | 40 | 4 | 160 |
| 12V fans (x2) | 50 | 10 | 500 |
| Water pump | 60 | 0.5 | 30 |
| Coffee maker | 1,000 | 0.15 | 150 |
| TV + streaming stick | 120 | 3 | 360 |
| **Total** | | | **2,815 Wh/day** |

That's honest. That's comfortable full-time living without air conditioning. Not a worst-case scenario or a fantasy.

Your number will be different. Working remotely with dual monitors? Add 150-200 watts per screen. CPAP machine? Add 30-60 Wh per night. Air fryer instead of the propane oven? Another 300-400 Wh per cooking session. Go through your rig device by device. Nothing gets skipped.

Once you have that daily watt-hour number, everything else clicks into place.

## How Much Solar Panel Wattage You Actually Need

Here's the rule of thumb that actually works: in decent sun (4-5 peak sun hours per day), you need roughly 1.25 to 1.5 watts of panel capacity for every watt-hour of consumption. That buffer accounts for panel inefficiency, charge controller losses, wiring resistance, and the simple fact that the sun doesn't always cooperate.

Using the 2,815 Wh/day example:

2,815 x 1.4 = roughly **3,940 watts of solar panel capacity**

Nearly 4,000 watts. For full-time desert living without a generator running constantly, that's not unreasonable. I currently run 2,400 watts on the roof and supplement with 30-amp shore power hookup a few nights a week. It works. You make peace with some limits.

**Tier 1: Weekend and part-time use (100-400 watts)**

Two or three weekend trips a month. Phone charging, a few LED lights, maybe a 12V fridge. This works fine for what it is.

**Tier 2: Frequent camping, no full-time living (400-800 watts)**

Hybrid campers spending 2-3 weeks a month in the rig. Runs a residential fridge, laptop, lighting, and fans without stress. Air conditioning stays off.

**Tier 3: Full-time living, mild climate (800-1,600 watts)**

Most full-timers start here. Comfortable in spring and fall. Summers in the desert or Pacific Northwest winters will push you toward the generator more than you'd like.

**Tier 4: Full-time living, year-round serious use (1,600-3,000+ watts)**

Remote work, residential appliances, electric pressure cooker, high-consumption everything. This is where I live. Roof space becomes your constraint before your budget does.

## Battery Bank Sizing: Solar Without Enough Storage Is Useless

Solar panels generate power during daylight. You live around the clock. The battery bank is the bridge between those two realities, and undersizing it is the most common mistake I see from people who did their research but didn't think it through.

For lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries, the math is straightforward: size your bank to hold at least 1.5 to 2 days of consumption, usable. LiFePO4 batteries run at 100% usable capacity, so a 200Ah 12V battery holds 2,400 Wh.

For the 2,815 Wh/day example: two days of storage = 5,630 Wh. You'd need roughly 470Ah at 12V in LiFePO4, so two 200Ah or two 250Ah batteries would get you there.

Still running AGM batteries? Use 50% of rated capacity as your usable number. A 200Ah AGM = 100Ah usable. You'd need roughly 940Ah of AGM to match 470Ah of LiFePO4. That's four to five heavy AGM batteries versus two lithium. The weight difference alone is worth the lithium price premium if you're full-timing.

A [battery monitor](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+battery+monitor&tag=contentportfo-20) is non-negotiable. Guessing your state of charge by voltage is like driving without a fuel gauge. I use a Victron BMV-712 and trust it completely.

## Charge Controllers and Inverters: Don't Cut Corners Here

Your panels are only as useful as the charge controller managing them.

**MPPT vs. PWM:** Get an MPPT controller. PWM controllers waste 20-30% of available solar production. On a tiny 200-watt system it's marginal. On a 1,500-watt system it's the difference between full batteries and watching your voltage drop. Victron, Renogy, and EPever make reliable MPPT units.

Size your charge controller to handle your total panel wattage with 25% headroom. A 1,200-watt array at 24V draws about 50 amps, so grab a 60-amp controller at minimum.

**Inverters:** If you're running AC appliances from your battery bank, you need an inverter. Size it based on your peak load, not your average. A 1,000-watt coffee maker plus a 65-watt laptop plus a 120-watt TV equals 1,185 watts peak. Buy a 2,000-watt pure sine wave inverter and you'll never sweat simultaneous loads. Modified sine wave inverters are cheaper and will damage sensitive electronics over time. Pay for pure sine once.

A [surge protector](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+surge+protector&tag=contentportfo-20) for shore power hookups is separate from your inverter but equally important. One bad campground pedestal can fry your entire electrical system. A 30-amp or 50-amp inline surge protector costs $80-300 and has saved my rig at least twice that I know of.

Math says you need 3,000 watts. Your roof holds 1,800 watts. Now what?

A standard RV roof yields somewhere between 15 and 30 square feet of workable panel space after you subtract vents, AC units, antennas, and roof rails. A 400-watt monocrystalline panel runs about 22 square feet. You see how fast roof real estate disappears.

**Portable ground-deploy panels** work well in open desert camping where you can angle them toward the sun and move them as it tracks. A pair of [100-watt portable panels](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=portable+solar+panels+rv&tag=contentportfo-20) folds up and stores in a basement compartment. They're genuinely useful as a supplement.

**Tilting panel mounts** can increase production 15-25% compared to flat-mounted panels in northern latitudes, especially in winter. If your panels are fixed flat, you're leaving production on the table from November through February.

**A generator as part of the system** is not a failure. I run a Honda EU2200i for 2-3 hours on heavy cloudy days and it tops off the batteries faster than a full day of weak sun. Purists will disagree. Purists also run out of power. A generator is a tool, not a crutch, when it's part of a planned system.

Don't forget a quality [water filter](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+water+filter&tag=contentportfo-20) while you're kitting out the rig. Unrelated to solar, but every new full-timer I've met regrets not installing one sooner. The Camco inline filter is a solid, cheap start.
