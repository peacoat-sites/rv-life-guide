Most people Google "how much solar do I need for my RV" and land on some manufacturer's page that tells them 200 watts is "a great starter system." Then they buy it, spend their first dry-camping weekend watching their battery voltage drop while their coffee maker sits unplugged and useless, and wonder what went wrong. What went wrong is that 200 watts might be fine for a weekend warrior charging a phone and running LED lights. It is nowhere near enough for someone actually living in their rig. The answer to "how much solar" depends almost entirely on how you use power, not on what size RV you drive.

## Start Here: Calculate What You Actually Consume

Before you buy a single panel, you need a consumption number. Not an estimate. A real number.

Every device in your rig has a wattage. Run it for some number of hours per day. Multiply those together and you get watt-hours. Add everything up and you have your daily load. That number is the foundation of every solar decision you'll make.

Here's a realistic breakdown from my own 40-foot fifth wheel during a typical full-time desert summer:

| Device | Watts | Hours/Day | Daily Watt-Hours |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residential fridge | 150 | 8 (compressor cycles) | 1,200 |
| Laptop | 65 | 5 | 325 |
| Phone + tablet charging | 30 | 3 | 90 |
| LED lighting | 40 | 4 | 160 |
| 12V fans (x2) | 50 | 10 | 500 |
| Water pump | 60 | 0.5 | 30 |
| Coffee maker | 1,000 | 0.15 | 150 |
| TV + streaming stick | 120 | 3 | 360 |
| **Total** | | | **2,815 Wh/day** |

That's a real, honest load for comfortable full-time living without air conditioning. Not a worst-case. Not a fantasy. Reality.

Your number will be different. If you work remotely and run dual monitors, add 150-200 watts per monitor. If you have a CPAP machine, add 30-60 Wh per night. If you run an air fryer instead of the propane oven, add another 300-400 Wh per cooking session. Go through your rig device by device. Don't skip anything.

Once you have that daily watt-hour number, everything else falls into place.

## How Much Solar Panel Wattage You Actually Need

Here's the rule of thumb that actually holds up in practice: in decent sun conditions (4-5 peak sun hours per day), you need roughly 1.25 to 1.5 watts of panel capacity for every watt-hour of daily consumption. That buffer accounts for panel inefficiency, charge controller losses, wiring resistance, and the reality that the sun doesn't always cooperate.

Using the 2,815 Wh/day example above:

2,815 x 1.4 = roughly **3,940 watts of solar panel capacity**

That's nearly 4,000 watts. For full-time desert living without a generator running constantly, that's not unreasonable. I currently run 2,400 watts on the roof and supplement with a 30-amp shore power hookup a few nights a week. It works, but I made peace with some limits.

Let me break this down into practical tiers so you can see where you actually land:

**Tier 1: Weekend and part-time use (100-400 watts)**
Two or three weekend trips a month. Phone charging, a few LED lights, maybe a 12V fridge. This works. Don't expect more from it.

**Tier 2: Frequent camping, no full-time living (400-800 watts)**
Hybrid campers spending 2-3 weeks a month in the rig. Runs a residential fridge, laptop, lighting, and fans without stress. Air conditioning is still not in the picture.

**Tier 3: Full-time living, mild climate (800-1,600 watts)**
This is where most full-timers start. Comfortable in spring and fall. Summers in the desert or Pacific Northwest winters will push you to the generator more than you'd like.

**Tier 4: Full-time living, year-round serious use (1,600-3,000+ watts)**
Remote work, residential appliances, electric pressure cooker, high-consumption lifestyle. This is where I live. Roof space becomes your constraint before your budget does.

## Battery Bank Sizing: Solar Without Enough Storage Is Useless

Solar panels generate power during daylight. You live around the clock. The battery bank is the bridge, and undersizing it is the most common mistake I see from people who did "enough research."

For lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries, the math is simple: size your bank to hold at least 1.5 to 2 days of consumption, usable. LiFePO4 batteries are rated at 100% usable capacity, so a 200Ah 12V battery holds 2,400 Wh.

For the 2,815 Wh/day example: two days of storage = 5,630 Wh. You'd need roughly 470Ah at 12V in LiFePO4, so two 200Ah or two 250Ah batteries would get you close.

If you're still running AGM batteries, use 50% of rated capacity as your usable number. A 200Ah AGM = 100Ah usable. You'd need roughly 940Ah of AGM to equal 470Ah of LiFePO4. That's four to five heavy AGM batteries versus two lithium. The weight difference alone is worth the lithium price premium if you're full-timing.

A [battery monitor](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+battery+monitor&tag=rvlifeguide-20) is non-negotiable. Guessing your state of charge by voltage is like driving without a fuel gauge. It works until it doesn't, and when it doesn't, you're stuck. I use a Victron BMV-712 and trust it completely.

## Charge Controllers and Inverters: Don't Cut Corners Here

Your panels are only as useful as the charge controller managing them.

**MPPT vs. PWM:** Get an MPPT controller. End of discussion. PWM controllers waste 20-30% of available solar production. On a small 200-watt system it's marginal. On a 1,500-watt system it's the difference between full batteries and frustration. Victron, Renogy, and EPever make reliable MPPT units at different price points.

Size your charge controller to handle your total panel wattage with 25% headroom. A 1,200-watt array at 24V draws about 50 amps. Get a 60-amp controller at minimum.

**Inverters:** If you're running AC appliances from your battery bank, you need an inverter. Inverter sizing depends on your peak load, not your average. A 1,000-watt coffee maker plus a 65-watt laptop plus a 120-watt TV equals 1,185 watts peak. Buy a 2,000-watt pure sine wave inverter and you'll never sweat simultaneous loads. Modified sine wave inverters are cheaper and will damage sensitive electronics over time. Pay for pure sine once.

A [surge protector](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+surge+protector&tag=rvlifeguide-20) for shore power hookups is separate from your inverter situation but equally important. One bad campground pedestal can fry your entire electrical system. A 30-amp or 50-amp inline surge protector costs $80-300 and has saved my rig at least twice that I know about.

## Roof Space, Portable Panels, and Real-World Constraints

Math says you need 3,000 watts. Your roof holds 1,800 watts. Now what?

This is the conversation nobody wants to have before they buy. A standard RV roof yields somewhere between 15 and 30 square feet of workable panel space after you subtract vents, AC units, antennas, and roof rails. A 400-watt monocrystalline panel runs about 22 square feet. You can see how fast roof real estate disappears.

Options when the roof isn't enough:

**Portable ground-deploy panels** work well in open desert camping where you can angle them toward the sun and move them as it tracks. A pair of [100-watt portable panels](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=portable+solar+panels+rv&tag=rvlifeguide-20) folds up and stores in a basement compartment. They're genuinely useful as a supplement, not as a primary system.

**Tilting panel mounts** can increase production 15-25% compared to flat-mounted panels in northern latitudes, especially in winter. If your panels are fixed flat, you're leaving production on the table from November through February.

**A generator as part of the system** is not a failure. I run a Honda EU2200i for 2-3 hours on heavy cloudy days and it tops off the batteries faster than a full day of weak sun. Purists will disagree. Purists also run out of power. A generator is a tool, not a crutch, when it's part of a planned system.

Don't forget a quality [water filter](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+water+filter&tag=rvlifeguide-20) while you're kitting out the rig. Unrelated to solar, but every new full-timer I've met regrets not installing one sooner. The Camco inline filter is a solid, cheap start.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can 200 watts of solar power an RV full-time?

No. Not unless your total daily power consumption is under 600 Wh, which means no residential fridge, no laptop work, minimal lighting, and no cooking appliances. 200 watts is appropriate for weekend trips with very light loads. Full-timers need to start the conversation at 800 watts minimum and go from there.

### Is 400Ah of lithium battery enough for full-time RV living?

It depends entirely on your consumption. For a moderate full-time setup drawing 1,500-2,000 Wh per day, 400Ah at 12V gives you roughly 4,800 Wh of storage, which is 2.5 to 3 days of capacity. That's a solid buffer. If your daily load is 3,000+ Wh, you'll feel constrained on cloudy days without a generator backup.

### Do I really need lithium batteries or will AGM work?

AGM works. It's heavier, has half the usable capacity per pound, charges more slowly, and doesn't tolerate deep discharges as well. If you're a weekend camper, a good AGM bank is fine and costs less upfront. If you're full-timing, the weight penalty and reduced lifespan of AGM usually make lithium the better total investment over 3-5 years. I made the switch to LiFePO4 in year three and haven't looked back.

### What's the best solar panel brand for RVs?

I've had good experiences with Renogy and Rich Solar for panel quality at reasonable price points. SunPower makes excellent high-efficiency panels if roof space is your constraint. For charge controllers and monitoring, Victron is the gold standard and worth the premium. Avoid the no-name panels on discount sites. The savings aren't worth the uncertainty when you're relying on them daily.

### Can I run air conditioning on solar?

Yes, but it's expensive to do right. A rooftop RV AC unit draws 1,300-1,800 watts while running and cycles frequently. Running it for 8 hours could consume 7,000-10,000+ Wh in a day. To support that on solar alone, you'd need 4,000+ watts of panels and a substantial battery bank, plus you'd need peak sun hours to align with peak cooling demand. Some full-timers make it work with large lithium banks and 3,000-4,000 watts of solar. Most use solar to offset the generator load rather than eliminate it entirely. It's a system design question, not a simple yes or no.

The honest bottom line: most people undersize their solar system because the upfront cost is real and the consequences feel hypothetical. They feel very real at 10pm when your battery monitor reads 20% and you have three more hours of work to finish. Size for your actual life, not your optimistic version of it. Get the consumption math right, buy a battery monitor before anything else, and build the system to match what you genuinely do in your rig every day. The investment pays back in freedom, not in some abstract sense but in the concrete ability to park anywhere you want and stay as long as you like.
