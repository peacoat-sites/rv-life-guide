You wake up at a campground outside Sedona, Arizona, with what feels like a kidney stone. The pain is real. The nearest urgent care is 12 miles away. Your insurance card lists a primary care physician back in Ohio you haven't seen in three years. This moment catches most new full-timers completely off guard, more than almost anything else about life on the road. Healthcare is the single most complicated logistical puzzle in the full-time RV world. But it's absolutely solvable if you understand your options before the emergency happens.

## Why Traditional Health Insurance Falls Apart on the Road

Most employer-sponsored health plans and even individual ACA marketplace plans are built around a specific service area or network. A Blue Cross plan tied to Ohio might cover emergencies nationwide, but routine care, prescriptions, and follow-up visits become out-of-network the moment you cross a state line. You're suddenly paying 40 to 60 percent of costs instead of a flat copay.

It gets worse when you realize that full-time RVers need to establish a "domicile state" for insurance purposes. Your domicile is where you're legally a resident, and it determines which plans you can even purchase. South Dakota, Texas, and Florida are the three most popular domicile states for full-timers because of their favorable tax laws and flexible residency requirements. That means you need a plan that works well in those states AND everywhere else you travel.

If you're still in early planning stages, our [full-time RV living guide](/full-time-rv-living-guide/) covers domicile setup, mail forwarding, and the broader logistics of making this lifestyle work legally. Get that foundation right first, and insurance becomes much easier to sort out.

## The Main Healthcare Options Worth Considering

There's no single "best" answer here. Your age, health status, budget, and travel style all play a role.

**ACA Marketplace Plans With Nationwide Networks**

Some ACA plans, particularly those offered through Blue Cross Blue Shield's "BlueCard" program, provide nationwide PPO access. A PPO lets you see any licensed provider without a referral, and the BlueCard network is accepted at most hospitals and urgent care centers across the country. If you're under 65 and not covered by an employer, this is often the first thing to look at. A 45-year-old in South Dakota might pay $450 to $700 per month for a mid-tier silver plan depending on income and subsidy eligibility.

When shopping ACA plans, search for "PPO" not "HMO." HMO plans require you to stay within a local network, which is useless when you're in a new state every few weeks.

**Health Share Ministries**

Health sharing plans like Sedera, Liberty HealthShare, and Zion Health aren't insurance in the traditional sense. Members contribute monthly to a shared pool that pays for qualifying medical expenses. Monthly costs run 30 to 50 percent lower than ACA premiums. A healthy 40-year-old couple might pay $500 to $600 a month combined versus $1,200 or more for a comparable ACA plan.

The trade-offs are real, though. Pre-existing conditions are often excluded for a period of time or permanently. Mental health coverage is limited. And there's no legal guarantee your bill gets paid the way it does with insurance. You're relying on the community and the organization's integrity. That said, many long-term RVers swear by these plans and have had positive experiences with major claims. Go in with open eyes.

**Medicare for Full-Timers 65 and Older**

Original Medicare (Parts A and B) is one of the most RV-friendly options available because it covers you at any Medicare-accepting provider in the country. The catch is that Medicare Advantage plans typically work like HMOs with local networks. Many full-timers over 65 find that original Medicare plus a Medigap supplemental policy works cleanest. You'll pay a monthly premium for Medigap (usually $100 to $200+ depending on age and plan type), but your out-of-pocket costs become very predictable.

For a deeper look at structuring your overall health coverage as a full-timer, check out our guide on [health insurance for full-time RV living](/health-insurance-full-time-rv/).

**Direct Primary Care (DPC)**

This model deserves more attention. Direct primary care practices charge a flat monthly membership fee, usually $50 to $150 per month, for unlimited access to a primary care physician. No insurance claims. No copays. Often same-day telehealth appointments. For full-timers, the telehealth component is gold. You can consult with your DPC doctor from a Montana campsite about a prescription refill or a symptom question without leaving your rig.

DPC doesn't replace insurance for hospitalizations or major events, but paired with a high-deductible ACA plan or a health share membership, it fills the day-to-day gap at a very reasonable cost.

## Telehealth: The Game-Changer You Should Use More

Telehealth isn't just a pandemic novelty. It's genuinely useful for a road-based lifestyle. Platforms like Teladoc, MDLive, and Amazon Clinic let you connect with a licensed physician in minutes for sinus infections, UTIs, skin rashes, and prescription refills. Many insurance plans now include telehealth at no additional cost. Even without insurance, a virtual visit costs $75 or less on some platforms.

The practical move is identifying a telehealth provider before you need one and confirming that prescriptions can be sent to a pharmacy chain with nationwide locations. CVS, Walgreens, and Walmart all work well. If you use a specialty medication or have an ongoing condition, call ahead and ask how they handle chronic condition management. Some do it well. Some don't.

## A Step-by-Step Approach to Building Your RV Healthcare Plan

1. **Lock in your domicile state.** South Dakota and Texas are the two most RV-friendly choices. Your domicile determines which insurance marketplace you shop on and which plans are available.

2. **Assess your health needs honestly.** Do you have chronic conditions? Take regular prescriptions? If yes, an ACA PPO plan is probably your safest bet. If you're healthy and under 50, a health share plan plus a DPC membership is worth pricing out.

3. **Shop the ACA marketplace with one filter in mind.** Search specifically for PPO plans with multistate or national networks. In South Dakota and Texas, you'll typically find good options. Call the insurance company directly before enrolling and ask: "Will I have in-network access to providers and urgent care centers in California, Colorado, Florida, and the Pacific Northwest?" If they hem and haw, keep looking.

4. **Sign up for a DPC practice or telehealth membership.** Even if you have solid insurance, having a known doctor you can reach by phone or video is priceless on the road. Many DPC practices work remotely with no geographic restrictions.

5. **Build a medication buffer.** Work with your doctor to get 90-day prescription supplies when possible. Many insurers allow this for maintenance medications. Pair this with a GoodRx card or SingleCare discount card for cash-pay situations.

6. **Know your nearest facilities.** Apps like Solv and Zocdoc help you find urgent care and primary care options wherever you're parked. Download them now. The iERASS app and Google Maps "urgent care near me" search also work in a pinch.

7. **Keep digital records.** Store your key medical records, medication list, allergy history, and insurance cards in a secure app like Apple Health or a password-protected PDF. When you're in a new state ER, having this information ready speeds everything up.

Healthcare costs money. There's no way around it. But full-timers have some cost management tools that stick-and-brick residents often don't think about.

The biggest one is income-based ACA subsidies. If you're self-employed, retired early, or working remotely and controlling your taxable income, you may qualify for significant premium tax credits. A couple earning $60,000 a year might pay only $200 to $400 a month combined for a solid silver plan after subsidies. This requires some planning around your income sources, but it's real money worth pursuing with a tax professional who understands the nuance.

A Health Savings Account (HSA) paired with a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) is another legitimate strategy. You can contribute up to $4,150 per year as an individual or $8,300 as a family in 2024, and those dollars go in pre-tax. The funds roll over indefinitely and can be invested. Over five or ten years of full-timing, a well-funded HSA becomes a serious financial cushion for medical costs.
