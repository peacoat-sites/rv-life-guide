The moving truck pulls away, and you're standing in a driveway that is no longer yours, holding the keys to a 28-foot travel trailer. Your phone has seventeen unread texts from friends asking if you've lost your mind. Your stomach is doing something complicated. I know that feeling. I stood in almost exactly that spot in September 2019, and I've put 90,000 miles behind me since. Here's what I wish someone had told me before I ever signed a single piece of paperwork.

---

## Figure Out Your "Why" Before You Buy Anything

This sounds like therapy-speak, but it's actually practical advice with real financial consequences.

People start RV life for wildly different reasons, and those reasons should drive every decision that follows. Are you trying to cut housing costs in an expensive city? Are you a remote worker who wants to spend winters in Arizona and summers in Montana? Are you retired with a pension and a bucket list of national parks? Or are you a family with kids who wants to unschool on the road?

Each of those scenarios calls for a completely different rig, a different budget, and a different pace of travel.

I've watched people spend $180,000 on a Class A motorhome because they wanted freedom, only to park it in one RV resort for six months because they hated driving something the size of a city bus. I've also watched minimalists buy a $9,000 cargo van conversion and discover they desperately needed a bathroom at 2 a.m. on a forest road in Wyoming.

Be honest with yourself about your physical needs, your travel pace, and your tolerance for discomfort. Write it down. Seriously, put it on paper. It will save you from a catastrophically expensive wrong purchase.

## Choosing the Right Rig: Don't Buy More Than You Need

The RV industry will happily sell you as much vehicle as your credit score allows. That is not the same as selling you the right vehicle.

Here's a rough breakdown of the main options:

| Rig Type | Typical Cost (Used) | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Class A Motorhome | $40,000 to $300,000+ | Spacious, self-contained | Terrible fuel economy, hard to park, expensive to repair |
| Class C Motorhome | $25,000 to $120,000 | Good balance of size and drivability | Still 10-12 MPG, costly chassis repairs |
| Class B / Camper Van | $30,000 to $120,000 | Easy to drive, stealth camp, great MPG | Tiny living space, no dedicated bedroom |
| Travel Trailer | $8,000 to $60,000 | Separate tow vehicle, cheaper to buy | Requires learning to back up, extra vehicle costs |
| 5th Wheel | $20,000 to $80,000 | Very stable tow, residential feel | Needs a truck, hitch eats up truck bed |

I started with a 28-foot travel trailer towed by a used Ram 1500, which was fine for two people and a dog. But I'd driven a car for twenty years and had never towed anything. The first time I had to back that trailer into a narrow campsite while a line of irritated RVers watched, I wanted to drive it into a river.

If you've never towed before, budget for a towing class. They're usually $150 to $300 and worth every cent.

One more thing: buy used. A new RV loses 20 to 30 percent of its value in the first year. Buy something two to four years old, get a pre-purchase inspection from an independent RV technician (not the selling dealer), and save yourself thousands.

## The Budget Reality: What Full-Time RV Life Actually Costs

This is where I'll lose some of you, and that's okay. The truth is better than a fantasy.

Full-time RV life is not automatically cheaper than renting an apartment. For some people, it's actually more expensive, at least in the first year when everything seems to break at once.

My first year costs broke down something like this:

**Fixed monthly costs:**
- RV loan payment: $480
- RV insurance: $110
- Health insurance (self-employed): $340
- Cell phone (two lines with hotspot): $180
- Campground memberships (Thousand Trails + Passport America): $90 amortized monthly

**Variable monthly costs:**
- Fuel: $300 to $600 depending on how much we moved
- Campsite fees (nights not covered by memberships): $200 to $400
- Groceries: $500 to $600
- Maintenance and repairs: $150 to $800 (this is the wild card)

Total range: roughly $2,300 to $3,500 per month. For two people in a mid-cost-of-living city, that's comparable to renting. Not some miraculous savings windfall.

The maintenance budget is the one that gets people. In six years, I've replaced two water pumps, three sets of trailer tires, a refrigerator, an air conditioner, slide-out seals, a sewer valve, and a water heater. None of that was dramatic. All of it was expensive. Keep a minimum $2,000 emergency fund specifically for RV repairs and don't touch it for anything else.

## Essential Gear That's Actually Worth Buying

The RV accessory market is enormous and full of junk. Here's what actually matters.

**Electrical protection** is non-negotiable. Shore power at campgrounds is notoriously dirty, with voltage spikes and low-voltage conditions that fry appliances and air conditioners. A [progressive surge protector](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+surge+protector+30+amp&tag=rvlife-20) rated for your service (30-amp or 50-amp) sits between the pedestal and your RV and monitors incoming power. I've seen it cut power automatically during a bad voltage event and probably save someone's entire electrical system. Budget $80 to $200 depending on amperage.

**Leveling blocks** sound boring until you've tried sleeping on a slant for three nights. [Andersen camper leveling blocks](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=andersen+rv+leveling+blocks&tag=rvlife-20) are the ones I've used for years. They're simple, durable, and they work for both wheel chocking and leveling on uneven sites.

**Water filtration** matters more than most beginners expect. RV fresh water tanks and campground water can carry sediment, chlorine, and in some places worse. A [two-stage inline water filter](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+inline+water+filter&tag=rvlife-20) on your city water connection is a $25 to $40 fix that makes your water taste dramatically better and protects your lines.

**Battery monitoring** becomes essential the moment you start boondocking or running on solar. You can't manage what you can't measure. A [Victron SmartShunt](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=victron+smartshunt+battery+monitor&tag=rvlife-20) or similar battery monitor tells you your state of charge, amps in and out, and how many hours of power you have left. It's the difference between confidently running your laptop all evening and waking up at midnight with a dead refrigerator.

**Solar panels** deserve their own article, but even a modest 200-watt panel setup with a quality charge controller can dramatically reduce your generator use and campground fees. If you're planning to boondock at all, [portable solar panels](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=portable+rv+solar+panel+kit&tag=rvlife-20) are worth the investment. Start small and add capacity as you learn your actual consumption.

## Finding Places to Sleep: Campgrounds, Boondocking, and Everything In Between

Where you sleep determines a huge chunk of your budget and your experience of RV life. Most beginners default to KOA and similar private campgrounds, which is fine but expensive, often $50 to $80 per night.

Here's the full spectrum of your options:

**Boondocking on public land** is free or nearly free. Bureau of Land Management (BLM) land and National Forests allow dispersed camping, typically for 14 days in any one spot. Apps like iOverlander and FreeRoam help you find specific sites. The catch is you need to be self-sufficient: solar or generator for power, enough fresh water, and a way to manage your gray and black tanks.

**Campground memberships** can make financial sense if you travel enough to use them. Thousand Trails zones work well for slow travelers. Harvest Hosts ($99/year) lets you stay at wineries, breweries, farms, and attractions for free. I've stayed at some genuinely magical places through Harvest Hosts.

**Casino parking lots** are a sleeper option (pun intended). Many casinos allow free RV overnight parking in exchange for you spending a little money inside. Not glamorous, but useful for a travel night.

**Walmart and Cracker Barrel** famously allow overnight parking in many locations, though you should always call ahead because local ordinances have closed many of these lots in recent years.

My honest advice: plan your first month at established campgrounds with full hookups. Pay the extra money. You need the time to learn your systems, find what leaks, discover what you forgot to pack, and just breathe. Launching straight into boondocking when you've never even filled a fresh water tank is a recipe for a very stressful beginning.

## Making Money on the Road: Remote Work, Seasonal Jobs, and the Mail Thing

Two questions I get constantly: "How do you afford it?" and "Where is your mail?"

On the money side: you need a plan before you leave. Don't quit your job and hope something works out. If you have a remote-capable job, negotiate that arrangement before you give notice on your house. If you're self-employed or freelance, make sure your income is genuinely location-independent and test it for a few months before going full-time.

Workamping is a real option. Campgrounds and resorts hire RVers to work in exchange for a free site plus sometimes hourly pay. Amazon's CamperForce program hires RVers seasonally at fulfillment centers and provides park model lodging. It's not passive income, but it covers your site costs during a stationary stretch.

The domicile question is genuinely complicated. You'll need a legal state of residency for your driver's license, vehicle registration, and taxes. South Dakota, Texas, and Florida are the three states most full-timers use because they have no state income tax and relatively straightforward residency processes. You'll need a mail forwarding service: Escapees RV Club, Traveling Mailbox, and PostScan Mail are the most popular. Escapees also operates a physical mail forwarding address in Livingston, Texas, which many full-timers use as their legal domicile.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should I try RV life before deciding if it's for me?

Give it six months minimum, and plan those six months to include at least one stretch of real boondocking, at least one long driving week, and at least one mechanical problem. The honeymoon phase lasts about eight weeks. After that, you find out whether you actually like the lifestyle or just the idea of it. A lot of people discover they love it more after the honeymoon ends. Some discover it's not for them, and that's a completely valid outcome.

### Do I need to buy an RV, or can I rent first?

Renting first is smart, but know that rental RVs are often poorly maintained and equipped very differently from what you'd actually live in full-time. Try to rent for two weeks minimum, not a weekend. RVshare and Outdoorsy are the main peer-to-peer rental platforms. A two-week rental will tell you whether you can tolerate the space and the driving. It won't fully simulate full-time life, but it's a much cheaper education than buying the wrong rig.

### What do I do about healthcare as a full-timer?

This is a real challenge and I won't pretend otherwise. If you're employed with benefits, protect those as long as possible. If you're self-employed, look at ACA marketplace plans. Many full-timers use their legal domicile state to access plans and then use urgent care clinics and telehealth for day-to-day needs. Sedera and Liberty HealthShare are health cost-sharing programs that some RVers use as an alternative to traditional insurance. Research them carefully because they are not insurance and have coverage limitations.

### Is full-time RV life safe for solo women?

Yes, and I say that as a woman who has done significant stretches solo. The RV community skews older and is generally watchful in a good way. Common sense applies: research your campgrounds and boondocking spots, trust your gut about situations that feel wrong, and invest in a quality door lock brace and carbon monoxide detector. Apps like iOverlander let you read reviews from other travelers. Solo women RVers have a strong online community: groups like Solo Women RV, Girls RV, and others are full of honest advice.

### What's the single biggest mistake first-time full-timers make?

Moving too fast. New full-timers often have this urge to cover ground immediately, to see everything at once because they can. Then they're exhausted, spending a fortune on fuel, and never actually sitting still long enough to feel like anywhere is home. I drove 14,000 miles in my first four months. It nearly broke me. Now I average 500 to 800 miles a week at most, stay places for a week or more, and I feel the difference completely.

Six years in, 90,000 miles, a refrigerator replacement I didn't see coming, and a thousand sunsets that nobody who stayed home got to see. RV life is real life, just with wheels under it, which means it has the same problems and the same joys, just rearranged. Go in with your eyes open, your emergency fund funded, and realistic expectations, and you'll figure out pretty quickly whether this is the life for you. Most people who do it seriously don't regret the leap. They regret not taking it sooner.
