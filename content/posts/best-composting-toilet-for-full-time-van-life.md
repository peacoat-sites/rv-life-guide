Nobody warns you about the smell. That's the thing. You spend weeks researching vans, researching solar setups, researching mattress foam density, and then you're six months into full-time [van life](/how-much-does-van-life-cost-per-month/) and you're dealing with a situation under your bed that you were not emotionally prepared for.

I've been living and sleeping in rigs for eight years now. I've used cassette toilets, a bucket with a trash bag (once, in desperation, never again), and four different composting toilets across two vans and one stint in a converted school bus. I don't have a sponsored opinion here. I have an opinion formed by cleaning out a urine diverter in a Walmart [parking lot](/rv-parking-lot-tips/) in Flagstaff in January.

So if you're sitting there trying to figure out which composting toilet makes sense for van life specifically, not for an RV with a bathroom the size of a closet, but for a van where every inch is a negotiation, here's what I tell people.

## Why Van Life Is a Different Category

Here's what most composting toilet reviews get wrong: they're written for people with bathroom compartments. You're not those people. In a van, your toilet might be 18 inches from where you cook dinner and 24 inches from where you sleep. The margin for error on odor control is basically zero.

The composting toilet category works on a simple premise: separate liquids from solids, and solids don't smell bad. That's true. What's also true is that any moisture getting into the solids chamber from condensation, splashback, or a poorly seated urine diverter will create ammonia smell fast. In a van, you'll notice before you would in a larger space. This isn't a reason not to use one. It's a reason to understand how they actually work before you buy.

Ventilation is the other thing people underestimate. Every composting toilet worth owning comes with or requires a small 12V computer fan, roughly 40-80mm, that runs continuously and vents air from the solids chamber directly outside. In a van, you're running a short vent hose (usually 1.5 to 2 inches in diameter) through the floor or a sidewall. If you don't do this, it doesn't matter which toilet you bought. You'll hate it.

**Nature's Head** is the one people have been recommending since roughly 2010, and honestly, the reputation is earned. The build quality is tank-like. The agitator handle that mixes the solids with your coir medium is satisfying to use and genuinely effective. It's made in the USA, the company actually answers the phone, and parts are available. The footprint is 13.5 x 17.5 inches. That's not small for a van, but most people make it work under a platform bed or in a dedicated toilet area.

**The Air Head** is the Nature's Head's main competitor, and I've used both. The Air Head is lighter (about 8 lbs vs. 13 lbs for the Nature's Head), slightly more compact, and uses a different agitating system (a rotating drum instead of a handle). Some people find it easier to service. The one thing I'll say: the plastic on the Air Head feels less substantial. That might not matter for years. It's something I noticed.

**The Separett Villa 9215** is interesting and a bit different. It doesn't try to compost anything in the unit itself. It separates urine into a bottle and drops solids into a bag-lined container. You bag and trash the solids instead of composting them. For van life, this is actually not a bad approach. Emptying a bag into a dumpster is easier than finding a composting site. The Separett requires 12V power to run its fan and the urine-diverter seat. Not a problem in most van builds.

**The OGO** ($639 currently) is the newcomer that's gotten real traction in the #vanlife community over the last couple of years, partly because it has an electric agitator you trigger with a button. No crank handle. The liquid bottle is front-access, which is genuinely nice in a tight space. I haven't owned one personally, but two readers I trust have used them for 8-plus months without complaints.

**The Cuddy** is the cheapest option here and it shows in some of the fit and finish. For occasional use or someone on a tight budget just getting started, fine. For full-time use, I'd stretch the budget.

## What Actually Makes the Difference Day to Day

The medium you use in the solids chamber matters more than most people expect. Coco coir bricks (usually around $12-15 for a brick that expands to fill the chamber) are what most people use, and they work well. Peat moss also works but it's heavier and less uniform. I made the mistake early on of using potting soil once, thinking it would be fine. The moisture content was too variable and I had a bad two weeks. Stick to coco coir.

Real scenario: a reader named Marcus from Portland switched from a Nature's Head with peat moss to coco coir last year and told me his emptying interval went from about 3 weeks to nearly 5 weeks for one person. Same toilet, different medium, meaningfully different result.

For the liquid situation, plan on emptying your bottle every 2-3 days if you're using the toilet full-time alone, more often with two people. Some people add a little water and a drop of dish soap to the bottle to cut smell. Others use a small amount of enzyme cleaner. I've done both. The enzyme approach (I've used BioKleen Bac-Out, about $9 for a spray bottle) is noticeably better.

Another scenario worth thinking through: if you're boondocking for a week, 7 days of liquid adds up fast. One person might be fine with a large bottle (the Nature's Head comes with a 2.2-gallon bottle). Two people sharing a van should plan for a secondary collection strategy or be prepared to find a dump site or bathroom option mid-week.

## Installation in a Van

This is where I'd tell you to think carefully before you buy. Mounting a composting toilet in a van isn't complicated, but the vent hose routing needs planning. You want the hose to exit the van on the downwind side if possible, ideally through the floor rather than a sidewall, because sidewall exits can suck exhaust or smell back in when you're parked facing into the wind.

The fan pulls roughly 0.2-0.4 amps continuously. Over 24 hours that's about 5-10 amp-hours per day. Not nothing, but manageable on any van solar system with even 100W of panels and a 100Ah battery. Wire it directly to your battery with an inline fuse (a 1-amp fuse is fine) rather than through a switch. You want it running all the time, including when you're away from the van.

Scenario with outcome: a couple I met at a Desert Southwest [dispersed camping](/dispersed-camping-guide/) spot had installed their Nature's Head without running the vent fan continuously. They were switching it on and off to save power. They reported persistent odor and assumed the toilet was the problem. They rewired to run the fan 24/7 and the odor issue resolved within two days.

If space is the binding constraint, the OGO or the Air Head are the two I'd look at hardest. The Cuddy's smaller footprint is appealing on paper but I'd want more long-term data before recommending it for full-time use.

### FAQ

#### Which composting toilet is best for one person living full-time in a van?

The Nature's Head is the most proven option for one person and has the longest track record in full-time use. If you're on a tighter budget or need a smaller footprint, the OGO is worth serious consideration as of 2026.

#### How often do you really need to empty a composting toilet in a van?

For one person, liquid every 2-3 days and solids every 4-6 weeks using coco coir. Two people roughly halve those intervals. Climate and diet affect it more than most people expect.

#### Do composting toilets actually smell in a van?

With continuous venting and proper liquid separation, no. The smell people associate with composting toilets almost always traces back to a vent fan that's switched off, liquid getting into the solids, or a poorly fitted diverter. The toilet design isn't the problem in those cases.

#### Can two people realistically share one composting toilet in a van?

Yes, but liquid management becomes a daily task rather than every-other-day. Some couples keep a secondary container (a sealed gallon jug) for overflow. It's not glamorous, but it's workable.

#### Is it legal to dump composting toilet waste in regular trash?

Solid waste in bags is generally legal to trash in most U.S. states when it's been processed with a composting medium, but regulations vary by county and state. Liquid can typically be poured down any standard drain or toilet. Always check local ordinances; this is one area where I genuinely can't give you a universal answer.
