Trailer sway has tried to kill me exactly once.

I was westbound on I-70 in Colorado, loaded down with about 400 extra pounds of gear I probably shouldn't have had in the back of my travel trailer, when a semi passed me at around 70 mph. The pressure wave hit, the tail started to swing, I overcorrected, and for about three seconds I was completely at the mercy of physics. I got it back. A lot of people don't.

That was year two of full-timing. I'd been running without a sway control device because I thought, honestly, that it was one of those upsell items the hitch guys try to push on you before you leave the dealership. I was wrong, and I nearly paid for that mistake on a mountain highway.

So if you're sitting here with a new trailer or a tow vehicle you're not totally sure about, wondering whether a sway control hitch is something you actually need or just something someone wants to sell you, I understand exactly where you are. Let me tell you what I've learned, mostly from doing it wrong first.

## What Trailer Sway Actually Is (And Why It Happens)

Sway isn't random. It has causes, and most of them are preventable once you know what you're looking for.

The physics version: your trailer is a pendulum attached to the back of your vehicle. Anything that pushes the tail sideways (a passing truck, a wind gust, a sudden lane change, an uneven road) starts that pendulum moving. If you have nothing to dampen that motion, each oscillation builds on the last one. That's how a small wobble becomes a full tank-slapper inside about four seconds.

The real-world causes I see most often are tongue weight issues and speed. Most people run too little tongue weight, which makes the trailer tail-heavy and much more prone to swinging. The rule is 10 to 15 percent of your loaded trailer weight should be sitting on the hitch ball. So a 7,000-pound trailer needs 700 to 1,050 pounds of tongue weight. Not at the hitch ball itself (that's what a weight distribution system handles), but in terms of front-to-back balance of the trailer's load.

Speed matters more than people want to admit. I know 65 feels slow when everyone's passing you, but a lot of rigs that are stable at 60 become genuinely unpredictable at 72. I keep a firm 62 mph limit in anything over 24 feet. That's not timid; that's physics.

## The Two Types of Sway Control: Friction vs. Active

You might be wondering why there are so many different products, different price points, and wildly different setups when you go looking for sway control solutions. It comes down to two fundamentally different approaches, and they're not equally good.

**Friction sway control bars** are the older, cheaper technology. A single arm connects your hitch head to the trailer A-frame with a sliding mechanism that creates resistance when the trailer tries to swing. They work. Sort of. They dampen sway once it's already starting, but they don't prevent it. They also require you to manually disconnect them before backing up, which means you'll either forget once and damage something, or you'll just get tired of the hassle. Reese and Husky both make decent versions in the $80 to $150 range. I used one for about eighteen months.

**Active or integrated sway control** is built into weight distribution hitches from brands like Equalizer, Andersen, and the Reese Pro series. Instead of a friction bar, the weight distribution bars themselves apply lateral resistance across the whole system, which means sway is being managed continuously, not just reacted to. These setups run $400 to $1,200 depending on the brand and your weight rating, and yes, that's a real chunk of money.

Here's what I tell people who ask whether to go with friction or integrated: if you're hauling anything over 5,000 pounds, or if you're planning to spend serious time on interstates, don't bother with a friction bar. Get a proper weight distribution hitch with integrated sway control and do it once. The Equalizer 4-Point system (currently around $900 to $1,100 as of June 2026) is what I run now, and it's the most confident I've ever felt towing.

## Weight Distribution: You Can't Skip This Part

A sway control bar on a hitch that isn't properly distributing weight is like putting a good tire on a car with a broken suspension. It helps a little. Not enough.

Weight distribution hitches work by transferring some of the tongue weight load back onto the front axle of your tow vehicle, which lifts the rear of the tow vehicle back up and restores steering feel. If your truck or SUV is squatting visibly in the rear when you hook up, you need weight distribution before you even think about sway control. A squatting tow vehicle means your front wheels have less contact pressure, which means you're steering with maybe 60 percent of the grip you think you have.

A reader named Marcus from outside Flagstaff emailed me last fall after his F-150 passed the GVWR check but kept feeling "floaty" in the front end when towing his 28-foot Keystone. He was running a friction bar and no WD hitch. Once he switched to a Reese Towpower Pro Round Bar WD kit (about $480) and got it properly set up, his front-end steering feel came back, and the floatiness went with it. He drove from Arizona to Michigan that winter without an incident.

## Setting Up Your Hitch Correctly (Where Most People Get It Wrong)

I thought for a long time that hitch setup was something you just did once at the dealer and forgot about. That's not right. And honestly, most dealer setups I've seen are mediocre at best.

Here's the process that actually matters:

**Step 1: Measure your hitch height unloaded.** With nothing hitched up, measure from the ground to the top of the hitch ball. Write it down.

**Step 2: Couple up, fully loaded.** Hook the trailer up with your actual travel load inside. Measure the front and rear of the trailer frame at the same point, relative to the ground.

**Step 3: Check your tow vehicle's squat.** Measure the front and rear wheel wells of your tow vehicle before and after coupling. You want less than 1.5 inches of rear drop and ideally no change at the front.

**Step 4: Adjust spring bar tension.** On most WD hitches, you'll add or remove chain links (or adjust the cam on Andersen-style systems) until your vehicle sits close to its unloaded stance.

**Step 5: Set sway control friction.** On friction bar systems, set the handle somewhere between 5 and 7 on a 1 to 10 scale. Tight enough to dampen, loose enough not to cause tire wear or excess heat.

Most people skip steps 1 through 3 and then wonder why the setup doesn't feel right. Spend 45 minutes doing this properly and your rig will feel like a different vehicle.

Concrete example from my own driveway: When I first set up my Equalizer on my 32-foot trailer, I had the spring bars tensioned about two chain links too loose. The trailer felt okay, but I noticed slight porpoising over freeway expansion joints at 60 mph. I tightened two links per side, re-measured my wheel wells (front came back up about 0.4 inches), and that porpoising disappeared completely. Small adjustment, real difference.

## What to Spend and What to Skip

Current pricing as of June 2026: a quality friction sway bar runs $80 to $150. A basic WD hitch with friction sway control like the Husky Center Line TS is around $350 to $450. A mid-tier integrated system like the Reese Strait-Line starts around $500. The Equalizer 4-Point is $900 to $1,100. The Andersen WD hitch (which uses a different, no-chains design) runs about $650 to $750 and has a passionate fan base, though I find the Equalizer easier to dial in precisely.

What I'd skip: any friction bar that doesn't allow you to leave it attached during backup. You will forget to disconnect it. Buy one that's rated for your tongue weight with some headroom (20 to 25 percent over your actual tongue weight is a reasonable buffer). And skip the cheapest ball mounts; a quality forged shank is worth the extra $30.

A [surge protector for your electrical connections](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+surge+protector) isn't sway control, but while you're investing in your tow setup, it's a smart addition. Similarly, a [quality ball mount and hitch receiver](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+weight+distribution+hitch) from a verified brand will last you years of full-time use.
