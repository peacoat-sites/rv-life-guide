Your roof is the one part of your RV you almost never see, which is exactly why it destroys more rigs than engine problems, bad tires, and slide-room failures combined. Water intrusion is slow, invisible, and merciless. By the time you notice a soft spot in your floor or a brown stain on your ceiling, the rot has usually been working for months.

Most roof maintenance articles written for full-timers aren't actually written for full-timers. They're written for weekenders who need to remember to "check the roof twice a year." That schedule is almost a joke if you're putting 20,000 miles on your rig annually and parking in places that range from a dusty Arizona desert at 110 degrees to a coastal campsite where salt air and UV work on your seals simultaneously.

Here's what actually matters, how often to do it, and what I've learned from doing this wrong before I figured out how to do it right.

---

## Know What Roof You Have Before You Touch It

This sounds obvious. It isn't. I've watched people at rallies scrub an EPDM roof with a petroleum-based cleaner and wonder why it started cracking. Different roof materials require different products, different inspection logic, and different repair approaches.

The four types you'll encounter on production RVs:

**EPDM rubber** (black or gray, slightly matte): Most common on Class A and Class C motorhomes. Feels somewhat rubbery underfoot. Extremely UV-resistant but will chalk over time and develop micro-cracks around penetrations. Petroleum-based products are its biggest enemy. Don't use them.

**TPO (thermoplastic polyolefin)**: Shinier than EPDM, usually white. More puncture-resistant but seams can delaminate if they weren't bonded correctly at the factory. More common on newer coaches and some fifth wheels.

**Fiberglass**: Common on high-end coaches and most slide-out roofs. Hard, smooth, doesn't flex. Gel coat can oxidize and crack. Heavy, but nearly impervious to UV degradation if maintained.

**Aluminum**: Mostly older rigs and some trailers. Seams are riveted or lapped. Great durability but seams are where water finds its way in.

Knowing your roof type determines every product choice you make. If you're unsure, pull the original documentation or call the manufacturer with your VIN. Don't guess.

## The Inspection That Actually Catches Problems Early

Full-timers should be up on the roof every 60 to 90 days. Not a glance from the ladder. Actually get up there, walk it slowly, and get your hands on the seams.

Bring a flashlight even in daylight. Bring a pocket screwdriver or a fingernail. You're looking for:

**Sealant condition.** Every penetration on your roof (vents, A/C units, antennas, skylights, refrigerator exhaust caps, lap joints where the roof meets the front and rear caps) is sealed with some form of caulk or putty tape. This sealant has a finite lifespan, and it degrades faster under UV exposure than anywhere else on your rig. Healthy sealant is pliable. It bends without cracking when you press it. Dead sealant is hard, brittle, and may have visible gaps or crazing. If you can work a fingernail under the edge of a seam, water can too.

**Soft spots.** Walk the entire surface, including corners and edges near the walls. Any soft or spongy feel underfoot means the decking below is compromised. Find it now, when you can fix it for a few hundred dollars. Find it after a year, and you may be looking at a $4,000 to $12,000 repair. I had a small soft spot develop near my rear ladder mount that I caught in year three of full-timing. Replaced a 14-inch section of decking and resealed. Caught it early enough that it was a weekend job.

**Standing water or debris pools.** Leaves, pine needles, and gutter gunk trap moisture against your roof membrane and accelerate sealant failure. Clear everything, every inspection.

**Cracks in fiberglass caps or fascia.** Even if your main roof is rubber, your front and rear caps are usually fiberglass, and they can crack. A crack in the cap above the windshield is a direct water pathway into your front overhead cabinet, your dash wiring, and your subfloor.

One thing most checklists skip: inspect the inside too. Pull the covers off your ceiling vents and look for any staining. Check the corners of your overhead cabinets. Water almost never comes straight through the roof membrane. It enters at a seam and travels, sometimes several feet, before it shows up on your ceiling. A stain above your dinette might be coming from a failed seal around your A/C unit six feet away.

## Cleaning: What to Use, What to Avoid

EPDM and TPO roofs should be cleaned two to four times a year depending on where you're parked. Tree sap, bird droppings, and industrial fallout all accelerate membrane degradation.

For EPDM: Dicor makes a rubber roof cleaner that's widely available, around $14 to $18 a quart, and it works well without attacking the membrane. Korkay is another brand that's been around forever and holds up in the field. Avoid anything containing petroleum distillates, harsh solvents, or abrasive compounds. Simple Green in heavily diluted form (about 1:10 with water) is acceptable for light cleaning between proper washes.

For TPO: Camco makes a TPO-specific roof cleaner that costs roughly $12 to $16. You can also use mild dish soap and water. Same rule applies: no petroleum products.

Use a long-handled soft brush, not a pressure washer. A pressure washer can lift sealant edges and force water under membrane seams. I've seen the aftermath of this, and it looked like someone had half-peeled a giant sticker off the roof.

After cleaning EPDM, apply a UV protectant. Dicor's Rubber Roof Treatment works. Some people use 303 Aerospace Protectant, which costs about $15 to $20 and works on both rubber roofs and exterior trim. This step dramatically slows chalking and micro-cracking.

## Resealing: The Unglamorous Work That Saves Rigs

This is where most part-timers defer and full-timers can't afford to.

Lap sealant around penetrations typically needs to be refreshed every one to three years depending on climate and UV exposure. In the Southwest desert, plan for annual resealing on anything that's south-facing and fully exposed. In the Pacific Northwest, your bigger enemy is biological growth working under seams.

Products worth knowing:

**Dicor Self-Leveling Lap Sealant** (for horizontal surfaces): This is the industry standard for EPDM roofs. A tube runs $8 to $12. It flows into gaps before curing and forms a solid, flexible seal. Don't use it on vertical surfaces. It'll sag.

**Dicor Non-Sag Lap Sealant** (for vertical surfaces and side walls): Same chemistry, stays where you put it.

**Self-leveling silicone for TPO roofs**: Dicor also makes a TPO-compatible version. Check compatibility before buying.

The proper procedure for resealing a vent or fixture: clean the area with a rag and denatured alcohol, let it dry fully, apply new sealant generously (don't be stingy), tool it smooth with a wet finger or a plastic spreader, let it cure a minimum of 24 hours before getting rain on it.

Do not just apply new sealant over old, cracked sealant. I know it's tempting. I know it takes 10 minutes instead of 45. It doesn't work. The old sealant beneath continues to crack, the bond between old and new fails within a season, and you end up re-doing the job anyway. Strip the old material with a plastic scraper or a commercial sealant remover, clean the surface, then seal fresh.

One product worth having in your toolkit: [Eternabond Tape](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=eternabond+tape&tag=contentportfo-20). It's a butyl-based repair tape that's almost absurdly effective for sealing cracks in rubber roofs, minor tears, or anywhere you need a fast repair before you can do a proper job. A 4-inch by 50-foot roll runs around $70 to $90 and lasts years. I've used it on an emergency repair in a Montana campsite at 6 PM in October and it held through a winter's worth of weather until I could get the rig into a shop.

Desert parking (Phoenix, Tucson, southern Nevada): UV is the dominant threat. Your sealant life is shorter here than anywhere. Annual resealing on all penetrations is not excessive. Cover your roof vents with vent covers like [Camco Vent Covers](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=camco+rv+vent+cover&tag=contentportfo-20) to reduce heat buildup and slow UV damage on the plastic rims.

Coastal parking: Salt air attacks aluminum and metal hardware on your roof. Every inspection, check the condition of screws and bolts holding down your vents and A/C units. A corroded screw head means a loose vent, means water intrusion. Use stainless hardware when you're replacing anything up there.

Mountain or northern climate: Freeze-thaw cycles are brutal on sealant. Water that's worked into a micro-crack expands when it freezes and makes that crack significantly larger. Before winter, do a thorough inspection and reseal anything questionable. Don't gamble on "it'll probably hold till spring."
