Most RVers don't realize their campground water hookup can hit 100 PSI or more ,  and that's more than double what your coach's plumbing is rated to handle.

I didn't know this my first year on the road. I was blissfully hooking up at full-hookup sites, thrilled to have "real" water pressure after a week boondocking, and it never crossed my mind that the campground's supply was basically a fire hose aimed at my shower valves. Then I replaced a cracked fitting behind my bathroom sink somewhere outside Taos, New Mexico, lying in a tight cabinet with a headlamp between my teeth, and I thought: there has to be a better way to not do this again.

There is. It costs about $12.

## Why the Pressure Problem Is Worse Than You Think

The RV Industry Association reports that fresh water system failures are among the most common warranty and repair claims submitted in the first three years of RV ownership. That tracks with what I've seen at campgrounds over eight years: people with burst [water heater](/rv-water-heater-troubleshoot/) bypass valves, leaking quick-connect fittings, cracked plastic manifolds. According to Watts Water Technologies, residential plumbing is typically rated to 80 PSI as a safe maximum, but RV-grade plastic fittings and push-in connectors are often only tested to 45-60 PSI. Municipal campground supplies frequently run between 60 and 120 PSI depending on the park's supply line and whether they bother to regulate at the pedestal (many do not).

What surprised me was how inconsistent campground pressure is even within the same park. I've seen the same site test at 52 PSI in the morning and 78 PSI after noon when adjacent sites cleared out. An RVIA technical bulletin on RV plumbing standards confirms that manufacturers build systems to a working pressure of 45 PSI with surge protection only to about 80 PSI. Anything above that is borrowed time.

The math on repair costs makes this embarrassingly obvious. A blown water heater check valve: $35-$80 in parts if you're handy, $150-$300 at a shop. A cracked PEX manifold: anywhere from $200 to $600 depending on accessibility. A Camco TastePURE flow-through pressure regulator: $12.99 at most Camping World locations. The calculus isn't complicated.

## Choosing the Right Regulator

Here's where people either overthink it or don't think about it enough. There are two basic types: fixed-pressure flow-through regulators and adjustable regulators with gauges. They're not interchangeable in terms of use case.

The fixed flow-through types (Camco, Valterra's basic model) set pressure to roughly 40-50 PSI and that's that. They're fine for occasional camping or for people on a strict budget, but I'll be honest: they wear out. The diaphragm inside fatigues, especially if you're full-timing and connecting to city water every few days. I've had two fail in a way where they actually restricted flow well below 40 PSI, which I didn't catch for almost two weeks because my shower pressure "felt normal." Not ideal.

Adjustable regulators with built-in gauges are worth the extra $20-$30 if you're camping more than a dozen nights a year. The Watts model (part number LF263A) is what I currently run. It's all-brass body, adjusts from roughly 25-75 PSI with a dial, and has a glycerin-filled gauge that doesn't bounce around like the cheaper dry gauges. I set it to 50 PSI, thread it onto the campground pedestal before my hose goes on, and I know exactly what's entering my coach.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Fixed flow-through (brass) | Moderate use, more durable | $18-$28 | 2-4 seasons | No |
| Adjustable, no gauge | DIY-set-and-forget users | $28-$38 | 3-5 seasons | Yes |
| Adjustable with pressure gauge | Full-timers, high use | $40-$65 | 4-6 seasons | Yes |

One thing the packaging won't tell you: when you thread an adjustable regulator onto the pedestal, turn the adjustment knob counterclockwise until it's fully loose before you open the water. Then slowly tighten clockwise while watching the gauge climb. If you crank it down first and then open the supply, you can spike the downstream pressure before the regulator kicks in. I learned that the hard way in a campground in Flagstaff.

## How to Set It Up Correctly

The install takes about 90 seconds, but the sequence matters.

Connect your pressure regulator directly to the campground spigot, before your hose. Always before the hose, not between the hose and your RV's inlet. This protects the hose too (yes, a garden hose can burst at sustained high pressure, usually at the crimp fittings). Connect your drinking water hose to the regulator output. If you're also running a [inline water filter like the Camco EVO](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=camco+evo+inline+water+filter&tag=contentportfo-20) *(the site may earn a commission on purchases)*, that goes after the regulator and before the RV inlet. Then your fresh water inlet connection last.

Real scenario: A reader named Greta emailed me earlier this year from a park in Quartzsite (peak season, notoriously high-pressure municipal supply there). She'd been getting micro-leaks at three different connection points and couldn't figure out why. She tested her campground pressure with a simple [water pressure gauge](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=water+pressure+gauge+garden+hose&tag=contentportfo-20) and got 94 PSI. Ninety-four. She added a Watts LF263A set to 50 PSI. Leaks stopped within the week as fittings re-seated at appropriate pressure. That's not magic, that's just physics.

Another scenario: My neighbor at a [state park](/california-just-banned-repeat-no-shows-from-state-park-campsites/) in Missouri had a flow-through Camco that was two seasons old. He was getting about 28 PSI at his shower. He assumed the park had low pressure. The park measured 72 PSI at his pedestal. His regulator diaphragm had worn and was restricting flow even in the "open" position. Replaced it with a new $13 unit. Pressure jumped to 48 PSI immediately.

## What About Winterizing and Storage?

A lot of people store their regulator inside the RV during the off-season and forget about it until next spring. Fine in principle, but two things to know: first, always blow the regulator dry before storing it. Trapped water freezes and can crack the body even on brass units. Second, those glycerin-filled gauges on adjustable regulators can fog or develop bubbles if stored in extreme heat (think a Texas summer in a storage unit). A fogged gauge still works mechanically but you can't read it, which defeats the point.

I keep my regulator in a small zip-lock bag inside my "hookup kit" bin, right next to my [Camco leveling blocks](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=camco+leveling+blocks&tag=contentportfo-20) and [surge protector](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+surge+protector+30+amp&tag=contentportfo-20) *(affiliate links, site may earn commission)*. Everything for hookup in one place, so it's impossible to forget.
