If you've spent three nights in a row wedged between two diesel pushers at a packed campground, paying $65 a night for the privilege of hearing someone else's generator at 10 PM, you already know why dispersed camping exists. It's free land, wide open, and nobody's telling you to quiet down by 9. But if you've never done it before, you might be wondering: where exactly do I park? Is it actually legal? What happens if something goes wrong when I'm 40 miles from the nearest town? Those questions are completely reasonable, and I want to answer all of them honestly, because dispersed camping is one of the best things about full-time RV life, and also one of the easiest ways to get into real trouble if you go in unprepared.

## What Dispersed Camping Actually Means (and Where You Can Do It)

Dispersed camping is camping outside of a designated campground on public land, most commonly on land managed by the Bureau of Land Management (BLM) or the U.S. Forest Service (USFS). No hookups. No sites. No hosts. You find a flat spot, you set up, and you're on your own.

The key thing people get wrong is assuming all public land allows it. It doesn't. National Parks, for example, almost never permit dispersed camping. State parks vary wildly by state. But BLM land, which covers roughly 245 million acres mostly in the western U.S., and National Forests, which add another 193 million acres across the country, allow dispersed camping on most of their land with minimal restrictions.

Here's what I tell people who are new to this: download the FreeRoam app or onX Offroad before you leave cell range. These apps show land ownership overlaid on satellite maps, so you can see at a glance whether you're looking at BLM, Forest Service, state, tribal, or private land. I've used both for years and they've saved me from accidentally camping where I wasn't supposed to more than once.

A few land types to know:

**BLM land** is the most permissive. Most areas allow a 14-day stay limit before you must move at least 25 miles. After that, you can find another BLM spot.

**National Forests** follow similar rules but often have specific zones that restrict camping. Some require you to be a certain distance from water sources, usually 100 to 200 feet from lakes and streams.

**State Trust Land** is tricky. In some states like Arizona, you need an annual permit (around $15 to $20) to camp on state trust land. In others, it's not accessible to the public at all. Always check before you assume.

## How to Find a Legit Spot Before You Go

You don't just drive until something looks good and pull over. Well, sometimes you do, but you're more likely to end up with a flat tire on a washed-out road or parked on private land if you wing it completely.

The research phase matters. Here's how I approach it:

**Start with the land management agency's website.** BLM field offices publish motor vehicle use maps (MVUMs) for free. These show which roads are open to which vehicle types. If your road isn't on the MVUM, you shouldn't be driving it.

**Cross-reference with satellite imagery.** Google Earth is still one of the best tools for this. You can zoom into a pull-off and see whether there's an established fire ring, how flat the ground looks, and whether the access road looks like something your rig can handle. I've turned around on plenty of roads that looked fine on a map but were clearly rutted out in the satellite view.

**Call the local field office if you're unsure.** I know this sounds old-fashioned, but BLM and Forest Service rangers are generally helpful and will tell you straight up if a road is passable for a 30-foot trailer or if the area has any current closures.

**Use the Campendium and iOverlander apps** for user-submitted reviews. Real people describe access road conditions, whether there's cell signal, and what the weather was like. These reviews age, so look at dates, but they're invaluable.

**Check recent fire and flood closures.** The western U.S. has seen significant wildfire-related closures in recent years. A spot that was open last summer might be closed today. The USFS InciWeb site tracks active incidents.

## What Your Rig Needs to Handle Dispersed Camping

This is where I get candid with people. Dispersed camping in a Class A diesel pushing 40 feet and 30,000 pounds is a different conversation than dispersed camping in a Sprinter van. Not impossible for a big rig, but you need to be honest about your clearance, your turning radius, and your tolerance for backing up on a dirt road.

For most full-timers, the practical checklist looks like this:

**Water.** You're off-grid. If your fresh tank holds 40 gallons and you're a single person with conservative habits, you might get 3 to 4 days. A couple using water normally might get 2 days. Know your tank size and your actual usage, not optimistic usage.

**Power.** Shore power is gone. If you don't have solar or a generator, you're living on your house batteries, which in many stock RVs means 1 to 2 days before things start going wrong. I run a 400-watt solar setup with a 200Ah lithium battery bank, and I can stay out indefinitely in sunny weather. A [battery monitor](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+battery+monitor&tag=rvlife-20) is not optional here. **Waste.** Your black and gray tanks will fill. Know your capacity. On a solo trip I can stretch 5 to 7 days before needing a dump station. Plan your route to include one when you break camp.

## Leave No Trace: The Rules That Keep Public Land Open

You might be wondering why some areas get closed to dispersed camping. It's almost always because people treated them badly. Human waste left on the ground, fire rings built in inappropriate places, trash everywhere. The closures hurt everyone, and they're preventable.

The seven Leave No Trace principles are the baseline. Here's what they mean in practical terms for RV campers:

| Principle | What It Actually Means for RV Campers |
|---|---|
| Plan ahead and prepare | Know regulations before you arrive, not when you're already parked |
| Travel and camp on durable surfaces | Stay on existing pull-offs and established tracks |
| Dispose of waste properly | Pack out all trash, use WAG bags if no dump is nearby |
| Leave what you find | Don't cut vegetation, don't move rocks to "improve" the site |
| Minimize campfire impacts | Use existing rings only, no fires in fire-restricted areas |
| Respect wildlife | Store food securely, don't feed anything |
| Be considerate of others | Give other campers space, especially on quieter roads |

One rule that's specific to motorized camping: don't drive off the existing tracks to "make your own spot." Even desert soil has a biological crust that takes decades to recover from a single tire track.

## Safety and Emergency Prep When You're Truly Off-Grid

This is the section I wish someone had walked me through more seriously in my first year. Being 40 miles from a cell signal with a blown tire or a propane leak is a different situation than being 40 miles from a cell signal in a well-stocked, capable camp setup.

Here's what my off-grid safety kit includes, after 8 years of refining it:

**Communication.** I carry a Garmin inReach Mini. It costs around $350 plus a $15/month subscription, but it works anywhere on the planet via satellite and lets me send SOS messages and two-way texts. This is not a luxury for solo campers. It's insurance.

**Medical.** A Wilderness First Aid course changed how I think about injuries in remote areas. At minimum, carry a trauma kit that goes beyond band-aids: Israeli bandages, a tourniquet, QuikClot gauze, and a splint. You can find pre-assembled kits on Amazon for around $50 to $80.

**Vehicle recovery.** For van and truck camper folks, a traction board set (like MAXTRAX) and a high-lift jack are worth their weight. For larger rigs, know your limits and don't push roads that look marginal.

**Copies of your paperwork.** If you're questioned by law enforcement or a ranger about whether you're legally camped, having your MVUMs downloaded offline and your land agency maps saved is genuinely useful.

**Weather awareness.** Flash flooding is the most underestimated risk in canyon country. If you're parked in a wash and it rains 30 miles upstream, you may have no warning. Camp above the flood line and know where you're parked relative to drainages.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long can I stay in one dispersed camping spot?

Most BLM land has a 14-day stay limit within any 28-day period, after which you must move at least 25 miles. National Forests often have the same 14-day limit, though some high-use areas have shorter limits of 7 or even 3 days. Check the specific rules for each field office or forest, because they genuinely vary. Some areas in the Southwest have no limit at all, while areas near popular recreation spots can be much more restricted.

### Do I need a permit to dispersed camp on BLM land?

In most cases, no. Dispersed camping on BLM land is free and permit-free for stays under 14 days. The exceptions are certain high-demand areas where BLM has implemented permit systems, like parts of the Alabama Hills in California or the Moab area in Utah during peak season. A quick check on the local field office website will tell you if permits apply.

### Can I have a campfire at a dispersed site?

Sometimes. Campfires are subject to the same fire restrictions as everywhere else on public land. During high fire danger periods, Stage 1 and Stage 2 restrictions can ban campfires entirely, even in existing rings. Always check current fire restrictions at campfire.org or the specific land management agency's site before you plan on a fire. I've started keeping a small propane campfire insert for exactly this reason: it satisfies the campfire experience without the risk.

### What do I do about trash and gray water disposal?

Trash you pack out, full stop. There are no dumpsters at dispersed sites. Gray water is a bit more nuanced: technically you should not dump gray water on the ground, especially near water sources, as it contains food particles and soap that affect wildlife. In practice, most land management agencies look the other way on small amounts of soapy rinse water dumped far from water sources, but your black tank always needs a proper dump station. Know where the nearest one is before you set up camp.

### Is dispersed camping safe for solo women?

I've solo camped for the majority of my 8 years on the road, and my honest answer is: it depends on how prepared you are, not on your gender. The actual risks in remote dispersed areas, wildlife, weather, vehicle breakdowns, are equal-opportunity problems. The satellite communicator matters more than anything else for solo safety. I also use a dash camera that covers the exterior of my rig at night, and I'm thoughtful about sharing my exact location publicly in real time. None of this has made me fearful of dispersed camping. It's made me feel genuinely capable.

Dispersed camping is how full-time RV life starts to feel like actual freedom rather than just a different kind of paying for a parking space. The learning curve is real, the preparation takes effort, and yes, you will occasionally end up on a road you have no business being on. But the reward, a sunrise over red rock with no neighbors, no generators, and no fees, is worth every bit of it. Go slow the first few times. Bring more water than you think you need. And download those offline maps before you lose signal.
