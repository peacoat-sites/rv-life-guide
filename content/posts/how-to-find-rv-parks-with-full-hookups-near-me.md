There are roughly 16,000 RV parks and campgrounds operating across the United States right now, according to the Recreational Vehicle Industry Association's current market data. That sounds like plenty. And yet I've watched people pull a 40-foot fifth wheel into a full-service resort on a Friday afternoon, only to discover that "full hookups" on the listing meant water and electric, no sewer, and the [dump station](/rv-dump-station-guide/) was a quarter mile away with a line. That one detail ruins a weekend. It can also ruin your holding tanks if you're living in the rig full-time.

Finding parks with genuine full hookups (water, 30/50-amp electric, sewer at your site) is more complicated than it should be. Apps and websites are inconsistent about what "full" means. Reviews are written by people who spent three nights there in a pop-up. And if you're not just passing through but actively shopping for a home base or a monthly spot, the stakes are a lot higher. I've been doing this for eight years, so let me tell you what actually works.

## What "Full Hookups" Actually Means (and Doesn't)

I made this mistake early on. I thought "full hookups" was a standardized industry term. It's not. There's no federal or state definition that parks are legally required to follow. A park in the Florida panhandle told me they had "full hookups" -- what they had was 20-amp electric, water, and a sewer stub that required a 90-degree adapter I didn't own. My fault for not asking specifically, but also: the listing should have been clearer.

The version you want is 50-amp electric (if you have dual A/C or a large rig), water at the pedestal, and a [sewer connection](/rv-sewer-connection-tips/) at your site. When you call or check a listing, ask those three questions separately. "Do you have full hookups?" will get you a yes every time. "Is there a sewer connection at each site?" sometimes gets you a pause.

Also worth knowing: 30-amp versus 50-amp matters more than most newer RVers realize. If you're running two air conditioners (common in anything over 30 feet), 30-amp isn't going to cut it in a Texas July. Ask specifically about 50-amp availability and whether it's on every site or just certain rows.

## The Apps That Are Actually Worth Using

The honest answer is that no single app has all of it. I've used most of them over eight years, and the combination that works best is The Dyrt Pro, Campendium, and either RV Life or RV Trip Wizard for route planning.

**The Dyrt Pro** ($35.99/year as of July 2026) has the largest user-generated database I've found, with photos and recent reviews that often show the actual hookup setup. The filter for "full hookups" is reliable enough that I trust it as a starting point. The offline maps saved me twice in Montana.

**Campendium** is free and leans toward long-term stayers and full-timers, which means the reviews tend to mention cell signal, whether the cable TV actually works, and the exact amp situation. These are things that matter to people who live in their rigs.

**RV Life** (formerly RVillage Trip Wizard) has a decent park-finder but its real value is the routing engine, which accounts for rig height, weight, and propane restrictions on tunnels. I use it for planning, then switch to The Dyrt for evaluating individual parks.

What most people don't realize is that Google Maps is shockingly useful as a backup cross-reference. Search "[city name] RV park full hookups," pull up the park's Google listing, and look at the photo reviews. Random visitors upload pictures of the actual pedestal hookups constantly. I've spotted gravel-only pads, tight spacing, and broken sewer caps this way before I ever called.

## How to Actually Find Monthly Rates (Because Apps Lie Here)

This is where I see people leave money on the table constantly. Monthly rates at RV parks are almost never posted online. They exist, they're often dramatically cheaper than 30 x the nightly rate, and the only way to get them is to call. In my experience, a park charging $58/night will often do $850-$1,100/month for a full-hookup site with electric included. That math matters.

Here's my actual process: I filter The Dyrt for full hookups within 30 miles of wherever I'm headed. I pull a list of 8-10 parks. Then I call each one and ask three things in this order: Do you have full 50-amp hookups with sewer at the site? What's your monthly rate? Is there availability in the next two weeks? Most parks will negotiate slightly on monthly rates if you're paying upfront or staying multiple months.

Real example from doing this in Tucson, Arizona last winter: I found 11 parks within 20 miles with full hookups listed. Called all of them. Three had no monthly availability. Two had monthly rates but 30-amp only. One had monthly rates at $1,450 (too high for what it was). I ended up at a park in Marana for $897/month, 50-amp, water included, sewer at site, decent Wi-Fi. Never would have found that rate online.

**Scenario-to-outcome examples:**

Passing through Oklahoma City for 3 nights → Used The Dyrt's full-hookup filter + "open now" toggle → Found a KOA with 50-amp availability for $59/night, confirmed sewer by calling. No surprises on arrival.

Needed a 2-month base near Asheville, NC → Called 9 parks from Campendium list → Got monthly full-hookup rate of $1,124 at a park that listed $67/night online (versus what would have been $4,020 at the nightly rate). Saved $2,896 by asking.

Searched for last-minute full hookups in Colorado Springs on a Saturday → Apps showed no availability → Posted in a local Facebook RV group → Got a tip about a small park with 6 full-hookup sites that doesn't list online at all. Stayed 4 nights.

## The Facebook Group Trick Nobody Talks About Enough

I'm serious about Facebook groups. This is maybe the single best source for current, accurate information about specific parks in a specific area, and I've never seen it mentioned in any "how to find RV parks" article.

Every major RV corridor has groups. "RV Living and Travel," "Full-Time RV Living," state-specific groups, regional groups. Post something like: "Looking for full hookup site with 50-amp in [metro area], monthly or weekly rate, rig is 36 feet. Any recent experience?" You will get responses within an hour from people who were just there. They'll tell you about the park that has a hookup fee buried in the listing, the one with shoddy electric that trips breakers, the one with a friendly host who'll negotiate.

What most people don't realize is that park managers and owners are also in these groups. I've had two separate park owners message me directly after seeing my search post with availability and pricing. One of them became my home base for four months.

## A Word on Boondocking vs. Full Hookups

I know this article is specifically about full hookups, so I'll keep this brief: don't let anyone guilt you into thinking you need to boondock to be a "real" full-timer. Some people love it. Some rigs are built for it. But if you have a medical device that needs reliable power, a pet that needs A/C, or a work setup that requires stable internet, full hookups aren't a luxury. They're how your life functions. Make the choice that fits your actual situation, not the aesthetic someone else is selling.
