You pull into a campground outside Cedar Rapids on a Wednesday afternoon in June, no holiday weekend in sight, expecting the usual mid-week availability. The host tells you they're full. Not just tonight. Full for the foreseeable future, with a waiting list. You ask who's taking up all the sites, and the answer surprises you: construction workers. Not campers. Not RVers. People building a data center who need somewhere cheap to sleep for the next two years.

This is happening right now across Eastern Iowa, and if you're planning a summer route through the Midwest, you need to know about it before you're the one sitting in a parking lot at 9 PM with nowhere to go.

## What's Actually Going On in Iowa

The AI infrastructure buildout is enormous, and it needs bodies on the ground to happen. QTS and other data center developers have projects running near Cedar Rapids, Palo, Amana, and Fairfax, Iowa, and those projects pull in thousands of construction workers who need housing for months, sometimes years. Local hotels run $100 to $500 per night, which is unsustainable on a construction wage. A campground site at a place like Pleasant Creek in Palo runs about $26 a night. You do the math. Workers are doing exactly what a lot of full-timers did when they first hit the road: they're cutting costs by living in an RV.

Stephanie Chrisman manages Unhitched Sleepy Hollow and Lazy Acres RV Parks, and she told KCRG her parks went from 20 to 30% winter capacity, which is normal, to nearly full year-round, with a waiting list of roughly 20 workers sitting on it right now. The Amana RV Park and Event Center, a massive 450-plus-site property on 80 acres near Iowa City, reported its highest volume of long-term camping ever in 2026, and they directly credit data center construction for it. These aren't small parks being accidentally overwhelmed. These are big, established operations that were simply not designed to handle this kind of sustained industrial demand.

I've seen demand spikes hit regional campground clusters before, usually around big events or temporary construction projects. What's different here is the timeline. These workers aren't leaving after a few weeks. Some of them plan to stay for years, locked into long-term monthly rates that campground owners have every financial reason to prefer over transient nightly traffic.

Port Washington, Wisconsin is already seeing the same pressure. A Vantage Data Centers site there is expected to employ around 700 workers initially, growing to thousands as construction ramps up. By March 2026, the situation had gotten serious enough that the Town Plan Commission was actively considering new RV parking ordinances after residents started raising concerns, according to a report from the Ozaukee Press. That's a community scrambling to write rules for something that caught them completely off guard.

Anywhere you see a major data center project going up, which right now means chunks of the Midwest, the Southeast, and parts of the Mountain West, you should expect this pattern to follow. The workers come first. The housing crunch comes second. Regular campers find out the hard way.

## The Supply Side Makes It Worse

Here's what most people don't realize: the campground industry isn't exactly in expansion mode right now. RV Industry Association data shows April 2026 RV shipments fell 17.4% year-over-year. Shipment numbers are a rough proxy for the health of the broader industry, including park investment and new site development. When manufacturers are shipping fewer units and park operators are watching those numbers, nobody's breaking ground on new campgrounds. Meanwhile, long-term industrial housing demand just swallowed available capacity in entire regional corridors.

The parks that do exist have little incentive to turn away workers paying reliably for months at a time. I don't blame campground operators for taking that business. It's smart financially. But it fundamentally changes what these parks are, at least temporarily, and it leaves traveling RVers with fewer options during what should be peak summer season.

## What This Means for Your Route Planning Right Now

If your summer or fall travel runs through Eastern Iowa, you need to treat that region the way you'd treat a major rally weekend: book early, have a backup, and don't assume mid-week availability means anything this year. The Cedar Rapids corridor, anywhere near the Palo or Amana areas, should be on your radar as high-risk for walk-up availability for the foreseeable future.

More broadly, this is a good moment to revisit how you're finding sites. Relying on apps that show real-time availability is more important than it's ever been, because static directories won't reflect a park that quietly flipped to 95% long-term occupancy three months ago. Calling ahead, even to big parks, isn't old-fashioned. It's just what you do in 2026.

I'd also start paying attention to data center project announcements in regions you travel regularly. Utility-scale construction doesn't happen quietly. A quick search for data center projects near your planned route can give you six to twelve months of advance warning before a campground crunch materializes. The workers show up fast, and the available sites disappear faster.

## The Harder Conversation for the RV Community

There's something uncomfortable sitting underneath all of this. Campgrounds in the US have increasingly become de facto workforce housing over the past several years, and the AI buildout is just the latest, most concentrated version of that pressure. Full-timers already compete with snowbirds, event attendees, and seasonal workers. Now we're competing with industrial-scale construction crews who have an employer's economic interest backing their ability to pay and stay.

That's not going away. The data center buildout is a multi-decade infrastructure project, and it's going to keep chasing cheaper land and available power across rural America. Rural areas happen to be exactly where many of the campgrounds we depend on are located. As Woodall's Campground Magazine noted in their June 2026 coverage, this trend is filling parks year-round in ways operators have never seen before.

None of this means the RV life is broken. It means the planning layer just got more complicated, and the people who adapt their research habits now will be the ones who still find great sites while everyone else is stuck at a truck stop. Stay flexible, book earlier than feels necessary, and keep an eye on where the construction crews are heading. They'll tell you exactly where the next capacity crunch is coming from.
