I've downloaded at least forty apps chasing free camps over eight years. Most are garbage. A few changed how I travel entirely.

If you're reading this, you're probably somewhere in one of two places: either you just started living in your rig and someone in a Facebook group told you "just use Campendium" without any further explanation, or you've been at this long enough to know that the big campground reservation sites are mostly paid sites and you're trying to figure out what else is out there. Either way, you're looking for a system, not just a list of app names. Here's what I tell people when they ask me this question in person.

---

## The App That Actually Changed My Routing

Freecampsites.net exists as a website, but the app version is where I spend most of my time. It's not beautiful. The UI looks like it hasn't been updated since 2014 and honestly that might be true. But the data is community-sourced and legitimately extensive, covering dispersed camping on BLM land, National Forests, and a lot of the oddball stuff that doesn't show up anywhere else, like a pull-out on a county road in New Mexico that fits a 34-foot fifth wheel with no issue.

The way I use it: before I leave a spot, I punch in my next general destination and spend fifteen minutes reading reviews filtered by recent. The date of the last review matters enormously. A spot reviewed in 2019 might now have a gate, a fee, or a fire closure on it. I've shown up to three spots like that. Now I sort by recency and I won't trust anything older than about eight months without cross-referencing.

One thing most people don't realize is that Freecampsites.net also lists Harvest Hosts and Boondockers Welcome spots. You can see them on the map but need a membership to access details. That's not a flaw. It just means the map gives you a sense of density and options before you commit to a membership you may or may not want.

---

## iOverlander and The Crowd It Attracts

You might be wondering why iOverlander keeps coming up in van life communities specifically. The answer is that its user base skews heavily toward overlanders and international travelers, which means the data quality in the American West and the international border regions is really strong. It's genuinely excellent for the Southwest, Baja, and anywhere you're in a 4WD van or a small rig.

I wouldn't use it as my primary app if I'm in a 36-foot Class A, and that's a real limitation to acknowledge. The filters aren't set up well for length restrictions. But for smaller rigs, the reviews often include genuinely useful notes like "rutted road past the turnoff, high clearance needed" or "lots of ATV traffic on weekends, noisy." That's the kind of thing that saves you a miserable night.

Free to download, free to use. No paywall.

---

## The One Worth Paying For

The Dyrt PRO runs about $35.99 a year right now, and I'll say plainly: it's worth it if you're doing this full-time or even half-time.

The free version of The Dyrt is a solid campground discovery tool. The PRO version is a different product. The offline maps are the main reason I pay. I travel in Wyoming, Montana, and northern Nevada regularly, and cell service in those areas is not a planning concept you can rely on. Having fully loaded offline maps with user-generated reviews cached before I lose signal has gotten me into free camps I never would have found otherwise.

The Dyrt also has a Trip Planning feature that lets you map a route and see camps along it, which sounds like a gimmick but actually changes how I structure my driving days. Instead of "I need to get from Moab to Grand Junction and then figure out where to sleep," I can plan the whole stretch and slot in a camp at the 200-mile mark without having to wing it at 4 PM when I'm tired.

The traps to watch out for: a lot of what shows up in searches includes paid campgrounds, and the sorting defaults don't always float free options to the top. You have to learn to filter by "free" and then cross-reference with the reviews to see if that's still accurate. Prices change, fees get added. I treat The Dyrt as a discovery tool and then verify before I commit.

## Campendium Is Underrated for One Specific Thing

Campendium gets recommended constantly, and it's a good app. The reviews are thorough, the cell signal reports are legitimately useful (they show AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile signal at the spot, reported by actual campers), and the photo quality tends to be better than competing apps. If you work remotely like I do, those signal reports are the difference between a productive work week and driving twenty miles to park outside a Starbucks.

Where Campendium falls short is density of listings, particularly in the Southeast and Midwest. The Western data is strong. The Southern data is thin in places. I've had good luck using it in tandem with Freecampsites.net rather than choosing one over the other.

Also worth knowing: you can access Campendium fully from a browser. If you hate downloading apps, it works just as well at campendium.com without taking up space on your phone.

## BLM and National Forest Land: The Map You Actually Need

No single app covers BLM and National Forest dispersed camping the way the land management agencies' own tools do. Here's what I mean by that.

The BLM has the MyBLM Lands tool, and the USFS has the Motor Vehicle Use Maps (MVUM) available through the USDA Forest Service website, also accessible through an app called Avenza Maps. Neither of these is intuitive. Both require some time to learn. But they're authoritative. If you pull up an MVUM for a national forest and a road shows as open to motor vehicles, you can legally camp 150 feet off that road without a permit in most forests. That's dispersed camping, and no commercial app tracks it comprehensively.

The workflow I use when I'm going deep into National Forest land: download the MVUM for that forest onto Avenza before I leave cell range, then cross-reference with iOverlander or Freecampsites.net for specific spots other campers have used. The combination of official road data and community experience gets you something no single app can give you.

Avenza is free to download with a limited number of free maps. The maps themselves are free from the USDA. Slightly clunky process but worth it.

## A Word About Apps That Are More Marketing Than Tool

I'm going to be blunt: Harvest Hosts and Boondockers Welcome are membership programs, not free camping apps, and they sometimes get lumped into "free camping app" lists in a way that's misleading. Harvest Hosts costs $99 a year and gets you access to wineries, breweries, farms, and attractions that let you overnight for free. It's a genuinely good program. I've had beautiful nights at a lavender farm in Oregon and a working vineyard outside Walla Walla, Washington. But it's not free, and it's not dispersed camping.

Similarly, Boondockers Welcome ($30 a year) connects you with private homeowners who have room for an RV. Also worthwhile. Also not free.

Know what you're signing up for before you hand over a credit card.

---

## The Underused Government Resources

Recreation.gov covers National Park campgrounds, most of which have fees. But the app also lists some first-come, first-served sites at smaller areas that people don't know about. Not free, but often $5 to $10 a night, which starts to matter when you're budgeting $600 to $800 a month for all campsite costs the way I was my first two years out.

For genuinely free federal land camping, don't sleep on the BLM's own website before you start an app search. Go to blm.gov, find the state office for wherever you're headed, and look for their "camping" or "recreation" page. I found a stunning pullout spot on BLM land outside Escalante, Utah this way, no app involved, just a PDF map from the Escalante field office site. It had been free for years, had a rock fire ring, and I had a canyon view that I still think about.

---

The honest truth is that no single app is going to do everything, and experienced full-timers typically end up using two or three in combination. Start with Freecampsites.net because it's free and comprehensive, add The Dyrt PRO once you're sure this lifestyle is sticking, and download Avenza Maps with the MVUM for any national forest you're planning to spend time in. That trio will cover about 90% of what you need.

The rest you learn by showing up, reading the road, and occasionally pulling into a spot you're not sure about and finding out it's perfect.
