If you've been watching the used RV market and waiting for prices to drop, November 2026 is not bringing the news you wanted. National Powersport Auctions released their Pre-Owned RV Report on November 4, 2026, and the headline number is stark: average wholesale RV prices are running 23% above where they were in November 2025. That's not a rounding error. That's the kind of jump that changes what you can afford and shifts the whole calculus of buying now versus waiting until spring.

The 23% figure deserves some unpacking, though, because it's not a simple story of surging demand. NPA's data makes clear that this year-over-year gain came on thinner auction volume and a higher-value mix of units moving through the lanes. In plain terms: fewer rigs sold, and the ones that did sell skewed toward more expensive units. The number reflects a shift in what was available, not a broad stampede of buyers pushing prices up. That's an important distinction. It means the market isn't necessarily hot everywhere. It means a 2019 Class C with 60,000 miles isn't automatically worth 23% more than it was last November. What it does mean is that quality inventory is tight, and the premium stuff is commanding serious money.

Meanwhile, the RV Industry Association has reported that wholesale shipments of new RVs are down this year. Dealers are receiving fewer new units. So buyers who might have walked onto a lot and found a wall of shiny new Class A's are instead finding gaps, longer wait times, and in some states, an entirely different set of legal complications.

## What the 6% Pullback Actually Means for Buyers

Here's the nuance buried in the same NPA report: while prices are up 23% year-over-year, they also eased 6% from the prior three-month average. That pullback matters. It means the peak of this particular pricing run was late summer and early fall. November is cheaper than September was. That seasonal dip is normal, but this year it's happening against a backdrop of compressed supply, which limits how far it can drop.

If you're a buyer, the 6% softening is real but modest. Don't expect to find fire-sale pricing. Expect to find slightly less competition from other buyers and dealers who are marginally more motivated to move inventory before the holidays. That's not nothing, but it's not the correction some people were hoping for either.

## The Margin Window: Why November Still Makes Sense to Buy

NPA's November report makes a point worth taking seriously. The gap between a November acquisition cost and a March retail price represents the clearest margin opportunity of the year. That framing is aimed at dealers buying at auction, but the same logic applies to any private buyer who understands seasonality. Sellers in November are not thinking about spring camping season yet. Dealers are trying to manage lot costs through winter. Private sellers with a rig sitting in a driveway are more likely to negotiate in November than they will be in April when the phones start ringing.

If you're shopping for a used motorhome or towable [right now](/why-right-now-is-a-rare-buyers-window-in-the-rv-market/), the window between now and February is probably the best pricing environment you'll see until at least fall 2027. That's not speculation. That's what the data consistently shows, and this year the dynamics are amplified by the supply constraints.

## The Regulatory Picture Is About to Get More Complicated

The supply issue isn't just about production slowdowns. It's also about who can legally sell certain rigs to certain buyers. Vermont joined the Advanced Clean Trucks states in 2026, which means new motorhomes over 8,500 lbs GVWR can no longer be sold new in that state. Vermont joined California, Oregon, Washington, Massachusetts, New York, and New Jersey, all of which have adopted the same ACT sales restriction.

That list is about to get longer. Colorado, Maryland, New Mexico, and Rhode Island are slated to adopt the same ACT sales restrictions in 2027. When those states come online, the affected market covers a substantial share of total U.S. RV sales. That's not a fringe regulatory issue. That is a structural supply constraint on new Class A and Class C [diesel motorhomes](/the-epa-just-killed-the-def-sensor-requirement-on-diesel-motorhomes/) that will keep pushing buyers toward the used market, further pressuring used prices upward.

## Should You Buy Now or Wait for Spring?

This is the question most buyers are wrestling with, and the honest answer depends on what you're buying. For diesel motorhomes over 8,500 lbs, the regulatory pressure alone makes a compelling case for buying before the 2027 state restrictions kick in. Supply of new units is already thinning. Add four more states to the restricted list and that supply gets worse.

For used towables and lighter gas rigs, the calculus is less urgent. Those units aren't affected by ACT rules. Supply is tighter than it was two years ago, but it's not critical. In that segment, the November-to-February seasonal window is a reasonable time to buy, but waiting until spring won't necessarily cost you a lot if you're patient and flexible on specific features.

What waiting until spring almost certainly will cost you: competition. By March, buyers who spent the winter researching are actively shopping. Sellers know it. Prices reflect it. The 6% November softening you're seeing right now evaporates by April.

The market right now is confusing precisely because it has two things happening simultaneously. Wholesale prices are historically high year-over-year, and they're also softening from a recent peak. Both things are true. The right read is that the market has peaked for this cycle but hasn't corrected. For buyers who've been waiting for a crash, that crash isn't showing up in the NPA data. For buyers who are ready to move, November and December offer a real, if modest, pricing advantage over what spring will bring.
