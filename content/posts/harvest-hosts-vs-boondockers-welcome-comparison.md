Most full-[time RVers](/best-remote-jobs-for-full-time-rvers/) join one of these two programs expecting to cut their overnight costs dramatically, then discover the reality is more complicated than the sales page suggests. I know because I burned through almost a full season figuring out which one actually worked for my travel style, and the answer wasn't what I thought it would be.

Here's the number that genuinely surprised me when I dug into this: [Harvest Hosts](/harvest-hosts-membership/) currently lists over 5,000 member locations across the U.S. and Canada, while Boondockers Welcome sits closer to 2,100 active hosts. That's a 2:1 advantage in raw site count, but count alone tells you almost nothing about which membership will actually save you money or make your trips better. A lot depends on what kind of rig you're in, how social you want your stays to be, and whether you're more comfortable at a winery than in a stranger's driveway.

I'll be honest: I was skeptical of both when I first heard about them back when I was still full-timing in my 2017 Thor Chateau. Free overnight parking sounded like the kind of thing that's "technically free" until you factor in all the wine you feel obligated to buy. But I've now used Harvest Hosts well over 60 times and Boondockers Welcome a dozen or so, and I have actual opinions here.

## What You're Actually Paying For

Let me lay out the basic numbers before we get into the nuance.

| Feature | Harvest Hosts | Boondockers Welcome |
|---|---|---|
| Annual membership cost (2026) | $99 standard / $149 Plus | $45 |
| Number of locations | 5,000+ | ~2,100 |
| Location types | Wineries, farms, breweries, museums, golf courses | Private driveways, rural properties |
| Hookups available? | Almost never | Occasionally (host-dependent) |
| Stay limit | Typically 1 night | Host sets limit, often 1-2 nights |
| Membership includes guest passes? | No | Yes (1 guest pass per year) |
| App quality | Strong, actively updated | Functional, improving |
| Canada locations | Yes | Yes |

Current pricing as of August 2026. Both programs have raised prices modestly over the past two years, so verify at checkout.

What the table doesn't show is texture. Harvest Hosts is essentially an agreement between you and a business: park here, preferably buy something, leave tomorrow. Boondockers Welcome is an agreement between you and a person who has voluntarily opened their home to fellow RVers. That difference in relationship changes everything about how the stays feel.

## The Harvest Hosts Experience (Real Talk)

The app is genuinely good. You can filter by location type, rig size, and whether a host has space for slideouts. What surprised me was how many hidden-gem spots exist on there that you'd never stumble across otherwise, I spent a night at a family-run cheese farm outside Bozeman, Montana that I still think about. They made an aged gouda that cost me $34, I slept like a rock, and I woke up to goats. Ten out of ten.

But I've also pulled into a "winery" that turned out to be a tasting room open two days a week, closed when I arrived, with a parking area that shared a fence line with a busy highway. No one to talk to, nothing to buy, road noise all night. The quality variance is real.

The unwritten spend expectation is something I want to be clear about. Harvest Hosts doesn't require you to buy anything, but the whole program's social contract is built on you being a paying customer, not just a freeloader with a rig. Most hosts I've talked to say they're genuinely happy to have visitors even if they don't buy much, but spending $20 or so is the right thing to do. If you're at a winery and you're trying to travel on $50 a day total, you can feel that tension. A couple I know, Pat and Renee (full-timers out of a 38-foot [fifth wheel](/how-to-back-up-a-fifth-wheel-for-beginners/)), told me they've passed on Harvest Hosts locations specifically because they couldn't afford to buy anything and it felt wrong to stay for free.

One practical thing only regulars figure out: call ahead. Not every Harvest Hosts location checks the app daily. I've driven 20 minutes to a spot to find it full or temporarily closed. A quick call the day before fixes this almost every time.

## Boondockers Welcome: Smaller Network, Different Energy

Forty-five dollars a year. That's currently less than a single night at most RV parks in the Pacific Northwest. And what you get is access to actual humans who have volunteered to host you.

The stays feel completely different. I've had hosts who left out a welcome note with the WiFi password, local restaurant recommendations, and a bag of homegrown tomatoes. I've also had stays where the host checked in briefly and we waved and that was it, totally fine, zero pressure. Most hosts are former or current RVers themselves, which means they actually understand what you need. They know you don't want to ask where the water spigot is at 10 p.m.

The hookup question is worth dwelling on. A meaningful subset of Boondockers Welcome hosts offer at least a water connection, and some offer 30-amp electric. This is legitimately rare in the free-camping world and it's something Harvest Hosts almost never has. If you're not fully self-contained, maybe you've got a shorter tank capacity or you rely on shore power for a CPAP, that changes the math considerably.

What I don't have good numbers on is host churn. Anecdotally, I've tried to book spots that were listed as available but hadn't had an active host in months. The platform is working on this, but it's worth sending a message before you route your trip around a Boondockers Welcome stop.

**Worked examples:**

Scenario: Weekend trip from Denver to Moab, two people, Class C, on a tight budget. Wanted two free nights.
Used Harvest Hosts at a craft brewery outside Grand Junction + Boondockers Welcome host east of Green River.
Result: Two nights free, spent $28 at the brewery, saved approximately $110 vs. two nights at commercial campgrounds. Tomatoes were included at the BW stop.

Scenario: Full-timer trying to average under $600/month in site fees (ambitious but doable).
Combined Harvest Hosts Plus with Boondockers Welcome, targeting 15-18 free nights per month.
Result: Averaged $514/month in site fees over 6 months, per their own tracking spreadsheet they shared in a Facebook group.

Scenario: 42-foot diesel pusher, slideouts fully extended.
Found that Harvest Hosts had better filtering for large-rig-friendly spots, but still called ahead every time after one bad experience trying to fit into a spot listed as "large rig OK" that absolutely was not.
Result: Success rate improved to about 90% once calling ahead became standard practice.

To read this plainly: if you use Boondockers Welcome just once or twice a year at a campground that would've cost $40 a night, the membership pays for itself. Harvest Hosts takes a little more use to justify, but it's still absurdly easy to break even.

## Which One Should You Actually Get?

I'll just say it: if you had to pick one and you're in a self-contained rig with decent tanks, start with Harvest Hosts. The network is larger, the app is better, and the location variety keeps things interesting over a full season. You'll find places you genuinely love visiting, not just sleeping at.

If you're in a smaller rig, not fully self-contained, or you're someone who finds random conversations with fellow RVers energizing rather than exhausting, Boondockers Welcome might actually win on a per-stay-quality basis even with the smaller network.

Honestly, at $99 + $45 = $144 combined for both programs, and given that a single prevented campground stay covers almost the entire cost of Boondockers Welcome, I'd just get both. I've had them both running for years and I treat them like different tools. Harvest Hosts when I want somewhere interesting to park near a town. Boondockers Welcome when I want a quiet driveway and maybe a conversation.
