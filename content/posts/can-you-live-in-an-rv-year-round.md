Yes, you can live in an RV year-round. Hundreds of thousands of people do it [right now](/why-right-now-is-a-rare-buyers-market-for-rvs/), full-time, across every climate and region of North America. The harder question is whether *you* should, and what it'll actually cost you, break down on you, and demand from you over the long haul.

This isn't the life the Instagram reels show. It's also not the nightmare that skeptical family members predict. It's somewhere more complicated and, for the right person, genuinely good.

## The Legal and Administrative Setup Nobody Talks About Enough

Before you sell your couch, sort out your legal domicile. An RV is not legally recognized as a permanent address in most jurisdictions, and without a physical address you can't renew your driver's license, register your vehicle, or get [health insurance](/how-to-get-health-insurance-as-a-full-time-rver/) through certain providers. The three states full-timers almost always choose are South Dakota, Texas, and Florida, because all three have no state income tax, relatively straightforward domicile processes, and mail forwarding infrastructure built up around the full-timer community.

South Dakota is probably the most popular. You can establish domicile there in a single day with an in-person visit, using a [mail forwarding service](/best-mail-forwarding-service-for-full-time-rvers/)'s address (companies like America's Mailbox in Box Elder specifically serve this need). You'll register your vehicles there, get your SD driver's license, and use that address on everything. Texas works similarly. Florida has more complexity around proving residency but works for people who spend chunks of the year there anyway.

Don't skip this step and assume you'll figure it out later. Your vehicle registration, mail, banking, and medical coverage all hinge on having a real, legal address. Sort it out before you leave, not three months in when your license is expiring.

## What It Actually Costs

The number you'll see thrown around is "$1,500–$3,000 a month." That range is real, but it hides enormous variation. Someone boondocking five nights a week on BLM land in Arizona pays almost nothing for a campsite. Someone staying in full-hookup RV parks near coastal cities is paying $800–$1,800 a month just for a pad to park on.

| Cost Category | Low End (annual) | High End (annual) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Campground/site fees | $3,600 | $18,000 | Mix of free boondocking vs. nightly resort parks |
| Fuel | $2,400 | $8,000 | Depends on how much you move |
| Insurance (RV + health) | $3,000 | $9,000 | Full-timer RV policy + ACA marketplace plan |
| Food/groceries | $4,800 | $9,600 | Largely lifestyle-dependent |
| Propane | $400 | $1,200 | Climate and usage vary widely |
| Maintenance & repairs | $1,500 | $5,000 | Budget higher in years 1 and 3+ |
| Cell/data plan | $600 | $2,400 | Starlink adds ~$1,500/yr for equipment + service |
| Miscellaneous | $1,200 | $3,600 | Campground memberships, gear, storage unit |
| **Total** | **~$17,500** | **~$56,800** | Real range for most full-timers |

That low end assumes very deliberate, slow travel with heavy reliance on free camping. The high end is someone who moves a lot, stays in parks frequently, and had a rough year for repairs. Most full-timers land somewhere in the $28,000–$42,000 annual range.

## Choosing the Right Rig for Full-Time Living

This decision matters more than most new full-timers realize, and the mistake is usually buying a rig optimized for vacation use rather than daily habitation.

Weekend-warrior units are built with the assumption you'll spend most nights in your house. The mattresses are thin, the storage is insufficient, the water tank capacity is modest, and the construction tolerates occasional use better than 365 days of it. For full-timing, you want to look at rigs from manufacturers who explicitly market to full-timers, like Newmar, Tiffin, and Grand Design.

Class A motorhomes give you the most living space. Fifth wheels come close, with slide-outs that can make them roomier than many apartments. Class B campervans (the Mercedes Sprinter-based rigs from Winnebago, Airstream, Thor, and others) sacrifice space for real-world maneuverability and fuel efficiency, and many couples live in them year-round without complaint. Class C is a middle ground that works well for families.

If you're considering a diesel pusher over a gas Class A, know that diesel fuel costs more per gallon but the Cummins and CAT engines in those rigs are built for far higher mileage, and many owners report dramatically lower long-term drivetrain costs. The Cummins ISL and ISB series engines, for example, are routinely documented in RV owner forums reaching 300,000+ miles with proper maintenance. The tradeoff is higher upfront cost and more expensive shop labor when something does go wrong.

One thing to check before you buy: the manufacturer's own documentation on rated holding tank capacity and freshwater tank size. For full-timing without hookups, you want at minimum a 50-gallon freshwater tank and a 40-gallon gray tank. Many coaches spec those sizes or larger; check the actual spec sheet, not the floor plan description.

## The Hardest Parts of Year-Round Life

**Winter.** RV insulation is not house insulation. Most rigs have an R-value somewhere between R-7 and R-11 in the walls, and many cheaper models have far less. When temperatures drop below about 20°F, exposed underbelly water lines freeze, and that means burst lines, damaged tanks, and expensive shop time. The fix is a combination of tank heater pads (most four-season rigs include them from the factory), skirting to trap heat under the rig, and keeping interior heat running. If you buy a rig marketed as "four-season" or "Arctic package," read the fine print: some packages are more robust than others. Forest River and Keystone both offer specific cold-weather packages on certain models; verify the actual specification, not just the label.

Boiling summer heat is the other side. Residential-style rooftop A/C units in modern Class A and fifth-wheel rigs can handle extreme heat, but they draw significant amperage (13–16 amps each at startup, per standard unit specs). On 30-amp shore power you can run one unit comfortably, two is tight. On 50-amp service you have more headroom. If you're boondocking in summer, you'll need a serious solar and battery setup to run cooling. Sizing that correctly takes some calculation; the [RV Solar Calculator](/rv-solar-calculator/) can help you figure out how many panels and amp-hours you actually need.

**Repairs.** Full-timers almost universally report more frequent repairs than expected. Slide-out motors fail. Roof seams separate. Water heater igniters go. Leveling jacks stick. This isn't a complaint about quality, exactly; it's what happens when systems designed for 30 days of annual use run 365. Budget at minimum $1,500–$2,000 per year for maintenance, and understand that in some years you'll spend $5,000. Keep a repair fund, not just a travel fund.

**Campground realities.** The good full-hookup sites at popular parks fill up weeks or months ahead during peak season. You'll learn quickly that "first-come, first-served" in Sedona in March is a fantasy. Membership programs like Thousand Trails, Passport America, and Harvest Hosts each have their own network gaps and rules; none of them covers every situation. Boondocking on BLM or Forest Service land is genuinely free and often beautiful, but it requires self-sufficiency: water carrying, gray water management, generator or solar for power, and periodic "sanity runs" to civilization for resupply.

## Health Insurance: The Real Barrier

For people under 65 without employer coverage, health insurance is frequently cited as one of the biggest obstacles to full-timing. ACA marketplace plans are available to full-timers, but many plans restrict coverage to in-network providers in a specific geographic area. If you're moving state to state, you can end up with a plan that only covers emergency care outside your plan's region.

The practical workaround most full-timers use is choosing a domicile state with broad network plans (South Dakota and Texas both have options that work better for travelers) and pairing that with a medical cost-sharing plan or a telemedicine subscription for routine care. This is an area where the specifics change frequently; check Healthcare.gov directly and filter for multi-state or nationwide plans.
