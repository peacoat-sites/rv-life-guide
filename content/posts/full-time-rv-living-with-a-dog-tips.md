My dog Biscuit has logged more miles than most people's cars. Somewhere north of 60,000 miles across 34 states, and she's done it all from a custom platform bed wedged into the passenger-side corner of my 32-foot fifth wheel. I'll be honest: when I started full-timing with her in 2018, I thought the hardest part would be finding pet-friendly campgrounds. I was completely wrong about that.

The hardest part is everything you don't see coming. The vet situation. The anxiety spikes when you're parked in a new spot every few nights. The campground neighbors who give you the look when your dog barks at 6am. The summer heat that turns your rig into an oven the second you leave to run errands. None of the "RV life with pets" content I found at the time was honest about any of it, so here's my attempt to fix that.

---

## Finding Campgrounds That Actually Work For Dogs

"Pet-friendly" is one of the most useless phrases in camping. Technically it means a campground allows dogs. What it doesn't tell you is whether there's a patch of grass bigger than a yoga mat, whether the leash rule is strictly enforced on 6-foot leads only, whether the dog park (if there even is one) is a fenced mud pit, or whether a dozen yappy dogs are parked within earshot of your site.

What I've learned to actually look for: KOAs are reliably dog-tolerant but inconsistent on space. State parks are almost always better on natural terrain and breathing room, though hookups vary. Thousand Trails properties I've used have been hit or miss -- some have fantastic dog walks, some have gravel pads wall to wall. Corps of Engineers campgrounds are genuinely underrated for dogs. Lots of space, natural ground, usually near water, and some of the best rates going.

What I do now before booking anywhere: I search the campground on Campendium and filter by reviews that mention pets. Real campers write things like "tiny sites packed together" or "gorgeous nature trails, dogs loved it" and that tells me more than the campground website ever will.

One thing worth knowing about Harvest Hosts: their membership includes breweries, wineries, farms, and golf courses, and a surprising number of them are fantastic for dogs. I've parked on working farms where Biscuit had an actual field to run in. Worth it if you're not traveling with hookup dependency.

Breed restrictions are real and worth researching in advance. Many private campgrounds and virtually every resort-style RV park has a breed ban list (usually the standard Pit Bull, Rottweiler, Doberman list). I've never had Biscuit flagged, but I know full-timers with mixed breeds who've been turned away at the office. Have documentation of your dog's breed if yours is ambiguous.

## Managing Vet Care On The Road

This is the one that keeps me up at night more than any mechanical issue.

I'll be honest: there is no perfect system here. What I've built over eight years is a patchwork that mostly works. First, I have a "home base" vet in Denver that I try to see twice a year when I loop through Colorado. She knows Biscuit's history, keeps her records updated, and will call in prescriptions to pharmacies in whatever state I'm in. That relationship took years to build and I'd argue it's worth structuring your travel around it.

For everything else, I use VetFinder and Banfield's location search. Banfield is inside Petsmart locations, which means they're everywhere, and their Optimum Wellness Plan (currently around $35-55/month depending on tier) covers annual exams and core vaccinations at any location. I've used it in seven different states. The care quality varies, I'll be honest, but for routine stuff it's been reliable.

What surprised me was how useful telehealth has gotten for non-emergency issues. I use Dutch and have used Pawp in a pinch. I can get a prescription for Biscuit's anxiety medication (more on that in a second) without having to find a vet in an unfamiliar city at 8pm on a Saturday.

Keep a printed and digital copy of your dog's vaccine records, especially rabies. Some states and campgrounds ask for them. Some boarding facilities won't take your dog without proof. I keep a folder in Google Drive and a laminated card copy in my glovebox.

## Heat, Safety, and the Very Real Problem of Leaving Your Dog in the Rig

Summer 2022, parked outside Moab in mid-July, I made the mistake of running to town for 45 minutes with Biscuit in the rig and my generator running the AC. Generator quit while I was in line at City Market. I got back to a 95-degree interior and a very distressed dog who was fine, ultimately, but it scared the hell out of me.

Never again.

My current setup: I have a dual-zone mini-split (installed by a mobile RV tech, not me) that runs off shore power, and when I'm on hookups I trust it more than a generator. But when I can't be on hookups, Biscuit comes with me or I don't go. Full stop. There are [temperature monitoring devices](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+temperature+monitor+alert&tag=rvlife-20) that send your phone an alert if the rig temperature spikes -- I use a Temp Stick, which is about $40 and connects to wifi. It's not a replacement for judgment, but it's a layer of protection.

Hot pavement is also something I underestimated for years. Asphalt in a desert campground in July can hit 150 degrees. Biscuit has a set of Ruffwear Grip Trex boots (around $65 for a set) that she tolerates better than I expected. I test the ground with the back of my hand for seven seconds -- if I can't hold it, she gets boots.

## Small Space Living and Your Dog's Sanity (And Yours)

The honest truth is that dogs adapt to small spaces better than people do. What they don't adapt to well is boredom and erratic routine.

I keep Biscuit's feeding times, walk times, and sleep spot completely consistent no matter where we are. The rig moves; her schedule doesn't. That one thing, more than any product or trick, has kept her calm across eight years and dozens of new environments.

That said, I've made real investments in her physical setup inside the rig. Her platform bed is built from a 2-inch memory foam cut from a Sweetnight queen foam I sliced down to size, covered in waterproof fabric. She has a non-slip mat in front of it. Her food and water bowls are in a drawer-style pull-out cabinet that I built (poorly, then rebuilt less poorly) so they don't slide around while driving.

A [quality dog crate](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=collapsible+dog+crate+travel&tag=rvlife-20) that folds flat is worth having even if your dog isn't a crate dog full time. I use a 36" Midwest iCrate for situations where I need to kennel Biscuit safely during hitching and unhitching, when she gets anxious near traffic. It folds flat under my bed when not in use.

Mental stimulation is non-negotiable in a small space. Biscuit gets at least 45 minutes of actual movement per day -- not just yard sniffing, but trail time or a real walk. On days when the weather makes that hard, she gets a Kong stuffed with frozen peanut butter and I work a snuffle mat into her routine. Bored dogs in small spaces become anxious dogs, and anxious dogs make campground neighbors miserable.

Separation anxiety came up for Biscuit around year two, probably because we were together almost 24/7 and she panicked when I wasn't within 10 feet of her. The telehealth vet put her on a low dose of Trazodone for high-stress situations (like driving through big cities or staying somewhere loud). The research on canine anxiety medication is actually pretty solid now -- it's not sedation, it's edge-removal. Worth discussing with your vet if your dog shows signs.

## Gear Worth Having (And Stuff That's Overpriced Hype)

Real quick on this because there's a lot of garbage marketed at dog-owning RVers.

Worth every penny: a good retractable hose with a spray nozzle for washing muddy paws before they come inside (I use a [Flexi retractable leash](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=flexi+retractable+leash&tag=rvlife-20) for campground walks, though a standard 6-footer is better for hiking), a hands-free leash for hikes so I can use both arms on rocky terrain, the Temp Stick I mentioned, and a proper first aid kit designed for dogs. The Kurgo first aid kit runs about $25 and has saved me from panicking over minor trail injuries more than once.

Not worth it in my experience: the fancy "dog rooms" some people build into rigs with separate ventilation and all that. Biscuit doesn't need a room, she needs my presence. The $200+ "travel water systems" for dogs are also unnecessary -- I use a [Ruffwear Bivy Bowl](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=ruffwear+bivy+bowl&tag=rvlife-20) that collapses flat and clips to a bag. Five bucks' worth of function at $16.

One thing I've seen people skip that they shouldn't: a [filtered water solution](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rv+inline+water+filter&tag=rvlife-20) for the rig. Campground water varies wildly in quality, and I give Biscuit the same filtered water I drink. GI upset in a dog when you're on the road is miserable for everyone involved.

The thing I want people to take away isn't that full-timing with a dog is easy or hard -- it's that the challenges are specific and manageable once you know what they actually are. Heat logistics, vet continuity, anxiety management, finding sites with real space: those are the real variables. Figure those out before you leave, stay flexible after, and you'll have a travel companion that makes the whole thing better in ways that are pretty hard to explain to people who haven't done it.
