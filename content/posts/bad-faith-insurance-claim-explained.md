You filed a claim six months ago. Your RV took hail damage at a campground in Colorado, the adjuster came out once, asked for a stack of documents, then went quiet. You called. Left messages. Finally got a letter saying your claim was "under review." No timeline. No explanation. No check. Meanwhile, you're living in a rig with a compromised roof seal, watching the interior ceiling start to bubble every time it rains. If that scenario sounds familiar, you may not just have a slow insurance company. You may have a bad faith insurance claim situation on your hands, and that changes everything about how you should respond.

## What Bad Faith Actually Means (And What It Doesn't)

Bad faith isn't just an insurer being annoying or slow. It's a legal concept with real teeth, and understanding the distinction matters because it determines whether you have a complaint or a lawsuit.

When you buy an insurance policy, both sides enter into what's called a contract of good faith and fair dealing. The insurer takes your premiums. In exchange, they're legally obligated to handle your claims honestly, promptly, and with genuine effort to honor the coverage you paid for. Bad faith happens when they deliberately or unreasonably fail to do that.

What most people don't realize is that bad faith can be either first-party or third-party. First-party bad faith is when your own insurer mistreats you directly. Third-party bad faith typically involves a liability insurer failing to properly defend or settle claims against their policyholder. For RV owners, first-party situations are far more common: your RV insurer delays your claim, lowballs your payout, or denies coverage without a solid reason.

What bad faith is NOT: a legitimate coverage dispute, a slow adjuster who's just overworked, or a denial that has a real policy exclusion behind it. Insurance companies are allowed to investigate claims, ask for documentation, and even deny claims if they have a valid reason. The line gets crossed when they investigate in bad faith, delay without justification, or deny claims they know they should pay.

## Common Bad Faith Tactics RV Owners Actually Face

I've seen a lot of claims go sideways over eight years on the road. The tactics vary, but certain patterns show up again and again.

**Unreasonable delays.** Your state likely has specific timeframes written into its insurance code. Many states require acknowledgment within 10 to 15 days and a coverage decision within 30 to 45 days of receiving all documentation. When an insurer keeps requesting documents you've already sent, or says the claim is "under review" for months without explanation, that delay itself can constitute bad faith.

**Lowball offers without explanation.** An adjuster quotes you $4,200 to repair a slide-out mechanism that three independent repair shops say costs $9,500 minimum. They don't explain their valuation methodology. They just send a check and call it settled. That's not a negotiation, that's a tactic designed to get you to accept less than you're owed before you realize it.

**Denial based on misrepresentation of the policy.** This one is subtle but common. The insurer cites a policy exclusion that doesn't actually apply to your situation, or quotes a clause out of context. Not everyone reads their entire policy, and insurers know that.

**Failure to investigate properly.** An adjuster spends 20 minutes looking at your rig and files a report. No photos of the actual damage area, no consultation with a specialist for complex mechanical issues. A lazy investigation that leads to a denial or underpayment can be bad faith if it was unreasonable given the circumstances.

**Pressuring you to accept a fast settlement.** Especially right after a loss, when you're stressed and need money to keep moving or make repairs. A fast settlement that's too low, pushed on you before you understand the full scope of damage, is a classic bad faith move.

## How to Document Everything Before You Do Anything Else

If you suspect bad faith, your single most important job right now is to build a paper trail. Don't wait for a lawyer. Don't wait for the insurer to respond. Start today.

Here's what to do, in order:

1. **Write down a complete timeline.** Every date you called, emailed, or received correspondence. Include the name of every person you spoke with and what they said. Do this from memory now if you haven't been keeping notes, and be as specific as possible about dates and statements.

2. **Get everything in writing going forward.** Stop handling this over the phone exclusively. After every call, send a follow-up email: "As per our conversation today, you indicated the claim decision would be made by [date]." Now you have a record.

3. **Gather three independent repair estimates.** If the insurer is undervaluing your damage, independent estimates from licensed RV repair facilities are your strongest counter-evidence. Keep the estimates detailed, itemized, and on shop letterhead.

4. **Pull your state's insurance code.** Seriously, Google "[your state] insurance bad faith statute" or "unfair claims settlement practices act." Most states have specific timelines insurers must follow. Print it. Know the deadlines they've already blown.

5. **Request your complete claim file in writing.** You have the right to see the adjuster's notes, photos, and internal communications related to your claim. Send a certified letter requesting the full claim file. What they send back, and what they don't, can be revealing.

6. **Save all physical evidence of damage.** Photograph and video everything, dated. If your RV has secondary damage because a repair was delayed, document that explicitly and note when it appeared relative to the claim filing date.

7. **Contact your state insurance commissioner.** Filing a complaint costs you nothing. It creates an official record, and some insurers move surprisingly fast once they know a regulator is watching. Find your state's department of insurance website and file online.

## When to Get an Attorney Involved

Most adjusters are not afraid of a persistent policyholder. They are afraid of a policyholder with a bad faith attorney.

Bad faith insurance law is a specialty. You don't want a general practice attorney who "handles insurance stuff sometimes." You want someone who does this specific work. Many of them take cases on contingency, meaning they don't get paid unless you win. That changes the math considerably.

You should seriously consider an attorney if any of these apply:

- Your claim has been denied and you believe the denial is wrong
- Your claim has been open for more than 60 days with no resolution and no legitimate explanation
- You've received a settlement offer that's less than 50% of your documented repair costs
- The insurer has misrepresented policy language to you
- You're experiencing secondary damage (like that bubbling ceiling) because the initial repair was delayed

The consultation itself is almost always free. Use it. A good bad faith attorney will tell you in 30 minutes whether you have a real case or whether you need to push harder through normal channels. Either way, you'll know where you stand.

What most people don't realize is that in many states, if you win a bad faith lawsuit, you can recover more than just your original claim amount. You may be entitled to attorney fees, emotional distress damages, and in egregious cases, punitive damages. The insurer's liability can far exceed what they were trying to avoid paying you in the first place. That's what makes bad faith claims serious legal territory for insurers.

## A Quick Comparison: Normal Claim Delays vs. Bad Faith Red Flags

| Situation | Probably Normal | Likely a Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Adjuster requests documentation | Once or twice, for reasonable items | Repeatedly requests same documents already submitted |
| Claim timeline | 30-45 days to resolution in most states | 90+ days with no coverage decision |
| Settlement offer | Lower than you hoped, but explained | No methodology provided, no line items |
| Denial reason | Cites specific policy exclusion | Vague language, misquoted policy sections |
| Communication | Responses within a week | No callbacks, no written follow-up |
| Investigation | Thorough inspection, specialist consulted if needed | 20-minute visit, no photos, no report shared |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if my insurance company is acting in bad faith versus just being slow?

The difference often comes down to whether their behavior is unreasonable given the circumstances. A claim involving a large loss and disputed liability might genuinely take time. But if they've received all your documentation, there's no legitimate coverage dispute, and they still haven't issued a decision or a payment after 45 to 60 days, that's a pattern worth scrutinizing. Check your state's specific deadlines. If they've blown past a legal requirement, that's not "just slow." That's a violation.

### Can I sue my insurance company for bad faith?

Yes. In most states, you can file a lawsuit against your own insurer for first-party bad faith. The specifics vary by state, some have explicit bad faith statutes, others rely on common law. An attorney who specializes in insurance bad faith in your state can tell you exactly what standard applies and what damages you could recover. Don't assume you can't just because the insurer acts like the decision is final.

### What if my claim was denied? Is that automatically bad faith?

No. Claim denials are not automatically bad faith. Insurers are allowed to deny claims for legitimate reasons, including policy exclusions, conditions you didn't meet, or coverage that genuinely doesn't apply. Bad faith comes in when the denial is unreasonable, unsupported by the actual policy language, or made without a real investigation. If you got a denial letter with a vague or questionable reason, have an attorney read it before you accept it as final.

### Should I accept a partial payment while the rest of my claim is still disputed?

Be careful here. Cashing a check that's labeled "full and final settlement" or "settlement of all claims" can legally close your claim. Before you accept any payment, read what you're being asked to sign. If there's any release language and the payment doesn't cover your full documented loss, ask an attorney before signing. Some states have laws that protect you from accidental waivers, but don't count on it.

### Does filing a complaint with the state insurance commissioner actually do anything?

More than most people expect. The insurer has to respond to a regulatory complaint, and that response is on record. It doesn't guarantee you'll get a check, but it creates documented pressure and sometimes triggers a real review of your claim by a supervisor who wasn't involved originally. It's also completely free to file and usually takes less than 30 minutes online. Even if it doesn't resolve your claim, it's evidence of the insurer's behavior if you later pursue legal action.

Know the signs, document everything, and don't let the complexity of insurance law convince you that you're powerless. You have more options than they want you to know about.
