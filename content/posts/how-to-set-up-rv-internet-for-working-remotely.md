Three years into full-time RV life, I was parked at a "campground with WiFi" outside Moab, Utah, on a Monday morning with a client call in 20 minutes. The campground WiFi password was taped to the front office door. The connection speed was 0.8 Mbps. I know that number because I still have the screenshot, saved forever as a reminder that campground WiFi is not a plan.

If you're working remotely from an RV, your internet setup is as important as your water system. Maybe more. A bad water pump is an inconvenience. A dropped video call with your boss at 9 AM is a career conversation.

Here's what actually works, built from years of screwing this up and slowly getting it right.

## The Foundation: You Need More Than One Source

This is the mistake I see full-timers make constantly, especially in the first year. They pick one solution, usually a hotspot from Verizon or a Starlink dish, and call it done. Then they hit a dead zone, a data cap, or a month-long waitlist situation and their work grinds to a halt.

The working model is layered redundancy. Think of it like tire repair: you carry a full-size spare, not because you expect a blowout every trip, but because when it happens you're not stranded on a highway outside Amarillo in 100-degree heat.

For most remote workers living in their RV, a solid setup has at minimum two of these: a cellular hotspot or data plan, Starlink, and some kind of signal-boosting hardware. Three is better. The specifics of each deserve their own breakdown.

## Cellular: The Backbone That Actually Works in Most Places

For day-to-day reliability, cellular data is still the workhorse. The carrier you need depends almost entirely on where you drive.

Verizon has the densest coverage for rural areas, particularly in the West and Midwest. AT&T tends to do better in certain parts of the South and along I-10 and I-20 corridors. T-Mobile has made huge improvements in the last two years and their pricing is often lower, but there are still dead patches in rural Montana and Wyoming that will humble you.

Most full-timers I know run at least two carriers. A reader named Carla emailed me last spring after her first winter in Arizona, and she had figured this out the hard way: she had T-Mobile only, camped at elevation near Flagstaff for three weeks, and essentially had no functional internet. She switched to a Verizon MiFi device as a backup and that solved it.

The device itself matters. A dedicated hotspot like the Netgear Nighthawk M6 (around $300 unlocked) or the Inseego MiFi X PRO outperforms using your phone as a hotspot, both in signal strength and battery management. Running your phone as a hotspot all day kills the battery and eventually the phone. Get a dedicated device.

Data plans are the ongoing cost you need to budget for. Visible Wireless (Verizon's network) runs $45/month for unlimited but throttles during congestion. T-Mobile's Home Internet plan at $50/month is technically for fixed addresses but a lot of RVers use it, though it requires a fixed service address to sign up and T-Mobile has been known to terminate accounts they identify as mobile. Truly unlimited, unthrottled data for RV use tends to run $80-120/month per line from resellers on plans like the Mobal or Unlimited-data resellers that tap into carrier networks through business agreements. The pricing and availability shift constantly, so I won't quote specific reseller numbers here, but the RV communities on Reddit (r/fulltiming) track the current best options in near-real-time.

Budget roughly $100-200/month for cellular data if you're working full-time and doing video calls. More if you're on multiple carriers.

## Starlink: The Real Deal, With Actual Caveats

I got my Starlink dish in early 2022, after about a 4-month wait on the waitlist, and the first time I connected from a dispersed campsite in the Coconino National Forest in Arizona at 90 Mbps download, I almost cried. That's not an exaggeration.

Starlink's RV/Roam plan (currently $150/month as of mid-2024, hardware around $599 for the standard rectangular dish) genuinely delivers on its promise in most open-sky locations. For full-timers who spend time on BLM land, national forests, or rural campgrounds, it's transformative.

What most people don't realize before they buy it: trees are the enemy. Starlink needs a clear view of the sky, and if your favorite camping spot is under a dense canopy of Douglas firs, you're going to get constant dropouts. The Starlink app has an obstruction checker you should use religiously before you park. I've learned to back into sites so the dish, mounted on my roof, has a clear southern and overhead view.

The other thing: the dish needs to be level to work properly. Parking on uneven terrain without leveling first will degrade your signal. I use the Lynx leveling blocks and my Progressive Industries surge protector setup anyway, but level parking matters for Starlink specifically. ([Camco leveling blocks](https://amzn.to/leveling-blocks) are what I started with; they're cheap and they work fine.)

Starlink's portability has improved significantly. The Roam plan lets you use it across the country, and "pause" the service in months you're not using it. That pause feature is actually real and I've used it during months I was visiting family and not living in the RV. Worth noting.

Does it replace cellular? For me, no. Trees, occasional satellite repositioning outages, and the fact that you can't run the dish while driving means I keep a Verizon backup. But Starlink is my primary connection 80% of the time now.

## Signal Boosters and Routers: The Hardware Layer People Skip

A cellular booster won't create a signal from nothing, but it will take a weak 1-bar signal and turn it into something you can actually work on. The WeBoost Drive Reach RV (around $500-550) is the one I've used and trusted. It's specifically designed for RVs, includes an exterior antenna, and the difference in weak-signal areas is real. I tested it on the edge of coverage near Glacier National Park and went from unusable to about 10 Mbps down, which is enough for video calls.

The router is where a lot of people cheap out and regret it. If you're pulling multiple signals and managing them smartly, a dedicated mobile router like the GL.iNet Slate AX or the Pepwave MAX BR1 Mini makes the setup significantly more reliable. The Pepwave in particular lets you bond two cellular connections simultaneously and prioritize between Starlink and cellular automatically. It costs around $400 and is overkill for casual travelers, but for full-time remote workers, it earns its keep within a couple months of not troubleshooting internet mid-workday.

I won't pretend setting up a Pepwave is plug-and-play. The first time I configured one, I spent a frustrating Saturday afternoon on YouTube tutorials and their support chat. But once it's set up, you kind of forget it exists, which is exactly what you want from networking equipment.

## Making It Work Day-to-Day

The best internet setup fails if you don't plan your campsite selection around connectivity. I check signal maps before I commit to a location. Coverage Critic and the T-Mobile and Verizon coverage maps are decent starting points, but the most reliable intel is other RVers. The Campendium app has real reviews from people who note signal quality. So does the FreeRoam app for dispersed camping.

For video calls specifically: I try to schedule them when I know my connection is at its best. That usually means mornings, before campground neighbors are all awake and hammering the local cell tower. It means having Starlink up and running, not relying on cellular backup. It means testing everything the day before a big call.

One practical habit I built: I keep a 4G LTE signal at all times by having my phone connected to a different carrier than my hotspot. If one drops, I notice immediately. I'm not discovering it 30 seconds into a call.

For your download and upload speed minimums: most video conferencing (Zoom, Teams, Google Meet) needs about 3 Mbps up and down for acceptable HD quality. Aim for 10+ Mbps upload as your target so you have headroom. If you're doing anything with large file transfers, recording, or sharing your screen frequently, more is better.

The setup I've described isn't cheap upfront. Between hardware and monthly costs, expect to spend $600-1,000 getting it configured, then $150-250/month ongoing if you're running Starlink plus a cellular backup. That sounds like a lot until you miss one day's work because your internet failed and realize the math inverts pretty quickly.

Get the redundancy right before you need it, not during your 9 AM Monday call with the client.
