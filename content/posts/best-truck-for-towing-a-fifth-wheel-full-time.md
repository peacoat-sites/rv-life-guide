Most people ask the wrong question.

They come to me asking "which truck should I buy?" when what they really need to ask is "what are my actual numbers?" Because here's what I've watched happen more times than I can count: someone buys a beautiful, loaded-out Ram 1500 because their neighbor has one and loves it, then they try to tow their 14,000-pound fifth wheel home from the dealership and realize they're 2,000 pounds over the truck's maximum tow rating before they've even packed their clothes. That's an expensive lesson. The truck question only makes sense once you know your rig's loaded weight and your pin weight, so if you don't have those numbers yet, that's your actual first step.

That said, I've been towing fifth wheels full-time since 2016, and I've had real conversations with hundreds of people in the same situation. Let me tell you what I actually think, not what the manufacturer spec sheets say.

## The numbers that actually matter (before any truck talk)

Fifth wheels are heavy. Heavier than most people expect, and heavier once you add your gear, food, water, bikes, tools, and the dog. A "10,000-pound" trailer is a marketing number. Your loaded, real-world Gross Vehicle Weight Rating (GVWR) is what matters, and your pin weight (the weight the kingpin transfers to the truck's rear axle) will typically run 15-25% of that total.

Here's the rule I give everyone: take your trailer's GVWR, add a 20% buffer, and that's the minimum tow capacity you should be shopping for. So if your fifth wheel has a 16,000-pound GVWR, you want a truck rated for at least 19,000 pounds. Why the buffer? Because tow ratings are measured under ideal conditions with a properly tuned truck and no passengers and no gear in the bed. You are not operating under ideal conditions. You are loaded up, driving over a mountain pass in Utah in July, with your partner in the passenger seat and a bed full of tools.

Pin weight matters just as much. It loads down your rear axle directly, and your truck has a separate payload rating that accounts for this. Most people completely miss this number. I've met folks with trucks technically rated to tow their trailer, but their payload was maxed out by the pin weight alone, which means any passenger, any gear, any toolbox puts them over their truck's legal and structural limits. Pull the yellow sticker inside your driver's door jam. That's your payload rating. Guard it like a number in a safe.

## Heavy-duty or not: this is where I'll just tell you directly

If you're living in your fifth wheel full-time, and your rig has a GVWR over about 13,000 pounds, you should be looking at heavy-duty trucks. Full stop.

I know that's not what everyone wants to hear. HD trucks are more expensive to buy, more expensive to insure, and harder to park in a Trader Joe's lot. But a half-ton pulling a big residential fifth wheel every few days, sometimes in wind, sometimes in mountains, sometimes in summer heat, is a truck that will age badly on you fast. I've watched people do it. The transmission repairs alone will cost you more than the money you saved.

The heavy-duty segment means Ford Super Duty (F-250 and F-350), Ram HD (2500 and 3500), and Chevy/GMC HD (2500 and 3500). Those are your realistic options. There is no secret fourth choice.

For most full-timers with a fifth wheel in the 13,000-18,000 pound range, an F-250, Ram 2500, or Chevy 2500 in a diesel configuration will handle the job comfortably. Get into 18,000+ territory or a heavier residential rig, and I'd push you toward the 3500 variants. Not because you need the massive max tow number as a flex, but because the bigger axles, brakes, and frame handle the day-in-day-out stress better. You're not doing this once. You're doing this every week for years.

## Breaking down the three main contenders

**Ford F-250/F-350 Super Duty (6.7L Power Stroke diesel)**

The Super Duty has been my daily driver since 2019 and it replaced a Ram 2500 I put 110,000 miles on before that. I'm not brand loyal. I just think the current-generation Power Stroke is genuinely excellent, and Ford's fifth-wheel tow ratings are among the highest in class when properly configured. An F-350 with the 6.7L diesel can tow up to 40,000 pounds in certain configurations (yes, that's real, though you'll never need it). More relevant to you: payload ratings on F-350 dually configurations regularly exceed 7,000 pounds.

The cab and bed combo matters a lot. For fifth-wheel towing, you want at least a crew cab with a 6.75-foot or 8-foot bed. The shorter beds can work with a slider hitch, but I'd honestly just get the long bed if you're full-timing. The shorter bed requires more precision maneuvering and adds one more thing to think about when you're tired and setting up camp at 9pm.

One real gripe about Fords: the MyFord Touch infotainment system on older models (pre-2018ish) is genuinely terrible. The newer SYNC 4 systems are much better. Also, if you buy an older used Super Duty, budget for the EGR cooler. It's a known weak point on pre-2011 6.4L engines. Stick to 6.7L engines (2011 and newer) and you'll be much happier.

**Ram 2500/3500 (6.7L Cummins diesel)**

The Cummins is a legendary engine and I say that without a trace of irony. The 6.7L turbodiesel that Ram uses makes 850 lb-ft of torque in its current high-output configuration. Eight hundred and fifty. For a fifth-wheel full-timer pulling through the Rockies, torque is the number that matters more than horsepower, and that Cummins delivers it without drama.

Ram's interior quality has improved substantially. The current-gen 2500/3500 cabins feel genuinely premium, and the Air-Ride suspension option (available on 2019 and newer models) is something I have heard fifth-wheel folks rave about for ride quality when not towing.

The legitimate knock on Ram HD trucks is transmission. The Aisin six-speed that comes with the higher-output Cummins is actually very well regarded. The standard 68RFE automatic has a more mixed reputation, especially under heavy tow loads over many miles. If I were buying a Ram for full-time fifth-wheel use, I'd spend the money on the Aisin. It's not optional in my mind.

**Chevy/GMC 2500HD/3500HD (6.6L Duramax diesel)**

The L5P Duramax (found in 2017-current trucks) is a very capable engine and GM's Allison transmission partnership is one of the best in the business. The Allison 10-speed automatic is smooth and smart, and it handles tow loads in a way that just feels effortless. Plenty of full-timers swear by the Duramax/Allison combo and I have no argument with them.

What I do hear as a consistent complaint: the Chevy HD and GMC HD ride stiffer than the Ram when unladen. If you're covering big miles between destinations (we regularly do 400-500 mile travel days), that can wear on you. It's worth test-driving empty as well as loaded.

Payload ratings on 3500 DRW (dually) configurations are competitive with Ford. The 2500 single-rear-wheel trucks have slightly lower payload ratings than comparable Fords in some configurations, so check your specific numbers if payload is tight.

## The diesel question

Get the diesel.

I know they're $10,000-$12,000 more at purchase. I know diesel has been expensive lately. I know some people on forums will tell you a gasser can do the job. And technically, yes, the gas options on HD trucks have gotten impressive. The 7.3L Ford Godzilla, the 6.6L Chevy gas V8, they're real engines and they'll tow a fifth wheel.

But full-time towing is cumulative abuse over hundreds of thousands of miles. Diesel engines are built for exactly this. The fuel economy under load is significantly better (I typically see 10-12 mpg towing my fifth wheel versus the 7-8 mpg people report with gassers under similar conditions). The engine brake/exhaust brake systems on diesels will save your actual brakes descending mountain grades. And diesel engines routinely last 300,000-400,000 miles with regular maintenance. The math works out. Get the diesel.

## A few things nobody mentions

The kingpin-to-rear-axle distance on your specific truck/trailer combo affects how your rig handles. Shorter beds with slider hitches work, but matching a long bed to your fifth-wheel is cleaner. Run your measurements before you buy.

If you're buying used (and honestly, a 2-3 year old HD truck is the sweet spot for value), get a pre-purchase inspection from a diesel-specific shop, not a general mechanic. Diesel trucks have quirks that a general shop may miss. The $150 inspection fee has saved readers thousands of dollars.

Don't forget your hitch. A quality B&W Companion fifth-wheel hitch runs around $1,200-$1,800 depending on the model. It's worth every penny. I've used mine for six years with zero issues. (B&W is not sponsored content. I just genuinely like the product.)

### FAQ

#### How do I know if my truck can actually tow a specific fifth wheel?

Add up your trailer's loaded GVWR, check your truck's tow rating (must exceed GVWR plus a safety buffer), then separately verify your truck's payload rating covers the pin weight plus all passengers and cargo in the truck. Both numbers have to work, not just one.

#### Is a dually (dual rear wheels) necessary for full-time fifth-wheel towing?

Not always, but it depends on your pin weight. If your pin weight exceeds roughly 3,000 pounds, a dually gives you significantly better stability and payload capacity. For very large residential fifth wheels (20,000+ pounds), I'd call a dually more or less mandatory.

#### Can I use a half-ton like a Ford F-150 or Ram 1500 for a fifth wheel?

For lighter fifth wheels under about 12,000 pounds, some half-tons can technically handle the numbers, particularly the F-150 with the Max Tow package (rated to 14,000 pounds). But for full-time living, I wouldn't. The wear on a half-ton doing constant fifth-wheel towing is significant, and your margin for error is very thin.

#### What year/generation truck should I target when buying used?

For Ford, 2011 and newer (6.7L Power Stroke). For Ram, 2019 and newer gets you the updated cab and better Aisin availability. For GM, 2017 and newer for the current L5P Duramax. All three of those are solid starting points for a used search.

#### Does the truck's cab configuration affect fifth-wheel towing?

Crew cab is worth the money for full-time living, full stop. You need the storage and the livability. Extended cab configurations can work for towing mechanically, but you'll feel that compromised living space every single day. It's not the place to save money.

---

The truth is, there's no single "best" truck for full-time fifth-wheel living. There's only the truck that fits your specific trailer weight, your specific budget, and the honest realities of how many miles you're going to put on it. Anyone who tells you there's one obvious answer is probably selling something. What I can tell you is that a properly spec'd Ram 2500, Ford F-250, or Chevy 2500HD with a diesel engine will serve you well for years on the road, as long as you did the math first.
