My first renewal quote after going full-time came in at $4,200 a year. I nearly choked on my coffee. I'd been paying $800 a year for the same Class A motorhome when it sat in my driveway as a weekend toy, and suddenly the number had more than quintupled. Nobody warned me. The insurance agent who sold me that original policy never mentioned that full-time RV living triggers a completely different underwriting category, with completely different pricing. If you're about to make the leap, or you're already on the road and wondering why your premiums feel astronomical, let's talk about what's actually going on.

## Why RV Insurance Costs So Much More Than You Expect

Most people price out an RV purchase and remember to factor in insurance. They get a quote online, see something in the $600-$1,500 range, and move on. What they don't realize is that quote almost certainly assumes the RV is a recreational vehicle used a few weeks a year, not a primary residence covering 15,000 miles annually.

Insurance companies underwrite RVs in fundamentally different risk buckets. A part-time rig that parks in storage ten months a year is low exposure. A full-time rig is essentially a moving home that also functions as a car, a house, and a liability source all at once. When you tell your insurer you live in it full-time, the actuarial math changes significantly.

There's also the size and class issue. A Class C with an MSRP of $90,000 costs more to insure than a compact sedan costs to buy. Comprehensive and collision coverage on a vehicle worth $150,000 or $200,000 adds up fast, especially when repair labor rates for RVs can hit $150-$200 per hour at a dealership.

And then there's the full-timer liability question. Your homeowner's policy probably lapsed when you sold your house. Your renter's policy doesn't apply to a vehicle. The personal liability coverage that used to protect you if someone tripped on your property now needs to live inside your RV policy, which bumps the premium again.

## The Main Factors That Drive Your Specific Premium

Understanding what insurers actually measure helps you shop smarter and, in some cases, push back on a quote.

**Type and class of RV.** Class A diesel pushers are the most expensive to insure. Class B vans sit at the lower end. Class C and fifth wheels fall somewhere in the middle. A $45,000 travel trailer will cost considerably less than a $300,000 Tiffin Allegro Bus, all else equal.

**Full-timer vs. recreational status.** Full-timer endorsements typically add 25-60% to a base premium. Some carriers, like National General and Progressive, have specific full-timer packages. Others simply won't write full-time coverage and will cancel you if they find out. Don't hide this from your insurer. If you have a claim while living full-time on a recreational policy, you could be denied.

**Annual mileage.** I average about 15,000-18,000 miles a year. Some full-timers do 25,000+. Higher mileage increases your collision exposure. Some carriers ask for an odometer reading at renewal.

**Driving record and age.** A DUI or multiple at-fault accidents will spike your premium dramatically. Younger drivers also pay more. After 25 years of clean driving history, I still get asked about incidents every single renewal cycle.

**Storage location and stationary periods.** If you winter in one spot for three or four months, some carriers offer a reduced rate during that stationary period. It's worth asking.

**Deductible choices.** Raising your collision deductible from $500 to $2,500 can drop annual premiums by $400-$700 on a higher-value rig. That's a legitimate strategy if you have a decent emergency fund.

## What Full-Timer Coverage Actually Includes (And What It Doesn't)

Full-timer RV insurance is roughly the auto coverage plus homeowner's coverage combination that regular people carry separately. But the details matter.

A comprehensive full-timer policy typically includes:

- Liability for bodily injury and property damage (think: you back into a car in a campground)
- Collision coverage
- Comprehensive coverage (fire, theft, hail, flooding)
- Personal belongings coverage (your laptops, clothes, cooking gear, everything inside)
- Emergency expense coverage if the rig is damaged and you need a hotel
- Full-timer liability for incidents on your "premises," meaning your campsite
- Medical payments or personal injury protection
- Roadside assistance and towing, sometimes up to a specified dollar amount per incident

What most base policies won't cover without add-ons: attached awnings that blow out in a storm, slide-outs damaged by a foreign object, or a satellite dish on the roof. Read the exclusions. Seriously, read them. I had a policy for eight months before I realized my solar array wasn't covered because it wasn't listed as permanently attached equipment. That was a $3,200 exposure I was carrying blind.

## Comparing the Major RV Insurance Carriers

Shopping around is not optional. I've gotten quotes that varied by $1,800 annually for nearly identical coverage on the same rig. Here's a general comparison of the major players as of recent years:

| Carrier | Full-Timer Coverage | Known For | Best Fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Progressive | Yes | Competitive rates, flexible deductibles | Most RV types, widely available |
| National General (now Allstate) | Yes, dedicated full-timer program | Agreed value option, strong personal property | Full-timers who need primary liability |
| Good Sam (underwritten by Nationwide) | Yes | Bundled roadside, campground discounts | Good Sam members already in the ecosystem |
| Foremost (part of Farmers) | Yes | Long-standing specialty RV insurer | Vintage or specialty rigs |
| State Farm | Limited | Low rates for recreational use | Part-timers, not ideal for full-timers |
| Geico | Recreational focus | Cheap quotes | Weekend warriors, not full-timers |

The "agreed value" vs. "actual cash value" distinction is critical. Actual cash value means if your 8-year-old Class A burns down, you get current market value minus depreciation. Agreed value means you and the insurer locked in a number at policy start, and that's what you get. For a rig that's your only home, agreed value is worth the higher premium almost every time.

## Step-by-Step: How to Shop for Full-Timer RV Insurance Without Getting Burned

1. **Determine your use category honestly.** Are you full-time (living in it more than 150 days per year, or no other fixed residence)? Part-time? Seasonal? Your answer changes which carriers will even consider you and what endorsements you need.

2. **Get the replacement cost or agreed value of your rig.** Check NADA, RV Trader recent sales, and your original purchase documents. Don't let an insurer default you into a depreciating actual cash value policy without at least comparing the premium difference.

3. **Make a personal property inventory.** Walk through your rig and list everything: electronics, clothing, tools, bikes, kayaks. Total it up. Most base policies cover $3,000-$5,000 in personal property. Full-timers carrying $20,000 in gear need a rider.

4. **Ask each carrier specifically about full-timer endorsements.** Don't assume. Ask: "Does this policy cover me as a full-time resident? Is there a full-timer endorsement? What's excluded?"

5. **Compare at least four carriers.** Get quotes from Progressive, National General, Good Sam, and one regional or specialty insurer. Use an independent RV insurance broker if you want someone to do the comparison work. They earn commission from carriers, so the service is free to you.

6. **Check the financial strength rating.** AM Best ratings matter. A carrier rated B or lower is a solvency risk you don't want to find out about when you're filing a $60,000 claim.

7. **Review the policy before you sign.** Ask for a specimen policy document, not just a summary. Look at exclusions, the definition of "full-timer," and what happens if you're in an accident out of your home state.

8. **Revisit annually.** My premium dropped $600 one year just because I switched carriers at renewal. Loyalty in insurance is rarely rewarded.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does RV insurance cost per year on average?

For a recreational user with a mid-range Class A or Class C, annual premiums typically run $800-$2,000. Full-timers should expect $2,500-$5,000 or more, depending on the value of the rig, mileage, and coverage levels. I've personally paid anywhere from $2,200 to $4,400 depending on the year and the carrier. A $250,000 diesel pusher with full-timer liability and agreed value coverage can easily hit $6,000 annually.

### Do I need full-timer coverage if I still technically have a home address?

If you're using a family member's address as a domicile state for licensing purposes but you actually live in your RV full-time, you should still disclose your actual living situation to your insurer. Using a mailing address to maintain recreational status on a policy you're actually using as a full-timer is considered material misrepresentation. A denied claim is a brutal way to learn that lesson.

### Does RV insurance cover my belongings inside the rig?

Yes, most full-timer policies include some personal property coverage, but the default limits are often too low for people who've moved their entire lives into a rig. Standard limits of $3,000-$5,000 won't cover a laptop, camera, e-bikes, and a year's worth of clothing. Ask specifically about the personal property limit and increase it if needed. Some policies also exclude items over a certain individual value, like jewelry or camera gear, which may require a scheduled rider.

### What happens to my RV insurance if I park for the winter?

If you're stationary at a campground or storage facility for 60+ consecutive days, some carriers offer a premium reduction during that period because the mileage exposure drops. You typically still need liability and comprehensive coverage even while parked. A falling tree branch, a campground flood, or a fire in a neighboring rig are real risks that don't go away because you've stopped driving.

### Is RV insurance required by law?

In every U.S. state, any motorized RV (Class A, B, or C) requires at minimum liability insurance to legally operate on public roads, just like a car. The minimums vary by state. Towable RVs like fifth wheels and travel trailers are generally not required to carry their own liability policy because they're covered under the tow vehicle's liability, but lenders typically require comprehensive and collision if you have a loan. Full-timer liability, personal property, and contents coverage are optional but, honestly, not something you want to skip when your home is on the line.

Six years and over 90,000 miles later, I've filed two claims: one for a slideout motor that failed from an impact I didn't even notice, and one for a cracked windshield from a rock on I-40 through New Mexico. Both times, the policy paid out and I was genuinely glad I hadn't cut corners on coverage. RV insurance isn't glamorous, it doesn't show up in YouTube vanlife videos, and it won't make your Instagram feed more interesting. But when something goes wrong, and eventually something always goes wrong, it's the thing standing between you and a catastrophic financial hit. Get the right policy, read it, and revisit it every year. That's really all there is to it.
