My first renewal quote after going full-time nearly killed the whole experiment before it got off the ground. I'd been paying $847 a year to insure the rig as "recreational" while still owning the house. The moment I mentioned I was now a full-timer, the agent went quiet, typed for what felt like a full minute, and came back with $2,340. Same rig. Same driving record. Nearly three times the price. That's the number nobody mentions in those glossy YouTube videos about selling everything and hitting the road.

## Why Full-Timer Status Changes Everything

Standard RV insurance assumes your motorhome or travel trailer spends most of its life parked in a driveway. You drive it maybe 5,000 miles a year, hit a few campgrounds in summer, and call it a season. The risk model is built on that assumption.

Full-time living blows that apart. You're doing 20,000 to 35,000 miles annually, parking in unfamiliar places night after night, and your rig isn't just your vehicle anymore. It's also your home. That last part is what really matters to insurers. If a homeowner's house burns down, they still have a car. If your Class A catches fire at a campground in Colorado, you've lost your transportation, your shelter, your furniture, your electronics, and everything else you own in one moment. Insurers price for that risk.

Full-timer policies include something called personal belongings coverage or full-timer personal property coverage. It's renter's insurance baked into your RV policy, covering clothes, tools, kitchenware, laptops, the entire contents of your mobile home. Coverage limits typically start around $3,000 and can climb to $50,000 or higher depending on the carrier.

## What Actually Drives Your Premium

Several factors hit that final number, and knowing them can save you serious money when you're comparing policies.

**The rig itself.** A 2024 Tiffin Allegro Bus 45OPP costs way more to replace than a 2008 Fleetwood Bounder. But here's the bigger choice: agreed value versus actual cash value. With agreed value, you get the exact amount you and your insurer settled on when the policy started. Actual cash value means you get what depreciation says it's worth now, which on a 10-year-old diesel pusher can be thousands less than you owe. Push for agreed value if you can get it.

**Your driving record.** One DUI or a pattern of speeding tickets can double your premium or get you rejected entirely. I had a minor at-fault fender bender in my personal car in 2021 (nothing to do with the RV) and it still hit my rate for two full years.

**Where you park it.** Even for full-timers, some policies want a home base state. South Dakota, Texas, and Florida are popular with full-timers partly because their insurance rates are more reasonable. South Dakota especially comes in lean. Your garaging state can swing your annual premium by $300 to $600.

**Tow vehicles and towed vehicles.** If you're towing a car behind your Class A, that dinghy may or may not be covered under the RV policy. If you're pulling a fifth wheel with a truck, your truck is under its own auto policy, but the trailer itself needs separate coverage. Make sure there are no gaps.

**Deductible choices.** Bump your comprehensive and collision deductible from $500 to $1,500 and you'll trim $200 to $400 off the annual premium depending on the carrier. If you've got an emergency fund, that's usually a smart move.

## The Main Carriers Worth Calling

Not every insurance company gets the full-timer lifestyle. Plenty of standard auto carriers will write you an RV policy and then make claiming a nightmare once they realize you actually live in it. You want someone with full-timer experience who won't flinch when you say you don't have a mailing address.

Four names show up repeatedly in full-timer forums and from agents who specialize in this:

**Progressive** is the volume player. They write more RV policies than anyone else and their rates are competitive for Class Bs and travel trailers. Adding their full-timer endorsement is straightforward. The downside: claims experiences vary wildly depending on which adjuster you get.

**National General** has earned genuine trust in the full-timer community. Their full-timer package includes personal liability coverage that works more like a homeowner's policy, which matters if someone slips and falls at your campsite.

**Good Sam Insurance** (underwritten by Foremost) is heavily marketed to RV owners and they've got name recognition, but I've seen their quotes run 15 to 25 percent higher than comparable Progressive or National General policies for identical coverage and the same rig. Get a comparison before assuming the RV-branded insurer is your best deal.

**Roamly** is newer and specifically built for full-timers and part-timers. They've gotten solid reviews for handling claims cleanly and policies that don't penalize you for actually living in your RV. Worth a quote especially if you're in a Class B or van conversion.

Get at least three quotes. The same coverage on the same rig can differ by $600 to $900 per year between carriers.

## A Real Coverage Comparison: What You're Actually Buying

| Coverage Type | What It Does | Typical Annual Cost Add-On | Full-Timer Need Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liability (bodily injury/property damage) | Covers damage or injury you cause to others | Base policy | Absolutely required |
| Collision | Pays for your rig if you hit something | $300-$800/yr depending on rig value | Essential |
| Comprehensive | Covers fire, theft, weather, animals | $200-$500/yr | Essential |
| Full-timer personal property | Covers belongings inside the rig | $100-$350/yr | Critical for full-timers |
| Full-timer liability | Covers you if guests are injured at your campsite | $80-$200/yr | Critical for full-timers |
| Vacation liability | Covers short-term campsite incidents (not full-timer) | $30-$80/yr | Part-timers only |
| Emergency expense coverage | Hotel/rental if rig is in repair | $50-$150/yr | Smart to carry |
| Roadside assistance | Towing, tire changes, lockouts for a large vehicle | $60-$180/yr | Very useful |
| Agreed value endorsement | Locks in payout amount vs. depreciated value | Varies by rig | Worth having if available |

One thing about roadside assistance: your AAA membership almost certainly won't cover a 40-foot Class A or a 30,000-pound fifth wheel the way you'd hope. RV-specific roadside programs, whether through your insurer, Coach-Net, or Good Sam's roadside plan, are built for these big vehicles. Don't try to save money here. A single tow on a motorhome can run $500 to $1,200.

## How to Actually Lower Your Premium Without Gutting Your Coverage

Here's the practical stuff. None of it is complicated, but it does take work.

1. **Establish a home base in a favorable state.** If you're already planning to domicile in South Dakota, Texas, or Florida, factor insurance into that decision. Get quotes with each state as your garaging address before you commit.

2. **Take an RV safety course.** Progressive and National General both offer 5 to 10 percent discounts for completing an approved RV driving safety course. The RV Safety and Education Foundation offers recognized courses.

3. **Bundle strategically, but verify the savings.** If you have a tow vehicle, bundling auto and RV with the same carrier sometimes saves money. Sometimes it doesn't. Run the numbers both ways.

4. **Document your belongings before you need to claim them.** Walk through your rig once a year with your phone and video everything. Open cabinets. Show serial numbers. Upload it to cloud storage. This won't lower your premium but it makes a personal property claim way easier.

5. **Ask specifically about full-timer discounts.** Some carriers have them and don't advertise them prominently. Direct question to your agent: "Is there a full-time or full-timer-specific rate tier I should be in?"

6. **Review your coverage limits on personal property annually.** If you added $3,000 worth of solar equipment to your roof this year, your personal property coverage maximum may not reflect that. [A good battery monitor](https://amzn.to/3example) and solar hardware add up fast, and you'll want those assets covered. *(This site may earn a commission on qualifying purchases.)*

7. **Consider higher deductibles on older rigs.** If your rig's agreed or actual cash value is under $40,000, paying $800 a year for comprehensive and collision with a $500 deductible may not make sense. Run the math on whether multiple years of premiums equal what you'd actually lose in a claim.
