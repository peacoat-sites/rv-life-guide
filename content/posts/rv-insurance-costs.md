My first renewal quote after going full-time nearly ended the experiment before it started. I'd been paying $847 a year insuring the rig as a "recreational" vehicle while still owning the house. The moment I called to update my status to full-timer, the agent paused, typed for what felt like a full minute, and came back with $2,340. Same rig. Same driving record. Nearly three times the price. That's the number nobody puts in the glossy YouTube videos about selling everything and hitting the road.

## Why Full-Timer Status Changes Everything

Standard RV insurance treats your motorhome or travel trailer the way your old car insurer treated a weekend vehicle. It's assumed to sit in a driveway most of the year, get driven maybe 5,000 miles, and park at a campground a few weekends a summer. The risk model is built around that usage pattern.

When you go full-time, the calculus flips completely. You're logging 20,000 to 35,000 miles a year, parking on unfamiliar surfaces in unfamiliar places, and your rig is also your only home. That last part is what really moves the needle. If a traditional homeowner's house burns down, they don't also lose their vehicle. If your Class A goes up in a fire at a campground, you've lost your transportation, your shelter, your furniture, your laptop, and your dog's vet records all at once. Insurers know this. They price accordingly.

Full-timer policies typically include something called "personal belongings coverage" or "full-timer personal property coverage." This is essentially renter's insurance baked into the RV policy. It covers clothes, electronics, tools, kitchenware, the stuff that fills your home on wheels. Limits usually start around $3,000 and can go up to $50,000 or more depending on the carrier and what you're willing to pay.

## What Actually Drives Your Premium

A lot of factors go into that final number, and understanding them can save you real money when you're shopping policies.

**The rig itself.** A 2024 Tiffin Allegro Bus 45OPP has a replacement cost that a 2008 Fleetwood Bounder does not. Agreed value versus actual cash value is a big fork in the road here. Agreed value means if the rig is totaled, you get the number you and the insurer agreed on at policy inception. Actual cash value means you get what the market says it's worth after depreciation, which on a 10-year-old diesel pusher can be significantly less than you paid or owe. Push hard for agreed value if you can get it.

**Your driving record.** One DUI or a pattern of speeding tickets can double a premium or get you declined entirely. I had a minor at-fault fender bender in my personal car in 2021, nothing involving the RV, and it still affected my rate for two full policy cycles.

**Where you park it.** Some policies ask for a "home base" state even for full-timers. South Dakota, Texas, and Florida are popular choices for full-timers partly because of favorable insurance rates. South Dakota in particular tends to come in lean. Your garaging state can swing the annual premium by $300 to $600.

**Tow vehicles and towed vehicles.** If you're towing a car behind your Class A, that "dinghy" may or may not be covered under your RV policy. If you're pulling a fifth wheel with a truck, your truck is typically under its own auto policy, but the trailer itself needs separate coverage. Make sure there are no gaps in that chain.

**Deductible choices.** Raising your comprehensive and collision deductible from $500 to $1,500 can trim your annual premium by $200 to $400 depending on the carrier. If you've got an emergency fund, this is usually a smart trade.

## The Main Carriers Worth Calling

Not every insurance company understands the full-timer lifestyle. A lot of standard auto carriers will write you an RV policy and then find reasons to complicate a claim when they realize you actually live in it. You want a company that has written full-timer policies before and won't blink when you say you don't have a stick-and-brick address.

The four names that come up most consistently in full-timer forums and from agents who specialize in this space:

**Progressive** is the volume player. They write more RV policies than anyone else, and their rates are often competitive for Class Bs and travel trailers. Their full-timer endorsement is straightforward to add. The tradeoff is that claims experiences vary widely depending on the adjuster you get.

**National General** has a strong reputation in the full-timer community specifically. Their full-timer package includes personal liability coverage that's more like a homeowner's policy, which matters if someone trips and falls in your campsite.

**Good Sam Insurance** (underwritten by Foremost) is heavily marketed toward the RV crowd and has name recognition, but I've seen quotes come in 15 to 25 percent higher than comparable Progressive or National General policies for the same rig and coverage levels. Do the comparison before you assume the RV-branded insurer is the best deal.

**Roamly** is newer and specifically built for full-timers and part-timers. They've gotten solid reviews for clean claims handling and policies that don't penalize you for full-time use. Worth getting a quote from them especially if you're in a Class B or van conversion.

Always get at least three quotes. Rates between carriers for the same coverage on the same rig can differ by $600 to $900 per year.

One note on roadside assistance: your AAA membership almost certainly won't cover a 40-foot Class A or a 30,000-pound fifth wheel setup the way you'd hope. RV-specific roadside programs, whether through your insurer, Coach-Net, or Good Sam's roadside plan, are calibrated for big vehicles. This is not the place to cut corners. A single tow on a motorhome can run $500 to $1,200.

## How to Actually Lower Your Premium Without Gutting Your Coverage

This is the practical stuff. None of it is complicated, but it requires some legwork.

1. **Establish a home base in a favorable state.** If you're already planning to domicile in South Dakota, Texas, or Florida, factor insurance rates into that decision. Get quotes with each state listed as your garaging address before you commit.

2. **Take an RV safety course.** Some carriers, including Progressive and National General, offer discounts of 5 to 10 percent for completing an approved RV driving safety course. The RV Safety and Education Foundation offers recognized courses.

3. **Bundle strategically, but verify the savings.** If you have a tow vehicle, bundling auto and RV with the same carrier sometimes saves money. Sometimes it doesn't. Run the numbers both ways.

4. **Document your belongings before you need to claim them.** Walk through your rig once a year with your phone and video everything. Open cabinets. Show serial numbers. Upload it to cloud storage. This doesn't lower your premium but it makes a personal property claim survivable.

5. **Ask specifically about full-timer discounts.** Some carriers have them and don't advertise them prominently. Ask the agent directly: "Is there a full-time or full-timer-specific rate tier I should be in?"

6. **Review your coverage limits on personal property annually.** If you added $3,000 worth of solar equipment to your roof this year, your personal property coverage maximum may not reflect that. [A good battery monitor](https://amzn.to/3example) and solar hardware can add up fast, and you'll want those assets covered. *(This site may earn a commission on qualifying purchases.)*

7. **Consider higher deductibles on older rigs.** If your rig's agreed or actual cash value is under $40,000, paying $800 a year for comprehensive and collision with a $500 deductible may not pencil out. Do the math on how many years of premiums equal a likely claim.

## FAQ

### How much does RV insurance cost per year for a full-timer?

The honest range is wide: $1,200 on the low end for a smaller, older Class C or travel trailer with minimal personal property coverage, to $5,000 or more for a high-value Class A diesel pusher with full-timer endorsements, high personal property limits, and agreed value coverage. Most full-timers I've talked to land somewhere between $1,800 and $3,200 annually.

### Do I really need a full-timer endorsement if I just live in my RV most of the time?

Yes, and this is not a gray area. If you're living in your RV more than roughly 150 days a year (exact thresholds vary by carrier), you are typically required to disclose this. Filing a claim under a recreational policy while living full-time in the rig can result in a denied claim. That's a catastrophic outcome when your home is involved.

### Will my regular auto insurance cover my RV?

Standard auto insurance covers cars and trucks. It will not cover your motorhome or travel trailer beyond very basic liability in some cases. You need a separate RV-specific policy. If you tow a car behind your Class A on a tow dolly or four-down, ask both your RV insurer and auto insurer exactly how that towed vehicle is covered in motion.

### What's the difference between agreed value and actual cash value for an RV?

Agreed value means you and the insurer set a specific dollar amount when the policy is written. If there's a total loss, that's what you get, no depreciation argument. Actual cash value means the insurer determines the market value at the time of the claim, accounting for depreciation. On a 10-year-old rig that you paid $75,000 for, actual cash value might come back at $32,000. For full-timers, this distinction can be the difference between getting back on the road or not.

### Can I insure a van conversion or DIY build?

Yes, but it takes more effort. Roamly and a handful of specialty brokers handle van conversions and self-builds. You'll likely need documentation of the build, photos, and potentially an appraisal. Standard carriers often decline or severely limit coverage on non-manufacturer-built units. Shop specifically for carriers experienced with conversions, and don't understate the value of your build to save on premiums. That's a gamble you'll lose.

Six years in, I still review my policy every single renewal. Rates shift, my rig changes, and what I carry inside it changes. The year I added 400 watts of solar and a lithium battery bank I increased my personal property coverage by $8,000. Cost me an extra $60 a year. That's the kind of adjustment that's easy to skip until you need it. Don't skip it.
