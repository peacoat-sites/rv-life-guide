Every October, somewhere around Quartzsite or Casa Grande, you start seeing the same thing: a slow parade of Class A coaches, fifth wheels, and dusty pickup-towing trailers all pointing southwest. If you're reading this, you might be wondering whether you should be one of them. Whether Arizona in winter is actually as good as people say, or whether it's just a crowd of retirees circling a Walmart in the sun.

Here's what I tell people who ask: it's both. And knowing which parts are which before you commit to a five-month stay can save you a lot of money and frustration.

I've spent parts of eight winters in Arizona, from the concrete-jungle RV parks along Yuma's outskirts to the high desert silence outside Benson. I've made expensive mistakes (I'll get to those), found parks I'd return to every year without hesitation, and developed some strong opinions about how to do this well.

---

## Why Arizona, and Why Now

The numbers are what get people through the door. Average daytime highs in the low-to-mid 70s from November through February in the Tucson and Phoenix basins. Yuma averages more sunny days per year than any other city in the United States, which sounds like a brochure claim until you've spent a January there watching people sit outside in shirt sleeves while your friends back in Ohio are shoveling. That part is real.

But it's not just the weather. Arizona has a genuine snowbird infrastructure in a way that, say, New Mexico doesn't. That means parks built specifically for long-term winter guests, not retrofitted campgrounds tolerating them. Good laundry facilities, full hookups with 50-amp service, organized activities, and neighbors who've been doing this for thirty years and can tell you exactly which grocery store has the best produce. There's a social fabric to the established parks that you don't get at a campground designed for weekend campers.

The flip side: it's crowded. Seriously crowded. The window from mid-October to mid-April is when everyone shows up, and "everyone" is not an exaggeration. Parks that are half-empty in September are reservation-only by November 1. If you're thinking about showing up without a reservation and finding something decent, you might be in for a shock.

## The Main Snowbird Clusters (and What They're Actually Like)

Arizona's snowbird population doesn't spread itself evenly. It concentrates in four main areas, and each has a completely different feel.

**Yuma** is the most committed snowbird destination in the state. The area around Yuma draws somewhere north of 90,000 seasonal residents every winter, and it shows. The parks here tend to be the most organized, the most social, and the most amenity-heavy. Think: heated pools, pickleball courts, organized dances, craft rooms, and a general atmosphere of people who've built a second social life here. Fortuna de Oro and Cactus Gardens are well-regarded 55+ parks. Imperial Dam Long Term Visitor Area, run by the BLM, offers a completely different experience, basically free camping ($180 for the season as of this year) on public land outside town, very popular with the self-sufficient crowd. Water available, but minimal amenities. You'll see everything from beat-up trailers to six-figure coaches out there.

**The Tucson area** has a more mixed feel. You've got full-amenity destination resorts like Rincon Country East and West, which genuinely feel like small towns, and then you've got quieter parks in Green Valley and Sierra Vista for people who want proximity to hiking and the Mexico border towns without the Yuma social scene. I spent two winters in Benson, which is east of Tucson on I-10, and it's underrated. Quieter, cheaper, and you're forty minutes from Tombstone and the Chiricahua Mountains.

**The Phoenix metro** is a different animal entirely. Parks here are closer to RV resorts, often with the price tags to match. Thousand Trails Sunland Springs in Mesa and Good Life RV Resort in Mesa both run in the $1,500-$2,000/month range during peak season if you don't have a membership. The trade-off is access to a major city: world-class hospitals, Costco, every specialty store you could want, and a lot of cultural activity. If you have serious medical needs or just like urban convenience, Phoenix makes sense. If you want quiet and cheap, it doesn't.

**Quartzsite** deserves its own paragraph. It's technically a town in the desert west of Phoenix, and for a few weeks in January it becomes one of the strangest gatherings in North America: the Quartzsite Sports, Vacation, and RV Show draws hundreds of thousands of people. The surrounding BLM land hosts a city of boondockers. If you've never boondocked before, it's a good place to try it, because you're not alone out there. After the big show ends, the crowds thin and the desert gets quiet again. Long-term BLM camping here is also around $180 for the season.

## The Reservation Problem (Read This Before You Make Plans)

I want to spend some real time here because this is where I see people get burned.

The parks that are worth staying at in Arizona book out for their prime months, October through March, sometimes a full year in advance. I'm not exaggerating. If you decide in August that you want a spot at a well-regarded resort in Yuma or Mesa for January, you may find nothing available, or you'll be looking at last-minute spots in parks you've never heard of with no reviews.

Here's what I tell people who are new to this: contact your target parks no later than February or March for the following winter season. Seriously. Call them, not just email. Ask specifically about their reservation window and whether they have a waiting list. Some of the best parks have wait lists for seasonal spots that run two to three years out.

The membership parks, specifically Thousand Trails, Outdoor Resorts, and Sun Outdoors, change the math somewhat. If you're planning to snowbird regularly, a membership at one of these networks can pay for itself over two or three seasons. The upfront costs vary wildly (I've seen Thousand Trails memberships sell on the secondary market for anywhere from $1,500 to $8,000 depending on the tier), and the parks within the network vary a lot in quality. Do your homework before buying.

One thing I learned the hard way: don't book a five-month stay at a park you've never visited based only on online reviews. If you can, spend a week there in the fall before committing to a season. Parks that look great on paper sometimes have maintenance issues, noise problems, or a vibe that just doesn't fit you. Reviews also age. A park that was excellent three years ago may have changed management twice since then.

## The Budget Reality Nobody Talks About Straight

Arizona snowbirding has a reputation as an affordable lifestyle. It can be. It can also quietly cost you more than you expected.

The mid-range parks in Yuma and Tucson are running $600-$950/month for a full-hookup site with reasonable amenities as of this year. The Phoenix resorts are closer to $1,200-$2,000. Add propane (minimal in mild weather, but you'll use some), activity costs, groceries in resort areas (which tend to be marked up), and the incidental repairs that always seem to happen when you're 1,500 miles from your mechanic at home, and your monthly number climbs.

One specific budget trap: Arizona summers are brutal on RV systems. Even if you're only there for winter, a lot of snowbirds roll in with AC units that are marginal, batteries that are old, or roof seals that haven't been checked in two years. The dry heat finds every weakness. Get a full inspection before you leave home, not when you arrive. I've watched neighbors spend $1,800 on an emergency AC replacement in February that could have been caught for $200 at home.

If you're doing any amount of boondocking on BLM land, a solid solar setup and a good battery monitor will change your life. I run a Victron BMV-712 battery monitor ($80-90 on Amazon) and can tell you exactly how much capacity I have at any moment. Worth every cent for peace of mind. ## What Actually Makes a Good Park for Long-Term Living

After this many seasons, here's my honest breakdown of what matters and what doesn't.

The wifi situation at most Arizona snowbird parks is, charitably, challenged. Shared bandwidth across 300 rigs doesn't work for anyone who needs to stream or work remotely. Budget for a cell data plan as your primary connection. A Pepwave or similar hotspot router can help you manage your data and combine signals. Don't choose a park based on their "complimentary wifi" claim.

The sites themselves: pull-throughs sound appealing but aren't always better. Some of the best long-term sites are back-ins with concrete pads, full utilities, and good neighbors. Check whether sites have shade, because six weeks in the Arizona winter sun in an all-black asphalt lot gets old. Check the spacing too. Some parks cram in rigs so tight you're essentially sharing a parking garage.

Activities matter more than you might expect, especially if you're there alone or if this is your first season. The organized breakfasts, game nights, and hiking clubs at the bigger parks aren't just filler. They're how people make the winter feel like a life instead of a waiting room. I was skeptical of this when I started. I'm not anymore.
