Every October, somewhere around Quartzsite or Casa Grande, you start seeing the same thing: a slow parade of Class A coaches, fifth wheels, and dusty pickup-towing trailers all pointing southwest. If you're reading this, you might be wondering whether you should be one of them. Whether Arizona in winter is actually as good as people say, or whether it's just a crowd of retirees circling a Walmart in the sun.

It's both, actually. And knowing which parts are which before you commit to a five-month stay can save you a lot of money and frustration.

I've spent parts of eight winters in Arizona, from the concrete-jungle RV parks along Yuma's outskirts to the high desert silence outside Benson. I've made expensive mistakes (I'll get to those), found parks I'd return to every year without hesitation, and developed some strong opinions about how to do this well.

## Why Arizona, and Why Now

The numbers are what get people through the door. Average daytime highs in the low-to-mid 70s from November through February in the Tucson and Phoenix basins. Yuma averages more sunny days per year than any other city in the United States, which sounds like marketing copy until you've spent a January there watching people sit outside in shirt sleeves while your friends back in Ohio are shoveling. That part is real.

But there's more to it than weather. Arizona has built genuine infrastructure for long-term winter guests in a way that New Mexico or Texas really hasn't. That means parks designed specifically for seasonal visitors, not campgrounds tolerating them. Full hookups with 50-amp service, good laundry facilities, organized activities, and neighbors who've been doing this for thirty years and can tell you which grocery store has decent produce. You get a social fabric at the established parks that doesn't exist at a campground built for weekend campers.

The flip side: it's crowded. Seriously crowded. Mid-October through mid-April is when everyone arrives, and "everyone" isn't an exaggeration. Parks that run half-empty in September become reservation-only by November 1. If you're thinking about showing up without a reservation and finding something decent, you're probably setting yourself up for disappointment.

## The Main Snowbird Clusters (and What They're Actually Like)

Arizona's snowbird population doesn't spread evenly. It concentrates in four main areas, each with a completely different feel.

**Yuma** is the most committed snowbird destination in the state. The area draws somewhere north of 90,000 seasonal residents every winter, and it shows. The parks tend to be the most organized, the most social, and the most amenity-heavy. Heated pools, pickleball courts, organized dances, craft rooms, a general atmosphere of people who've built an entire second social life here. Fortuna de Oro and Cactus Gardens are well-regarded 55+ parks. Imperial Dam Long Term Visitor Area, run by the BLM, offers something completely different: basically free camping ($180 for the season as of this year) on public land outside town. Water available, but minimal amenities. You'll see everything from beat-up trailers to six-figure coaches.

**Tucson** has a mixed feel. Full-amenity destination resorts like Rincon Country East and West genuinely feel like small towns, while quieter parks in Green Valley and Sierra Vista work better if you want hiking and Mexico border towns without the Yuma social scene. I spent two winters in Benson, east of Tucson on I-10, and it's underrated. Cheaper, quieter, forty minutes from Tombstone and the Chiricahua Mountains.

**Phoenix metro** is a different animal. Parks here function more like RV resorts, often with matching price tags. Thousand Trails Sunland Springs and Good Life RV Resort in Mesa run $1,500-$2,000 a month during peak season if you lack a membership. The trade-off is access to a major city: world-class hospitals, Costco, every specialty store imaginable, real cultural activity. If you have serious medical needs or just appreciate urban convenience, Phoenix makes sense. If you want quiet and cheap, it doesn't.

**Quartzsite** needs its own paragraph. It's technically a town in the desert west of Phoenix, and in January it becomes one of North America's strangest gatherings. The Quartzsite Sports, Vacation, and RV Show draws hundreds of thousands. BLM land around it hosts a temporary city of boondockers. If you've never boondocked, it's a good starting point because you won't be isolated. After the show ends in early February, crowds thin and the desert gets quiet. BLM camping here runs about $180 for the season.

## The Reservation Problem (Read This Before You Make Plans)

I need to spend real time here because this is where I see people get burned.

The parks worth staying at in Arizona book their prime months (October through March) sometimes a full year in advance. If you decide in August that you want a spot at a well-regarded resort in Yuma for January, you might find nothing available. Or you'll be staring at last-minute openings in parks you've never heard of with no reviews.

Contact your target parks no later than February or March for the following winter season. Call them, don't just email. Ask about reservation windows and waiting lists. Some of the best parks have waiting lists for seasonal spots that run two to three years.

Membership parks like Thousand Trails, Outdoor Resorts, and Sun Outdoors change the equation somewhat. A membership can pay for itself over two or three seasons if you plan to snowbird regularly. Upfront costs vary wildly, I've seen Thousand Trails memberships on the secondary market anywhere from $1,500 to $8,000 depending on tier, and parks within the network vary a lot in quality. Do your homework.

One thing I learned hard: don't book a five-month stay at a park you've never visited based only on online reviews. Spend a week there in the fall before committing to a full season. Parks that look great on paper sometimes have maintenance issues, noise problems, or a vibe that just doesn't match your life. Reviews age too. A park that was excellent three years ago may have changed management twice.

## The Budget Reality Nobody Talks About Straight

Arizona snowbirding has a reputation as affordable. It can be. It can also quietly cost you way more than expected.

Mid-range parks in Yuma and Tucson are running $600-$950 a month for a full-hookup site with reasonable amenities as of this year. Phoenix resorts hit $1,200-$2,000. Add propane, activity costs, groceries in resort areas (marked up everywhere), and the incidental repairs that always happen 1,500 miles from your mechanic at home, and your monthly number climbs fast.

One specific budget trap: Arizona summers destroy RV systems, even if you're only there winters. A lot of snowbirds roll in with marginal AC units, old batteries, or roof seals that haven't been checked in years. The dry heat finds every weakness. Get a full inspection before you leave home, not when you arrive. I've watched neighbors spend $1,800 on emergency AC replacement in February that could have been caught for $200 at home.

If you're boondocking on BLM land, a solid solar setup and a good battery monitor change everything. I run a Victron BMV-712 battery monitor ($80-90 on Amazon) and know exactly how much capacity I have at any moment. Worth every cent. After this many seasons, here's what matters and what doesn't.

The wifi at most Arizona snowbird parks is, charitably, challenged. Shared bandwidth across 300 rigs doesn't work for anyone who needs to stream or work remotely. Budget for a cell data plan as your primary connection. A Pepwave router can help you manage data and combine signals. Don't pick a park based on "complimentary wifi."

Pull-throughs sound appealing but aren't always better than back-ins with concrete pads, full utilities, and good neighbors. Check for shade, because six weeks in Arizona winter sun in an all-black asphalt lot gets old fast. Check site spacing too. Some parks cram rigs in so tight you're basically in a parking garage.

Activities matter more than you'd expect, especially if you're alone or it's your first season. Organized breakfasts, game nights, hiking clubs at the bigger parks aren't just filler. They're how winter feels like a life instead of a waiting room. I was skeptical when I started. Not anymore.

There's a reason the same people return to the same Arizona parks, year after year, for twenty winters. It's not just warmth. They've figured out a version of this that works for their rig, their budget, and their personality. That takes one season to learn, sometimes two. Give yourself the grace to treat your first year as scouting, not perfecting.
