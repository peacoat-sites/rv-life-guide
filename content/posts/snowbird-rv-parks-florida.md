Florida pulls about 800,000 RVers every winter season. That number sounds abstract until you're sitting in a line of 47 rigs waiting to check in at a park in Fort Myers in early November, wondering if you should have made that reservation back in March. You probably should have.

If you're reading this in October, I need you to understand something: the best parks in Florida aren't just "filling up." Many of them have been full since spring, with waitlists. That's not an exaggeration to scare you. That's the reality of snowbird country in 2024, and knowing it changes how you plan.

Here's what I tell people who are doing their first Florida winter: the state will not disappoint you. The weather alone is worth the drive. But the logistics can absolutely bite you if you walk in unprepared.

## Why Florida Still Dominates the Snowbird Circuit

People ask me every fall if Arizona or Texas is "better" now. Honestly, it depends on what you want. The Texas Hill Country is gorgeous and less crowded. Tucson in January is hard to beat for dry heat. But Florida has a density of affordable, amenity-rich RV parks within a few miles of actual ocean water that no other state has matched. That still matters.

The west coast of Florida, the stretch from Tampa down through Sarasota, Fort Myers, and into Naples, is where you'll find the highest concentration of long-term snowbird parks. The east coast (Vero Beach, the Space Coast, the Fort Lauderdale corridor) has its fans too, but it tends to run pricier and more transient. The Panhandle is underrated and often overlooked, which means better availability and lower rates if you don't need to be in the subtropical zone.

Cost-wise, expect somewhere between $700 and $1,600 per month for a full-hookup site in a decent park, depending on location, amenities, and how early you booked. Parks in Cape Coral or north Fort Myers tend to run $850 to $1,100 a month. Anything right on the water, or with a resort-style pool and pickleball courts, nudges toward $1,400. If someone's quoting you $600 a month in a coastal area in November, ask a lot of questions before you commit.

## The Parks That Actually Deliver (And What to Look For)

I'm going to name some names here, because vague recommendations are useless.

**Buttonwood Bay** in Sebring is a 55+ park that costs around $900 to $1,050 a month and has one of the best social calendars I've seen anywhere in the country. It sits on Lake Istokpoga, the fishing is legitimate, and it books up by July. If you're in the target age group and want genuine community, this one earns its reputation.

**Encore's Ramblers Rest** in Venice was one of the first parks I stayed at in Florida, back when I was new to this. It's a big-box park run by a corporate chain (Encore owns dozens of parks across the state), which some people love for consistency and some people hate because it feels like a franchise. The amenities are solid, the location is excellent, and if you have an Encore KOA or Thousand Trails membership, you'll get better rates. I'd stay there again.

**Waterway RV Resort** in Nokomis gets quieter recommendations from long-timers who don't want the secret out. It's not cheap, but waterfront access to the Intracoastal makes it worthwhile if that's your thing.

For budget-minded snowbirds, Florida state parks are the sleeper option most people discount because they assume you can't stay long enough. Florida actually allows up to 28 consecutive days per park, and with a bit of planning, you can do a circuit: two weeks at Hillsborough River State Park, then move to Myakka River, then down to Oscar Scherer. It's more work and you give up full hookups in many cases, but you'll pay $20 to $30 a night and end up in genuinely beautiful settings. I've done this. It requires a spreadsheet and a lot of advance reservations through ReserveAmerica, but it's doable.

## The Reservation Game: When to Book and How to Not Get Burned

Here's a real timeline that works:

If you want a season-long stay (November through April, roughly), start contacting parks in February or March of the prior year. Not April. February. Parks like Buttonwood Bay and most of the popular Charlotte County options will have their November-April slots locked by late spring.

If you've missed that window, late September and October aren't hopeless. Cancellations happen. People's plans change. Call parks directly, don't rely solely on their website. I've gotten spots that weren't showing as available online just by calling and asking to be put on the cancellation list.

Month-to-month bookings, where you show up and try to extend, work much better in January than in November. By mid-season, parks know who's leaving when, and a slot might open. This is genuinely how some people do their first Florida winter on short notice. It's nerve-wracking but not impossible.

One thing I always do before booking anywhere: I look at Google Maps satellite view of the site layout. A lot of Florida parks are tight, and some have beautiful photos that conveniently exclude the fact that you're parked 8 feet from your neighbor. I also use RV Park Reviews (rvparkreviews.com) and Campendium to read reviews from actual long-term guests, not weekend campers, because the experience is completely different.

## Gear That Makes a Florida Winter Work

Florida has specific demands on your rig. A few things I consider non-negotiable:

Power. Florida parks in peak season can have sketchy electrical infrastructure, especially older parks that were built before 50-amp service was standard. A quality surge protector is the single best investment you'll make. I run a Progressive Industries EMS-PT50X (around $350 right now). It's saved my inverter and my air conditioning unit from voltage spikes more than once. [Progressive Industries surge protectors are on Amazon here](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Progressive+Industries+surge+protector+RV) and the site may earn a commission if you buy through that link.

Water. I filter everything. Florida municipal water is heavily treated and can taste genuinely terrible depending on the area. A simple inline filter between the spigot and your rig handles the worst of it. I use a Camco TastePURE filter (about $20) and replace it every two months during a long stay. [Camco water filters are easy to find on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Camco+TastePURE+water+filter) if you want to grab one before you head south.

Leveling. Florida campgrounds are mostly flat, praise be, but some older parks have sites that slope toward the water or have settled over the years. A solid set of leveling blocks saves you the misery of sleeping at an angle for four months. I've used Camco's orange leveling blocks for years. [They're inexpensive and hold up fine](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Camco+leveling+blocks+RV).

And about that heat: yes, even in January you might run your AC in the afternoon. Your roof sealant and any vent covers matter. If you haven't checked your Dicor sealant before heading to Florida, do it. Humidity plus heat cycles will find every crack you've ignored.

## What Nobody Warns You About

The social dynamics of a big Florida snowbird park can be a lot. You are living, at close quarters, with hundreds of other people, many of whom have been coming to the same park for 15 years and have strong opinions about where to park your golf cart and how loud your grandkids are.

Most parks are genuinely wonderful communities. I don't want to overstate the negative. But if you're introverted, or you're younger than 55 and trying to fit into a 55+ park on a "family member" exception, or you just want to be left alone to read your book, some parks are better fits than others. Reading reviews specifically for those dynamics is worth your time.

Bugs are real. Love bugs, no-see-ums, and palmetto bugs (the Florida euphemism for very large cockroaches) are part of the deal. Keep your screens in good shape. Don't leave food out. The no-see-um season varies by location but the Everglades corridor and the interior lakes can be miserable in certain months without good screening.

Traffic on US-41 (Tamiami Trail) in the Fort Myers and Naples corridor gets absolutely punishing from December through March. If you're planning day trips, budget significantly more time than Google Maps suggests.

## FAQ

### How far in advance do I need to book a Florida snowbird RV park?

For a November-through-April season, contact parks the prior February or March. The most popular parks in the Fort Myers, Sarasota, and Venice areas are routinely full by June for the following season. If you're booking for a shorter stay of a month or two, mid-October outreach can still get you something, but you'll be working with whatever's left.

### Are 55+ parks worth it for snowbirds who qualify?

For a lot of people, yes. The social programming, the quieter atmosphere, and the community infrastructure in established 55+ parks is noticeably better than general parks of the same price point. The trade-off is less flexibility and sometimes stricter rules about guests and rig age. Most have a rule that your RV must be under 10 or 15 years old, so check that before you drive 1,200 miles.

### What's the cheapest way to do a Florida winter in an RV?

Florida state park camping, done as a circuit with careful advance reservations, gets you to $20 to $30 a night with beautiful scenery. You'll give up full hookups in most cases and need to dump every few days. Harvest Hosts and Boondockers Welcome aren't as rich in options in urban south Florida, but the interior of the state has more possibilities than most people realize.

### Is the west coast or east coast of Florida better for RV snowbirds?

West coast (Fort Myers, Sarasota, Naples area) has more dedicated snowbird park infrastructure and generally better winter weather. East coast parks tend to be pricier and more tourist-oriented. If cost and long-term community matter, west coast wins. If you want proximity to Miami or prefer the Atlantic, east coast has its appeal.

### Can I bring a large Class A (40 feet or more) to most Florida snowbird parks?

Most established parks in the main snowbird corridors can handle a 40-foot rig. Always call ahead and confirm site length and pull-through versus back-in availability. Some of the older parks along the Gulf were built for smaller rigs and have genuinely tight turns. A few will cap rig age at 10 years regardless of size, so ask about both.

---

One last thing worth saying plainly: Florida as a snowbird destination is genuinely good. The winters are mild, the sunsets on the Gulf coast are the real deal, and there's a reason the same people come back to the same parks for 20 years running. Book early, protect your electrical, and go easy on the Tamiami Trail during rush hour. The rest tends to take care of itself.
