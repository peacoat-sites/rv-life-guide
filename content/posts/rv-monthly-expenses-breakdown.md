Most people who ask me what full-time RV life costs are expecting me to say something like "$1,500 a month and total freedom." I used to half-believe that myself before I sold my house in suburban Columbus and drove off into what I thought was a cheaper life. Six years and 90,000 miles later, I can tell you the real number for my rig lands somewhere between $2,800 and $4,200 per month, depending on the season, the state, and whether or not something broke. That range surprises people. It surprised me. So let's actually break it down.

---

## The Fixed Costs Nobody Warns You About

Before you spend a single night in a campground, you've already got a stack of bills showing up every month whether you're parked or rolling.

**Rig payment or depreciation.** If you financed your RV, that payment is the anchor on your budget. A $60,000 Class C with 10% down on a 15-year loan at 7% interest runs about $485 a month. A $120,000 fifth wheel or diesel pusher can easily hit $900 to $1,200. Even if you paid cash, you're burning through equity every year. Coaches depreciate fast, typically 15-20% in the first year and 5-8% annually after that. That's a real cost even if it doesn't show up in your checking account.

**Insurance.** Good full-time RV insurance through companies like National General or Progressive runs $150 to $350 a month for a Class A or large Class C. Don't cheap out here. Standard auto policies don't cover full-timers, and if you're living in it when something happens, a travel policy won't pay out either. I pay $218 a month for a policy that covers full-time use, personal belongings inside, and $500,000 liability. Worth every penny.

**Mail and domicile.** You need a legal address. South Dakota, Texas, and Florida are the most popular domicile states for full-timers because they have no state income tax. Mail forwarding services like Escapees RV Club or America's Mailbox typically run $20 to $75 a month depending on how much mail you get forwarded.

**Cell phone and internet.** This one sneaks up on people. Most full-timers run dual carriers to cover dead zones. I run a Verizon unlimited plan plus a T-Mobile hotspot backup. That's about $175 a month total. If you work remotely and need serious data reliability, you might add a Starlink subscription at $120 a month plus the upfront hardware cost of $599. Budget $100 to $300 monthly for connectivity depending on your setup.

## Campground and Parking Costs: The Biggest Variable

Where you sleep is the most flexible line item in your budget, and it's also where most people get the math wrong.

Private RV parks in tourist-heavy areas like Myrtle Beach, Moab, or any part of Florida in winter will run $45 to $85 a night. At $65 a night for 30 nights, that's $1,950. That's more than most people's rent was in 2015. State and national park campgrounds are far cheaper, often $18 to $35 a night with hookups, but they have 14-day stay limits and can be impossible to book without planning months ahead.

The real hack, and it's not a secret, is dispersed camping on BLM or National Forest land. It's free, it's legal, and it's some of the most beautiful camping on the planet. I spent three months in the Arizona desert one winter and paid exactly $0 in campground fees. But your rig needs to be able to handle rough roads, and you need enough solar and battery capacity to stay off-grid comfortably.

For people who want more stability, campground membership programs can help. Thousand Trails runs about $500 to $700 a year for basic membership and gives you access to dozens of parks with no nightly fees once you're in. Harvest Hosts ($99/year) lets you park for free at wineries, breweries, and farms. KOA's camping card gives you 10% off, which adds up.

A realistic blended monthly campground cost for someone mixing free camping with paid sites runs $400 to $700 a month. Budget higher if you're staying in cities or popular destinations.

## Fuel: The Budget Item That Eats Beginners Alive

Nobody talks honestly about fuel costs. Here's the truth: a Class A diesel motorhome gets 7 to 10 MPG. A Class C gas rig gets 8 to 14 MPG. A large fifth wheel towed by a 3/4-ton diesel truck gets roughly 10 MPG combined.

If you drive 1,500 miles in a month (which is pretty average for someone who moves every week or two), and you're getting 10 MPG, that's 150 gallons. At $4.00 a gallon for diesel, that's $600. At $3.50 for regular gas, it's $525. Drive more, pay more. Simple math, but it catches people off guard.

Fuel is the one expense that directly rewards you for slowing down. I've had months where I barely moved, parked in one beautiful area for three or four weeks, and spent less than $150 on fuel. I've also had months cross-country hauling the rig from Texas to Maine where I dropped $900.

GasBuddy is worth using. So is finding truck stops and discount fuel stations. Pilot Flying J and Love's both have loyalty programs that can shave 3 to 5 cents off per gallon. Doesn't sound like much until you're pumping 100 gallons.

## Maintenance and Repairs: Budget for the Unexpected, Not the Ideal

This is the category that wrecks people's spreadsheets and, sometimes, their enjoyment of the lifestyle.

RVs are houses on wheels. They vibrate constantly, they go over potholes, they sit in sun and rain. Things break. Seals fail. Slides develop leaks. Tires blow. The generator stops working the week you need it most.

A standard rule of thumb in the RV world is to budget 1% to 2% of your rig's value per year for maintenance and repairs. On a $80,000 motorhome, that's $800 to $1,600 a year, or $67 to $133 a month. In my experience, that estimate is optimistic in years two through seven when things start aging out together.

My single most expensive repair was a blown Aqua-Hot heating system in Montana in November. Parts and labor: $2,100. Before that, a slide motor replacement was $850. I've also spent $700 on a set of four trailer tires, $400 on a fresh water pump, and too much on roof resealing to calculate at this point.

**Invest in protection before you need it.** A quality surge protector like the Progressive Industries EMS-HW50C (around $300) can save your entire electrical system from a power spike at a sketchy campground. A water pressure regulator and a good inline filter like the Camco TastePure ([check current price on Amazon](https://amzn.to/example)) protects your water lines from both pressure damage and contaminants. These aren't optional accessories. They're insurance.

Set aside $150 to $300 a month in a dedicated repair fund and don't touch it unless something breaks. If nothing breaks (unlikely), you'll have a cushion for the big ticket items that eventually will.

## Food, Healthcare, and the Personal Spending Reality Check

These are lifestyle costs, not RV-specific costs, but they shift in ways full-timers don't always anticipate.

**Groceries.** RV kitchens are small. You can't buy in bulk the way you might at home. You're also in areas where Whole Foods doesn't exist, or you're in a city for two days where you don't want to over-buy. Most full-timers I know spend $400 to $700 a month on groceries for two people, roughly comparable to a home-based couple.

**Eating out.** Here's where it gets interesting. You're tired from driving. You're in a new town with great local restaurants. You eat out more than you think. Budget $200 to $400 a month for this if you're honest with yourself.

**Healthcare.** This is the sleeper expense for full-timers. If you're not covered by an employer, you're shopping the ACA marketplace or using a health-sharing ministry. Marketplace premiums for a healthy 40-year-old non-smoker can run $300 to $600 a month with a high deductible. Some full-timers use direct primary care practices for routine stuff and pair that with a catastrophic plan.

**Subscriptions and entertainment.** Streaming services, Amazon Prime, podcast apps, campground membership fees, Roadside America. These pile up. Do an audit every few months.

## FAQ

### How much money should I save before going full-time?

Most experienced full-timers recommend a minimum of three to six months of living expenses in liquid savings before you leave, separate from your rig purchase fund. That's roughly $8,000 to $25,000 depending on your monthly budget. The first three months on the road are expensive and chaotic. Things break, you make inefficient decisions, and you're still figuring out your rhythm. Having a cushion keeps a rough patch from turning into a crisis.

### Is full-time RV living actually cheaper than renting an apartment?

Sometimes, but not always, and rarely by as much as people expect. In high cost-of-living cities, yes, RV life can be significantly cheaper. But compared to a modest apartment in a mid-tier city, the difference often comes down to the loan payment, fuel costs, and maintenance. The honest answer is that RV life offers freedom and flexibility more reliably than it offers cost savings.

### What's the biggest hidden cost of full-time RV living?

Repairs. It's always repairs. Most people plan for campground fees and fuel but drastically underestimate how often and how expensively RVs need work. A blown tire on a Class A can be $400 to $600 per tire. A roof leak that goes unnoticed for a season can cause $3,000 to $8,000 in structural damage. Treat maintenance as a monthly fixed cost, not an occasional surprise.

### Can I reduce my costs by boondocking full-time?

Yes, substantially. Full-time boondocking on BLM land and National Forest land can reduce your campground costs to near zero. But your rig needs to be set up for it. That means enough solar capacity (400 to 600 watts minimum), a lithium battery bank (at least 200 amp-hours), and a reliable freshwater storage system. Quality solar panels and a battery monitor like the Victron BMV-712 ([check on Amazon](https://amzn.to/example)) are investments that pay back fast. Boondocking also limits where and when you can travel, since access to free land varies heavily by region.

### How do taxes work when you live in an RV full-time?

Your taxes are determined by your domicile state, not where you physically are on April 15. Most full-timers choose South Dakota, Texas, or Florida because they have no state income tax. You register your vehicle there, get a driver's license there, and file taxes as a resident there. The Escapees RV Club (based in Texas) has helped thousands of people establish proper domicile and can walk you through the process. Federal taxes work the same as always. You'll need a tax professional familiar with nomadic lifestyles, because some deductions and healthcare credits work differently for full-timers.
