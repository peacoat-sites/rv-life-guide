Three years ago, I paid $595 for a Thousand Trails Zone membership and spent the next six months figuring out whether that was brilliant or a colossal mistake. I'm still not sure I have a clean answer. But I've got a much better one than most of the reviews I read before I bought it, which ranged from "best thing I ever did" to "total scam" with very little useful detail in between.

Here's what I actually know after using it, talking to dozens of other full-timers who use it, and watching the membership structure change over the years.

## What You're Actually Buying (And What That Means for Full-Timers Specifically)

Thousand Trails is not a campground chain in the traditional sense. It's a membership camping network, which means you're buying access to a system, not a specific location. The base product right now is the Elite Membership, which runs around $595 to $699 for the initial buy-in depending on when you catch a promotion, plus an annual maintenance fee that currently sits around $299 to $399 per year. That annual fee is the number most people gloss over when they're evaluating the math, and it's the one that will make or break the value proposition for you.

What you get: unlimited camping at Thousand Trails-owned properties with no nightly fees, subject to a 14-day stay limit at any single park, plus access to Trails Collection properties (a broader network of partner campgrounds) at rates that are cheaper than walk-up prices but not free.

What most people don't realize is that the Trails Collection properties are treated very differently from the core TT parks. I've shown up expecting a Trails Collection discount and been told the park was fully booked for members, or that the rate was barely better than what I'd find on Campspot or directly calling the park. That inconsistency is real, and it matters more for full-timers than for weekend warriors who can just try a different weekend.

The 14-day-on, 7-days-off rule is the other thing nobody explains clearly in the marketing. You can stay at a Thousand Trails property for up to 14 consecutive days. Then you have to leave and stay somewhere else (paying out of pocket) for 7 days before you can return to any Thousand Trails property. Not just the same park, any TT park in your zone or membership tier. If your travel rhythm doesn't account for this, you'll end up with a week-long gap every month that costs you real money.

## The Zone System and Why It Matters More Than You Think

When I bought in, Thousand Trails still had a Zone membership structure where you paid for access to a specific geographic zone (Northwest, Mid-Atlantic, etc.) and paid upgrade fees to access others. They've since moved more toward the nationwide Elite Membership as their primary product, but some older zone memberships still exist in the resale market, and people are still selling and transferring them.

If you're a full-timer who moves with the seasons, a zone membership is probably not the move. I've talked to full-timers who bought a Pacific Northwest zone membership thinking they'd winter there and summer there and found themselves paying out of pocket for huge stretches of the country every time they went anywhere else.

The Elite Membership with nationwide access makes a lot more sense for people who are actually moving around. But even then, the distribution of parks matters. Thousand Trails is genuinely concentrated in certain regions: the Pacific Northwest has a solid cluster, the Southeast has decent coverage, and the Northeast and Midwest are thinner. If you're planning a year that includes several months in western Pennsylvania or central Kansas, you should pull up the TT park locator before you buy and honestly assess whether there are enough parks on your route to justify the fee.

I made a rough map of my annual travel pattern and counted 9 TT parks I realistically would use in a given year. At an average nightly rate of $40 to $55 for comparable campgrounds, and assuming I'd use those parks for maybe 90 nights combined, the math looked pretty good. The membership fee (amortized annual maintenance plus cost of the initial buy-in spread over 5 years) came out to roughly $560 per year. That's a savings of somewhere between $3,000 and $4,000 against pay-as-you-go camping. On paper, very good.

In practice? Closer to $1,500 to $2,000 in actual savings, because park availability wasn't always there when I needed it, I had to pad out weeks at non-TT campgrounds during the 7-day-off periods, and a few of the parks I'd planned on using were honestly not worth staying at.

## The Part Nobody Likes to Talk About: Park Quality

This is where full-timers need to get honest with themselves, because park quality across the Thousand Trails system is wildly inconsistent.

Some of the parks are genuinely excellent. The TT Orlando/Clermont property in Florida has been a reader favorite for years. TT Leavenworth in Washington is stunning. Idyllwild in California is one of the better mountain parks I've stayed at anywhere, membership or not.

But I've also stayed at TT properties with electric hookups that couldn't sustain a hair dryer without tripping the breaker, Wi-Fi that gave up when more than 20 rigs were in the park (which is always), and sites that were clearly designed before slide-outs were a thing. A 40-foot fifth wheel with two slides has no business in some of these spots, and I've watched people try.

The maintenance gap is something longtime TT members will recognize immediately. Some parks have clearly had capital investment in recent years. Others feel like they're running on 1992 infrastructure with a fresh coat of paint. Thousand Trails was acquired by Equity LifeStyle Properties (ELS) in 2014, and while ELS has generally improved the system, the quality floor is still pretty low at certain locations.

Before I use any TT park I haven't been to, I check recent reviews on RVillage, The Dyrt, and Campendium. Not the reviews from five years ago. Recent ones. Infrastructure can change fast in either direction.

## Resale Memberships: The Actual Smart Play

Here's the thing I wish someone had told me before I handed over $699 at a Thousand Trails sales presentation: you can buy a used membership for a fraction of that price.

Resale Thousand Trails memberships show up constantly on Facebook Marketplace, eBay, and dedicated sites like Wholesale Campgrounds. I've seen functional Elite Memberships sell for $150 to $300 with the annual maintenance fee simply transferring to the new owner. The catch is that you still pay the annual maintenance fee, and Thousand Trails does charge a transfer fee (currently around $149 to $199, though this has changed over time, so verify before you buy).

Even factoring in the transfer fee, buying resale at $200 instead of $699 saves you $500 right out of the gate. Over five years of ownership, that's $100 per year back in your pocket on top of whatever campsite savings you generate.

The one thing I'd scrutinize carefully on a resale membership is whether there's any outstanding balance owed on it, and whether it includes the full nationwide access or only a limited zone. Ask for documentation. Get the membership number and verify it directly with Thousand Trails member services before you send any money.

## Stacking It With Other Memberships

Most full-timers I know don't use any single membership in isolation. Thousand Trails works well in combination with Harvest Hosts (around $99/year for winery, brewery, and farm stays), Boondockers Welcome, and a Passport America membership if you're still paying nightly fees at a lot of commercial parks.

The strategy I've landed on personally: TT covers probably 60 to 70 nights of my year at no additional cost. Harvest Hosts covers maybe 20 nights. Free boondocking on BLM land covers another 30 to 40 nights. That leaves maybe 60 to 80 nights where I'm paying out of pocket, and those tend to be either urban stops where nothing is cheap anyway, or shoulder-season moves between regions.

The people I've seen get the least value out of Thousand Trails are the ones who bought it expecting it to replace all their camping expenses. It won't. The people who get the most value are the ones who planned their travel around TT park locations before they bought in, and who treat the membership as one tool among several.

### FAQ

#### How long does it take to "break even" on a Thousand Trails membership?

If you buy a resale membership for around $200 to $300, pay the transfer fee, and your annual maintenance is around $350, you're looking at roughly $650 in year one. At average campsite rates of $45 per night, you break even at around 15 nights of use. For a full-timer who spends even a few weeks at TT parks, that's achievable pretty quickly. Buying retail at $699 pushes that break-even point out to 30 or more nights in year one.

#### Can you use Thousand Trails as your only camping membership as a full-timer?

Technically yes, but I wouldn't recommend it. The 14-day-stay, 7-day-off rule means you'll always have gap weeks, and TT park coverage is uneven enough geographically that you'll hit stretches of travel where there's nothing useful on your route. Most full-timers stack it with free options like boondocking or at least one other membership.

#### Is Thousand Trails good for full-timers with larger rigs?

Hit or miss. Some parks have great big-rig sites with full hookups and pull-throughs. Others have sites with tight turns, low-hanging trees, and 30-amp service that can't handle a 45-foot diesel pusher running two rooftop ACs. Always call ahead or check recent reviews on Campendium for site dimensions before you commit. Don't trust the official TT site listing for this.

#### What happens if you don't pay the annual maintenance fee?

Your membership goes into default, and Thousand Trails can eventually terminate it. Unlike a timeshare, though, the process is generally not as aggressive. That said, defaulting on the maintenance fee wipes out whatever you paid for the membership, so if you're thinking of buying in, make sure the annual fee is sustainable for your budget long-term. That $350ish per year doesn't go away.

#### Are there blackout dates or peak season restrictions?

There are no formal blackout dates on TT-owned properties for Elite members, but peak summer weekends at popular parks fill up fast and reservations open 90 days in advance. In practice, July 4th weekend at a park like TT Leavenworth or TT Natchez Trace feels like a blackout date because it's nearly impossible to snag a spot unless you're booking the day reservations open. The Trails Collection partner properties sometimes do have restricted dates for member rates.

---

Whether a Thousand Trails membership pencils out for you isn't really a question I can answer without knowing your travel map, your rig size, and your camping style. But if you've read this far and you're doing math on 50 or more nights a year at mid-range campgrounds with hookups, it's probably worth at least pricing out a resale membership and laying your planned route over their park map. The answer will become obvious pretty fast. And if the parks on that map look thin, save your money.
